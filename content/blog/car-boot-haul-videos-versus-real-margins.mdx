This is for anyone who watches car boot haul videos and wonders why their own Sunday mornings do not seem to add up the same way. It looks at the claim these videos tend to make, spoken or implied, that the difference between what you paid and what an item sells for is your profit. It sets out the costs that usually fall outside the frame, works through one example using official eBay fees, Royal Mail prices and HMRC's mileage rate, and gives you a way to track your own real margin. It does not name any creator: haul videos can be fun and genuinely useful for learning what sells. The point is to test the arithmetic. This is general information, not tax or financial advice.

## The short version

- **The claim:** "Paid £3, sold for £20, that's £17 profit." The £17 is a gross margin on one item, before any costs.
- **Left out on each sale:** platform fees, postage you pay, packaging, and returns.
- **Left out on each trip:** fuel and wear on the car, parking, entry fees, and the items that never sell.
- **Left out entirely:** hours. Sourcing, cleaning, photographing, listing, answering messages and posting all take time.
- **In our worked example**, a "£17 profit" item leaves £10.88 after fees and postage, and a whole haul that looks like £136 profit ends up at £32.40, or £4.63 an hour. The inputs are made up; the fees and rates are official.
- **Track your own:** one line per item, one line per trip, and a monthly total. Our [trip cost](/tools/worth-it?tab=trip) and [worth my time](/tools/worth-it) tools do the sums.

## What a haul video usually shows

The typical format is familiar. A table of things bought at a boot sale, the price paid for each, and either a guess at what each will sell for or, in follow-up videos, what some of them did sell for. The headline is the gap between the two.

There is nothing dishonest about showing a haul. The trouble is what the format leaves out by its nature. A haul video is filmed at the moment of greatest hope: everything bought, nothing listed yet, no unsold items, no returns, and no hours counted. Even a follow-up "what sold" video tends to focus on the winners.

## What is usually missing

### Costs on every sale

- **Platform fees.** If you buy to resell, eBay treats you as a business seller: its [business seller policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/business-seller-policy?id=4710) names people who "sell items they have bought to resell". Business sellers pay a final value fee by category, a per-order fee, and a 0.35% regulatory operating fee, all on the total including postage and all excluding VAT ([eBay business seller fees](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/business-selling-fees?id=4809)). On Vinted, reselling for profit needs a Pro account ([Vinted: commercial selling](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/4/1120-commercial-selling)); Vinted says Pro is free for sellers to list and sell ([Vinted Pro](https://www.vinted.co.uk/pro)), so there the fee line is paid by the buyer, not you.
- **VAT on fees.** eBay's business fees are shown excluding VAT, and its [taxes page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/taxes-import-charges?id=4121) lists paying VAT on selling fees among the seller's responsibilities. If you are not VAT registered, you cannot reclaim it. The standard rate is 20% ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/vat-rates)).
- **Postage and packaging.** If you offer free postage, the label comes out of your price. Mailing bags and tape are small per item but real.
- **Returns.** An item that comes back costs you the outbound postage, possibly return postage, your time, and sometimes the sale itself.

### Costs on every trip

- **Travel.** Fuel is only part of what a car costs to run. HMRC's flat rate for business miles in a car, for 2026 to 2027, is 55p a mile for the first 10,000 miles and 25p after that, and it is meant to cover "buying and running your vehicle, for example insurance, repairs, servicing, fuel" ([GOV.UK: simplified expenses, vehicles](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/vehicles)). It is a tax rate, not your exact cost, but it is a fair stand-in when you want an honest figure.
- **Parking.** GOV.UK says parking is claimed separately on top of the mileage rate. It is a separate cost for your sums too.
- **Entry fees.** Many boot sales charge buyers to get in. What they charge, and whether it depends on when you arrive, varies from sale to sale, so check the sale's own details and write down what you paid.

### Stock that does not sell

Every haul has items that sit. They still cost what you paid for them, they take up space, and some will end up donated or binned. A margin worked out only on what sold makes the haul look better than it was.

### Time

Driving there and back, walking the pitches, cleaning and testing, photographing, writing listings, answering "is this still available", packing and the post office. None of it appears in the price gap.

## A worked example

Everything below marked **example** is made up to show the method. It is not a claim about what boot sales cost or what items sell for. The fee rates, postage price and mileage rate are official figures, checked in September 2026.

### One item

Say you buy a jacket for £3 (**example**) and sell it on eBay as a business seller for £20 (**example**) with free postage, sent 2nd Class as a large letter.

| Line | Working | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sale price, free postage | example | £20.00 |
| Final value fee | Clothes, shoes and accessories, 11.9% of £20 | £2.38 |
| Regulatory operating fee | 0.35% of £20 | £0.07 |
| Per-order fee | order over £10 | £0.40 |
| VAT on eBay fees | 20% of £2.85 | £0.57 |
| Postage | Royal Mail 2nd Class Large Letter, 501 g to 750 g, online price | £2.70 |
| Left after fees and postage | | £13.88 |
| What you paid | example | £3.00 |
| Margin on this item | before packaging, travel and time | £10.88 |

The fee rates are from [eBay's business seller fees page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/business-selling-fees?id=4809). The postage price is from [Royal Mail's 2nd Class page](https://www.royalmail.com/sending/uk/2nd-class) in September 2026; Royal Mail changes prices from time to time, so check before relying on it. The "£17 profit" is £10.88 before any trip costs.

### The whole haul

Now say the jacket was one of 8 items bought at the same sale for £3 each (**example**), and a haul video would have said each could sell for £20.

| | Haul video arithmetic | Counted properly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Items sold | 8 (assumed) | 5 (**example**) |
| Money from sales | 8 x £20 = £160 | 5 x £13.88 = £69.40 after fees and postage |
| Stock | £24 | £24 |
| Mileage | not counted | 20 miles round trip (**example**) x 55p = £11.00 |
| Entry fee | not counted | £2 (**example**) |
| Profit | £136 | £32.40 |
| Hours | not counted | 7 (**example**) |
| Per hour | not counted | £4.63 |

The three unsold items are still in a box. They may sell later, and if they do, the numbers improve. But until they do, the £9 you paid for them is spent. Packaging and any returns would lower the figure further.

For comparison, the National Living Wage for people aged 21 and over is £12.71 an hour from April 2026 ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates)). That is not the only way to judge whether reselling is worthwhile for you: some people enjoy it, fit it around other things, or are building skills. But it is a fair benchmark to hold your own figure against.

Change any example input and the result moves a lot. Sell all 8, drive half the distance, or take less time per listing and the hourly figure climbs. Sell 3, and it can go negative. That is the point: the haul does not decide the margin. The whole process does.

## How to track your own real margin

You do not need a spreadsheet course. Three habits cover it.

1. **Log each trip on the day.** Date, where, miles, parking, entry fee, cash spent on stock, number of items, and hours including travel. Our [sourcing trip cost tool](/tools/worth-it?tab=trip) takes the miles at HMRC's rate, parking and fees, and sets them against what you expect the haul to make.
2. **Log each item when it sells.** Sale price, fees, postage, packaging, what you paid, and roughly how long it took to list and post. The [worth my time tool](/tools/worth-it) turns those minutes into an hourly figure for a single flip.
3. **Once a month, total it.** Sales after fees and postage, minus stock bought, minus trip costs, minus tools and packaging. List unsold stock separately at cost. Then divide by hours.

After two or three months you will know which sales are worth the drive, which types of item earn their listing time, and when to stop buying things that look good on the table. For practical advice on the morning itself, including timing, cash and checking items before you pay, see our guide to [car boot sales for resellers](/guides/car-boot-sales-for-resellers).

If you want to share what you find, with what you paid and what it sold for, post it in [Pickups](/community/pickups). And if you have three months of numbers, our [90-day template for eBay and Vinted](/blog/first-90-days-in-real-numbers-ebay-vinted) is a way to share the whole picture.

## What to take from haul videos

Watch them for what they are good at: learning which brands, eras and categories people look for, how to spot quality, and how to handle a pitch. Treat the profit figures as gross margins on the best items, not as a forecast for your own mornings. And when someone says an item made "£17 profit", the useful question is: after what?
