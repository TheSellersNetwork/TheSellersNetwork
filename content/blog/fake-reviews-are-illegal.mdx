This is for UK resellers who ask customers for feedback, publish reviews on their own site, or buy from sellers whose reviews look too good to be true. Since 6 April 2025, fake reviews and reviews that hide the fact they were paid for have been banned outright under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. After reading this you will know what counts, what you can and cannot do to get reviews, what platforms have to do, and where to report problems. This is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- The ban is in [paragraph 13 of Schedule 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20/paragraph/13) to the Act. It applies to commercial practices from [6 April 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/272/made).
- It is illegal to write, post or commission a **fake review**, or a review that **hides that it was incentivised**.
- It is illegal to **publish reviews in a misleading way**, for example by hiding genuine negative reviews or showing only the good ones.
- Anyone who publishes reviews must take **reasonable and proportionate steps** to prevent and remove fake and hidden-incentive reviews.
- Selling fake review services is banned too.
- The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) can fine businesses up to [10% of global turnover](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cma-to-boost-consumer-and-business-confidence-as-new-consumer-protection-regime-comes-into-force) for breaking consumer law.
- The CMA's [fake reviews guidance (CMA208)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews) explains how it reads the law. Most of what follows comes from it.

## What the law says

Schedule 20 lists commercial practices that are automatically unfair, with no need to show that any consumer was actually misled. Paragraph 13 covers reviews. In summary, it bans:

| Paragraph | What is banned |
| --- | --- |
| 13(1) | Submitting, or commissioning someone else to submit or write, a fake consumer review or a consumer review that conceals it was incentivised |
| 13(2) | Publishing consumer reviews, or information drawn from them, in a misleading way |
| 13(3) | Publishing consumer reviews without taking reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and false or misleading review information |
| 13(4) | Offering services to traders to do, or help do, anything in 13(1) or 13(2) |

The definitions are broad:

- A **consumer review** is "a review of a product, a trader or any other matter relevant to a transactional decision". The [CMA guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews) says it does not have to be written by a consumer, and covers text, video, speech and star ratings. Marketplace feedback about a seller and product ratings are likely to count.
- A **fake consumer review** is one that "purports to be, but is not, based on a person's genuine experience".
- **Consumer review information** means information derived from or influenced by reviews, such as star ratings, review counts and rankings.

Other banned practices can apply at the same time. The CMA guidance points out that falsely representing yourself as a consumer, for example reviewing your own products from another account, is also banned under paragraph 25 of the same Schedule.

## What counts as fake

The CMA's examples of fake reviews include:

- saying a product or seller met expectations when it did not, or when the reviewer never used it;
- a video claiming to show the results of one product while actually using another;
- reviews generated by software or bots made to look like individual customers.

Fake reviews can be negative as well as positive. A fake one-star review of a competitor is banned in exactly the same way as a fake five-star review of yourself.

The guidance is also clear about what is **not** fake. A review is not fake just because the seller disagrees with it, as long as it reflects the reviewer's genuine experience.

## What counts as a concealed incentivised review

A review is incentivised when the reviewer was commissioned to write it. The [CMA guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews) says commissioning is not limited to money and gives examples including:

- money or commission;
- discounts or vouchers;
- free products;
- free stays or invitations to events;
- loans or leases on better terms than the public gets;
- a request to a member of staff to review the business's product.

The guidance says that entering customers into a prize draw for leaving a review, where there is no guaranteed benefit, is unlikely to count as commissioning.

An incentivised review is not automatically illegal. It becomes illegal when the incentive is hidden. Where incentivised reviews are allowed, the CMA says they must be clearly identifiable, usually with a prominent label such as "advert", not hidden behind a "learn more" link.

**For resellers this is often academic, because the platforms ban incentives anyway.** eBay's [feedback manipulation policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/feedback-policies/feedback-manipulation-policy?id=4231) prohibits offering discounts, free items or any other incentive in exchange for positive feedback. Amazon's [customer product reviews policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G201972140) prohibits offering rewards, discounts, free products or refunds in exchange for reviews. The CMA guidance notes that where a platform does not allow incentivised reviews, submitting one is likely to be misleading.

### Examples the CMA gives of banned commissioning

- Offering a customer a free or discounted product in return for a five-star review that does not reflect their experience.
- Posting on social media offering free products for five-star reviews.
- Contacting a customer who left a negative review and offering a refund or gift card if they change it.
- Asking people to buy an item and promising to reimburse them once they post a positive review.

The third one catches sellers out. Resolving a complaint is fine. Making the refund conditional on changing the review is not.

## What sellers can do

The guidance is clear that asking for genuine reviews is allowed. Emailing customers generally to ask whether they would like to leave a review, without steering what they say, is not prohibited. Our guide on [asking for feedback](/guides/asking-for-feedback) covers how to do that on each platform.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
| --- | --- |
| Asking every customer for honest feedback | Asking only customers you think are happy |
| Fixing a problem and then asking if they would like to update their feedback, with no condition | Offering a refund, discount or gift in return for changing or removing a review |
| Replying publicly and politely to a negative review | Threatening legal action to get a genuine review removed |
| Reporting a review you believe is fake to the platform | Reviewing your own items from another account, or asking friends to |
| Showing all reviews, including negative ones, on your own site | Showing only the five-star reviews, or quietly deleting the bad ones |
| A clearly labelled review from someone you gifted a product to, where the platform allows it | The same review with the gift left out |

### If you publish reviews on your own website

If you run your own shop and show reviews, you are a publisher of reviews and the 13(2) and 13(3) duties apply to you. The CMA guidance says you must not:

- suppress genuine negative reviews, or make them hard to find;
- cherry-pick positive reviews, including by only inviting satisfied customers;
- hide that a reviewer has a financial interest, such as an employee, shareholder or supplier;
- merge reviews from different products where the difference matters to the buyer;
- show a star rating or review count that still includes reviews you have removed as fake.

You also need a simple published policy on reviews and a way of checking for fakes, proportionate to the size of your site. The next section explains what that means.

## What platforms and review publishers must do

Paragraph 13(3) puts a positive duty on anyone who publishes reviews. The [CMA guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews) says there is no single tick-box approach, but every publisher needs:

1. **A clear, published policy** that prohibits fake reviews and sets out the approach to incentivised reviews, written in plain English and easy to find.
2. **A risk assessment** of how likely fake and hidden-incentive reviews are to appear, reviewed regularly rather than done once.
3. **Detection** of banned reviews before and after publication, for example rules on who can post a review, or checking that the reviewer actually bought the product.
4. **Investigation** of suspicious reviews and reports.
5. **Action**, including removing banned reviews and sanctions against those responsible.
6. **Regular review** of whether these steps are working.

The guidance also says publishers must not treat a negative review as a complaint and then not publish it, or try to talk someone out of leaving a review because their complaint was resolved. And it warns that removing a genuine negative review can mislead buyers as much as publishing a fake positive one.

For a small seller with a review widget on their own website, the proportionate version might be: only verified buyers can review, every review is published unless it breaks a written policy, and the policy and a report link are shown next to the reviews. For a large marketplace the expectations are much higher.

## How to report fake reviews

| What you have found | Where to report it |
| --- | --- |
| A fake or suspicious review on a marketplace or review site | The platform's own report tool. The CMA expects publishers to investigate reports they receive |
| A seller or business buying, selling or posting fake reviews | Trading Standards, through the [Citizens Advice consumer service](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/) |
| A business offering fake review services, as a business reporting a market problem | The CMA, through its [contact page](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/contact-the-cma). The CMA says it cannot act on individual consumer complaints |
| An ad or influencer post with hidden paid reviews or endorsements | The [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html). The CAP Code says ads must make clear where consumer reviews have been incentivised ([rule 3.45](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html)) |
| Someone who took your money using fake reviews to look genuine | [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/), which replaced Action Fraud (in Scotland, Police Scotland on 101) |

When you report, include screenshots with dates, links to the reviews and profiles, and why you think they are fake: the same wording across reviews, a burst of reviews in a short period, reviewers with no other history, or an offer you were sent. The government's [reviews and endorsements collection](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/online-reviews-and-endorsements-information-for-businesses) has further guidance for businesses, review sites and content creators.

### If you receive a fake negative review

Report it to the platform with your evidence, reply calmly if the platform allows replies, and keep a record. Do not threaten the reviewer or offer them anything to remove it. If a competitor appears to be behind a pattern of fake reviews, that is a matter for Trading Standards through Citizens Advice.

## Reviews and course sellers

Many courses and "mentorships" sold to resellers lean heavily on testimonials. The same law applies to them. A testimonial from someone who was given the course free, paid commission, or offered a discount for a video must say so. Several US enforcement cases also involved contracts stopping buyers from leaving negative reviews; see [how to spot a reselling course scam](/blog/how-to-spot-a-reselling-course-scam).

## What goes wrong

- **Offering a partial refund "if you update your feedback".** Resolve the problem, then ask without conditions.
- **Asking friends and family to review your shop.** Unless it is a genuine purchase experience and any connection is disclosed, it is likely to be fake or misleading, and the platforms prohibit it.
- **Joining "review for review" groups.** Swapping reviews with other sellers is likely to be an incentivised review that hides the incentive, and eBay's policy bans exchanging feedback to build scores.
- **Removing bad reviews from your own site.** Suppressing genuine negative reviews is banned.
- **Buying from a supplier or service that promises reviews.** Offering review services is itself banned, and using them puts your account at risk as well.
