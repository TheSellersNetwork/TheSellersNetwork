This is for anyone who buys branded goods in shops or online and resells them on Amazon, and has been told either "arbitrage is illegal" or "it's totally legal, brands can't stop you". Both are wrong in ways that matter. By the end you will know what the Trade Marks Act 1994 actually says about reselling, where the UK and EEA rules differ for goods from abroad, and why being legal and being allowed on Amazon are two separate questions. This is general information, not legal advice. If a brand's solicitor contacts you, get your own advice.

## The short version

- Section 12 of the Trade Marks Act 1994 says a trade mark is not infringed by using it on goods that the owner, or someone with its consent, put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area ([section 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/12)). This is "exhaustion of rights", and it is why reselling genuine goods is generally lawful.
- It has limits. A brand can still object where there are "legitimate reasons", in particular where the condition of the goods has been changed or impaired.
- Goods first sold outside the UK and EEA, such as in the US, are not covered by section 12. Bringing them in without the owner's consent can infringe.
- The UK recognises goods placed on the EEA market as exhausted in the UK, but not the other way round. Selling UK sourced goods into the EU can need the brand's consent ([GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exhaustion-of-ip-rights-and-parallel-trade)).
- Amazon decides separately what you may sell. Brand and category approval, IP complaints and invoice requests can stop you even when the resale itself is lawful.

## Myth 1: "Arbitrage is illegal"

**Reality:** buying genuine goods and reselling them is not, in itself, unlawful in the UK.

The rule is in [section 12(1) of the Trade Marks Act 1994](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/12): "A registered trade mark is not infringed by the use of the trade mark in relation to goods which have been put on the market in the United Kingdom or the European Economic Area under that trade mark by the proprietor or with his consent."

The government's Intellectual Property Office explains the idea in one line: exhaustion "means that IP rights cannot be used to stop the further distribution or resale of those goods" ([GOV.UK, exhaustion of IP rights and parallel trade](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exhaustion-of-ip-rights-and-parallel-trade)). When the brand sold that bottle of shampoo to a UK retailer, its trade mark rights in that bottle were used up. You can buy it from the shop and sell it on.

Using the brand name to describe what you are selling, on a listing for the genuine item, is part of that resale. Where resellers get into trouble is suggesting they are the brand, an authorised stockist, or connected to it when they are not. Keep listings to plain descriptions of the genuine item.

## Myth 2: "Brands can never stop a genuine resale"

**Reality:** they can in some situations, and the law says which.

[Section 12(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/12) switches off the protection where "there exist legitimate reasons for the proprietor to oppose further dealings in the goods", in particular "where the condition of the goods has been changed or impaired after they have been put on the market", and the objection goes no further than necessary.

In practice, for a reseller, the risk areas are:

- **Changing the goods.** Repackaging, relabelling, splitting multipacks or removing batch codes.
- **Damaged or deteriorated stock.** Crushed boxes, expired or short dated items, products stored badly.
- **Bundles that look official.** A bundle of branded items presented as if the brand made it. Our guide to [bundles and multipacks](/guides/amazon-bundles-and-multipacks) covers Amazon's rules.

Whether a particular case gives the brand a legitimate reason is a question for a court, not a forum thread. The practical point is that "genuine" is necessary but not always sufficient.

## Myth 3: "If it's genuine, it doesn't matter where I bought it"

**Reality:** where the goods were first put on the market matters a great deal.

Section 12 only covers goods put on the market "in the United Kingdom or the European Economic Area". The GOV.UK guidance confirms the government decided in May 2025 to keep this "UK+" regime ([announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/certainty-for-businesses-and-choice-for-consumers-as-uk-maintains-ip-rights-regime)). Under it, once a product has been legitimately sold in the UK or the EEA, the IP owner may not subsequently prevent it from being re-sold in the UK.

That leaves three directions of trade with different answers:

| Goods first sold by or with consent of the brand in | Reselling in the UK | Reselling into the EEA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The UK | Covered by section 12 | May need the brand's consent. GOV.UK says goods placed on the UK market "may no longer be considered exhausted in the EEA" |
| The EEA | Covered by section 12, under the UK+ regime | Covered by the EU's own rules |
| Anywhere else (US, Asia) | Not covered by section 12; importing without consent can infringe | Not covered; Amazon warns sellers to take legal advice |

Amazon says the same thing in its own [Intellectual Property Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201361070?locale=en-GB): rights owners "may prohibit you from importing or selling their goods in the UK unless you, your supplier or an upstream supplier have the rights owner's consent to sell the items in the EEA or UK". It also warns that a brand can object to UK sourced items being sold in Amazon's EU stores if it has not consented to their sale in the EEA.

For arbitrage, this bites in two places. First, **US or grey market stock** bought cheaply to sell in the UK. Second, **selling UK shop stock into Amazon's EU stores** through Pan-European or cross-border listings. Our guide to [selling into the EU from the UK](/guides/amazon-selling-into-the-eu-from-the-uk) covers the logistics; this is the IP side of the same decision. Northern Ireland has its own position for goods coming from the EU, so take advice if that applies to you.

## Myth 4: "If it's legal, Amazon has to let me sell it"

**Reality:** Amazon is a private marketplace and sets its own conditions. Legal and allowed are different questions.

Four Amazon rules catch arbitrage sellers far more often than trade mark law does.

### Brand and category approval

Amazon restricts some brands, categories, subcategories and individual products until you are approved. Its [approval page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G200333160?locale=en-GB) lists the documents to prepare: purchase invoices dated within 180 days, clear product photos, brand authorisation letters where applicable, and safety certifications where required. If you are told "Your account does not qualify", Amazon says the decision cannot be appealed. See our guide to [ungating in the UK](/guides/amazon-ungating-uk).

### IP complaints from brands

A brand can report a listing for infringement and Amazon can remove it. Amazon's policy says that even if you infringe without knowing, "we will still take action and the seller's account might receive a warning or be suspended". Amazon terminates repeat infringers "in appropriate circumstances".

The same page says what to send if you believe the complaint is wrong: reply to the notice with specific reasons and "any invoice or Order ID that demonstrates the authenticity of the product". For multiple warnings, the appeal needs the list of ASINs and at least one of invoices proving authenticity, order IDs showing authenticity, or an authorisation letter from the rights owner "that is not a forwarded email" ([Amazon Intellectual Property Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201361070?locale=en-GB)).

Amazon also says it may refuse to act on notices it identifies as abusive, such as a trade mark registered mainly to pressure sellers and demand payment, and asks you to report those through the appeals process with evidence.

### Authenticity checks

Amazon's [Anti-Counterfeiting Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201165970?locale=en-GB) makes each seller responsible for sourcing only authentic products. Breaches can mean loss of selling privileges, withheld funds and disposal of stock in Amazon's warehouses at your expense. Amazon also says it does "not pay sellers until we are confident that our customers have received the authentic products they ordered".

### The invoice problem

This is where lawful arbitrage most often fails on Amazon. Exhaustion depends on the goods being genuine. Amazon's process for proving that runs on paperwork that shows where the goods came from. A seller who cannot produce it, or whose documents do not match the ASINs, quantity and dates in question, can lose the listing or the account even though every item was genuine. Our guide to [brand complaints and inauthentic claims](/guides/amazon-brand-complaints-and-inauthentic-claims) walks through what to keep and how to respond.

## How to reduce the risk

1. **Keep every purchase record** against the ASIN it was sold on. Order confirmations for online buys, receipts for shop buys, and a note of which listing the stock went to.
2. **Sell only in the condition you bought.** Do not repackage, relabel or remove codes.
3. **Check the brand before you buy.** If Amazon says "Apply to sell", decide whether you can meet the approval requirements before you spend money on stock.
4. **Be careful with imports.** Stock first sold in the US or elsewhere outside the UK and EEA is a different legal position. Take advice before you build a business on it.
5. **Think before you list in the EU.** UK sourced goods may need the brand's consent there.
6. **Answer complaints with evidence, not argument.** Amazon's own page tells you what it accepts.

Our [retail arbitrage guide](/guides/amazon-retail-arbitrage-uk) covers sourcing and paperwork habits in more detail, and our guide to [online arbitrage](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage) covers the same for online buying.

## What goes wrong

- **Relying on "exhaustion" in an Amazon appeal.** Amazon's process asks for invoices, order IDs or authorisation. Quote the law if you like, but send the documents.
- **Assuming a big brand's shop stock is safe everywhere.** Legal in the UK does not mean legal in the EU store.
- **Buying cheap imports because they are "genuine".** Genuine goods from outside the UK and EEA are exactly what section 12 does not cover.
- **Ignoring the first warning.** One complaint is a warning. Several can end the account.

If you have a brand complaint and want a second opinion, post it (with details removed) in [Account health and suspensions](/community/c/amazon-account-health-and-suspensions).
