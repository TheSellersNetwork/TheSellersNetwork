Every big release brings the same row. A new Pokémon set, a retiring LEGO set or a new console sells out in minutes, and within hours the same boxes are listed on eBay at well above the shop price. Collectors and parents call it scalping. Resellers call it trading. For anyone who buys new releases to sell on, the argument matters: it affects how retailers set limits, how platforms treat listings and how buyers see you. This post sets out what is legal, what retailers and manufacturers have said, the strongest case on each side, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Reselling ordinary goods, including cards, toys and consoles, above the shop price is legal in the UK.
- Using software to buy tickets beyond a sale limit, with a view to profit, is a criminal offence. That law covers event tickets only.
- Retailers set their own purchase limits and can cancel orders that break them. LEGO's UK shop and Nintendo's UK store both publish limits.
- The Pokémon Company has said publicly that it reprints products in high demand and that Pokémon Center uses a virtual queue for some releases.
- Resellers argue they take the risk and provide a service; collectors argue they take stock that was meant for fans.
- Restocks and reprints can bring resale prices down, so buying to resell early carries real risk.

## What is happening

Limited or high-demand releases often sell out on launch day. Some of that stock is bought by people who plan to resell it, and some of those resellers use automated tools to buy faster than a person can.

Manufacturers and retailers have responded in two ways: making more, and limiting how much any one buyer can take.

**More stock.** In March 2025, The Pokémon Company International published a statement saying some fans were having difficulty buying certain Pokémon Trading Card Game products "due to very high demand". It said it was printing more of the affected products "at maximum capacity", was "maximizing production" for future expansions, and would reprint products to replenish stock at retailers including Pokémon Center ([Pokémon: update on TCG product availability](https://www.pokemon.com/us/pokemon-news/update-on-pokemon-trading-card-game-product-availability)). The statement was published on Pokémon's US site; it does not give UK-specific stock figures.

**Limits.** LEGO's UK shop says it sets "a limit per household on most items", that the limit applies across all your orders rather than per order, and that orders may be cancelled if you have already reached it ([LEGO Shop order limits](https://www.lego.com/en-gb/service/help-topics/article/lego-shop-order-limits)). LEGO's [UK terms](https://www.lego.com/en-gb/page/terms-and-conditions) say the shop only sells to consumers and that it may cancel accounts or orders placed to get round the limits. Nintendo's UK store says that for products "in limited quantity or in high demand" it may apply "a strict one per customer / per household limit", and that customers who place multiple orders will have them cancelled and reduced to one ([My Nintendo Store products](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Support/Purchases-Subscriptions/My-Nintendo-Store-Products-3057869.html)). Pokémon's statement says Pokémon Center uses a virtual queue for certain products when traffic is high, and uses technology to get products "into the hands of fans first and foremost".

## The case for reselling limited stock

Resellers make these arguments.

**It is legal and it is trade.** Buying something at the shop price and selling it for what buyers will pay is how every market works. Nobody is forced to buy from a reseller, and the price only holds if someone is willing to pay it.

**Resellers carry the risk.** They tie up cash in stock, pay fees and postage, and take the hit when a reprint or restock lands and prices fall. If the product turns out to be less popular than expected, they sell at a loss.

**They provide a service.** Not everyone can queue online at 9am on a weekday or visit a shop on launch day. Paying a reseller is a choice some buyers make to save time, especially for items that have left the shops.

**Retail stock would sell out anyway.** High demand is created by the product, not by resellers. Without resellers, the same items would still sell out, just to a different set of early buyers.

**Staying within limits is fair play.** A reseller who buys the permitted quantity has done nothing a collector could not also do. If retailers want to limit resale, they can set tighter limits.

## The case against

Collectors, parents and many fans argue the other way.

**It takes stock from the people it was made for.** Children's toys and trading cards are meant to be played with and collected. When resellers buy up launch stock, a child's birthday present suddenly costs twice as much.

**Bots are not a fair fight.** A person clicking a button cannot compete with software placing dozens of orders in seconds. Critics say bot buying is scalping by definition, whatever the law says about goods.

**Getting round limits is dishonest.** Using several addresses, cards or accounts to beat a household limit breaks the retailer's terms. LEGO and Nintendo both say they will cancel orders placed that way.

**It damages the hobby.** Fans say high resale prices put new collectors off, sour the community and push manufacturers into queue systems and restrictions that make buying harder for everyone.

**The "service" is one the buyer did not ask for.** Critics point out that the shortage the reseller is "solving" is partly one the reseller created by buying in bulk.

## What the rules and the law say

**Tickets are different.** Under the [Breaching of Limits on Ticket Sales Regulations 2018](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/735/made), it is an offence to use software designed to get round a ticket sale process, with intent to obtain tickets beyond the sales limit, with a view to financial gain. It covers tickets for recreational, sporting or cultural events in the UK, and the penalty is a fine in England and Wales and up to £50,000 in Scotland ([regulation 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/735/regulation/5/made)). In November 2025 the government also announced plans to make reselling live event tickets above face value illegal ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-bans-ticket-touting-to-protect-fans-from-rip-off-prices)). None of this covers cards, toys or consoles.

**Goods are not covered by a scalping law.** A private member's bill presented in February 2021 would have prohibited "the automated purchase and resale of games consoles and computer components" ([TheyWorkForYou](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/debates/?id=2021-02-03b.970.4)), but it did not become law. Reselling goods above the retail price is legal.

**Retailers' terms still bind you.** Breaking a purchase limit is not a crime, but it breaks the contract. The retailer can cancel orders and close accounts, and LEGO's terms say it only sells to consumers, not businesses.

**Platform rules apply to the listing.** eBay's [presale policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/presale-listings-policy?id=4252) says listings for items you do not yet have must be marked as presale in the title and description, must state when the item will be available, and must guarantee posting within 40 business days of purchase. Counterfeit cards and sets are illegal to sell, and our guide to [spotting fakes before you buy](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy) covers the checks.

**If you do it for profit, you are trading.** That brings tax duties and, on eBay, business seller status. See [when eBay says you are a business seller](/blog/when-ebay-says-you-are-a-business-seller).

Our view: using bots to buy event tickets beyond a sale limit is a crime, and getting round a retailer's purchase limits breaks its terms. Neither is something this site will help with.

## Questions to think about

1. Is there a difference between buying one extra to sell and buying the maximum of every release?
2. Should children's toys be treated differently from consoles or collectables aimed at adults?
3. If a manufacturer reprints and prices fall, who carried the risk, and does that change your view of the profit?
4. Should the UK extend the ticket bots offence to goods, as a 2021 bill proposed?
5. Is a reseller who stays within every limit doing anything a collector would object to?
