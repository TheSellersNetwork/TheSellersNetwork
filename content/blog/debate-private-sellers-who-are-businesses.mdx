On eBay and Vinted, a private seller and a business seller can list the same jacket side by side. The private seller pays no selling fee on either platform and does not owe buyers the full set of consumer rights. The business seller pays fees, offers a 14 day right to cancel and may be keeping full tax records. When the private seller is quietly buying stock to sell on, business sellers call it unfair competition. Others say the line between clearing out and trading is blurry, and that the answer is clearer rules rather than suspicion. This post sets out both sides and the rules, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal or tax advice.

## The short version

- eBay and Vinted both say that if you buy items to resell for profit, you should be on a business or Pro account.
- UK private sellers on eBay pay no final value fee in most categories. Business sellers pay a final value fee, a per-order fee and a regulatory operating fee, plus VAT on those fees.
- Business sellers must give consumers the 14 day right to cancel under the Consumer Contracts Regulations. Private sellers do not, though they must still describe items accurately.
- Pretending to be a consumer when you are trading is one of the banned practices listed in UK consumer law.
- Your tax position is a separate question, decided by HMRC's badges of trade, not by the account type you pick.
- There is no published sales number on either platform that makes you a business.

## What is happening

### The platforms' rules

eBay's [business seller policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/business-seller-policy?id=4710) says a seller "must register as a business if, for example, they sell items they have bought to resell, they make items in order to sell them, or if they buy items for their business". It adds that business sellers "can't represent themselves as private individuals", and that breaking the policy can lead to listings being removed, warnings, restrictions or suspension.

Vinted's [commercial selling rules](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/4/1120-commercial-selling) say standard accounts "can't be used to sell items professionally", including "buying or sourcing items and reselling them at a profit". If you "sell on Vinted to make a profit", Vinted says you need to sign up as a Pro seller. It warns that it may restrict accounts, and may permanently ban an account that does not comply after a warning.

Neither platform publishes a number of sales or a turnover figure that makes you a business. Our explainer on [when eBay says you are a business seller](/blog/when-ebay-says-you-are-a-business-seller) goes through eBay's wording in detail.

### What each account type costs

The difference in cost is real. On eBay, from eBay's own fee pages (our [eBay fee calculator](/tools/calculator/ebay) uses the same figures):

| | Private seller | Business seller |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Final value fee | None for UK private sellers, except motors ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/selling-fees?id=4822)) | Percentage by category, for example 11.9% for clothes ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/business-selling-fees?id=4809)) |
| Per-order fee | None | £0.30 for orders of £10 or less, £0.40 over £10 |
| Regulatory operating fee | None | 0.35% |
| VAT on eBay's fees | Not applicable | Added, because eBay's business fees exclude VAT |
| Fee paid by the buyer | Buyer Protection fee: £0.10 plus a sliding percentage ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling/pricing-items/buyer-protection-information-sellers?id=5595)) | None; eBay says business listings include Buyer Protection at no extra cost to buyers |

**An example.** Say a jumper sells for £20 and the buyer pays £20 in total. As a UK private seller you pay no selling fee; the buyer pays a Buyer Protection fee of £0.10 plus 7% of £20, which is £1.50, on top. As a business seller in clothing, you pay 11.9% of £20 (£2.38), a £0.40 per-order fee and 0.35% (£0.07), which is £2.85 before VAT and £3.42 with VAT at 20%. The buyer pays no extra fee.

On Vinted, sellers on standard accounts pay no selling fee and the buyer pays a fee on top ([Vinted](https://www.vinted.co.uk/how_it_works)). Buyers from Pro sellers pay a separate Buyer Protection Pro fee, which Vinted says is usually 3% to 8% of the item price plus £0.30 to £0.80 for orders under £500 ([Vinted](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/908-buyer-protection-pro-fee)).

### What each account type owes buyers

A business selling to consumers at a distance must offer the right to cancel. GOV.UK says you must offer a refund if a customer tells you within 14 days of receiving an item that they want to cancel, and refund within 14 days of getting it back ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds)). Vinted's help for buyers says items from Pro sellers can be returned within 14 days of receiving them ([Vinted](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/886-the-buyers-right-of-withdrawal)).

Private sellers carry less. Citizens Advice has described buying from a private seller as "buyer beware": the seller cannot misdescribe the item, but can leave information out ([Citizens Advice, 2018](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/about-us/media-centre/press-releases/citizens-advice-warns-buyers-need-to-beware-as-thousands-of-people-report-being-ripped-off-on-online-marketplaces/)). Our post on [eBay private sellers, returns and the law](/blog/ebay-private-sellers-returns-and-the-law) covers what that means in practice.

## The case for: it is unfair competition

Business sellers argue that the rules exist and some people ignore them. In their words: we pay fees, VAT on those fees, and the cost of 14 day returns, and we price with all of that built in. A seller who buys the same stock at the same car boot and lists it on a private account starts with a cost advantage they have not earned.

They point out that buyers are affected too. A buyer who thinks they are dealing with a person clearing a wardrobe does not expect the right to cancel, so does not miss it. But if the seller is in fact trading, that buyer has been denied rights the law gives them. Business sellers say this is not a technicality: it is exactly the situation the ban on posing as a consumer was written for.

They also argue it hurts the honest middle. Someone who does the right thing, registers, and starts offering returns can find their prices undercut by a seller who has not. Over time, they say, the incentive runs the wrong way, and the platforms' own rules become a tax on honesty.

Finally, business sellers say the platforms have the data. eBay and Vinted can see who lists 40 pairs of the same trainers in a month, and both already say they will restrict accounts that break the rules. They would like to see that enforced more consistently.

## The case against: the line is blurry and the burden falls on the small

Sellers on private accounts, and many who have moved between the two, argue that most people are not dodging anything. They are clearing out their own things, or flipping the odd item they picked up cheaply, and the point at which that becomes "a business" is genuinely unclear. Neither platform publishes a threshold. HMRC's own test has nine factors and says no single one decides it.

They say the consequences of getting it wrong are lopsided. Switching on eBay cannot be undone ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/account/signing-ebay-account/signing-ebay-account?id=4191)), fees start on every sale, and offering 14 day returns on low-value second-hand clothes can wipe out the margin. For someone making a few hundred pounds a year, that can mean giving up rather than going legitimate. Pressure to switch, they argue, lands hardest on people with the least room.

They also question how much of an advantage there really is. Buyers on eBay pay a Buyer Protection fee on private listings, so the private seller's price is not what the buyer pays. On Vinted, Pro buyers pay a different fee, not necessarily a higher one. And many business sellers earn more because they sell more, with better photos, faster dispatch and repeat buyers, not because private sellers are cheating.

Finally, some say enforcement risks catching the wrong people. A heavy clear-out after a house move can look like trading from the outside. Automated checks that treat volume as proof of a business could restrict people who are doing nothing wrong.

## What the rules and the law say

**Platform rules.** On both eBay and Vinted, buying to resell for profit means a business or Pro account. That is the platforms' rule, whatever you think of it. Our guide to [Vinted Pro and selling as a business](/guides/vinted-pro-and-selling-as-a-business) explains the Vinted side.

**Consumer law.** The [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, Schedule 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20) lists practices that are unfair in all circumstances. Paragraph 25 is "falsely claiming or creating the false impression that the trader is not acting for purposes relating to the trader's business or falsely representing oneself as a consumer". Traders selling to consumers at a distance must also meet the [Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents), including the 14 day right to cancel.

**Tax.** Tax is a separate question from account type. HMRC decides whether you are trading using the badges of trade, and its manual says the presence or absence of any one badge "is unlikely, by itself, to provide a conclusive answer" ([HMRC](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim20205)). If you are trading, the trading allowance means you do not need to tell HMRC if your gross trading income is £1,000 or less in a tax year ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/tax-free-allowances-on-property-and-trading-income)). Platforms report sellers who make 30 or more sales, or receive about £1,700 or more, in a calendar year, but GOV.UK says a report "does not automatically mean you owe tax" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/selling-goods-or-services-on-a-digital-platform)). Our post on [HMRC and your Vinted sales](/blog/hmrc-and-your-vinted-sales-what-is-true) separates the facts from the rumours. VAT registration only becomes compulsory when taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in 12 months ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration/when-to-register)), so most small resellers on either kind of account do not charge VAT.

Our view: if you are buying to sell for profit, the platforms' rules say you need a business or Pro account, and presenting yourself as a private seller while trading is a banned practice under consumer law. Where the line sits for occasional sellers is the genuine debate.

## Questions to think about

- Should eBay and Vinted publish a clear threshold, even a rough one, for when a seller must switch?
- Is a private seller who flips a few charity shop finds a year really competing with a full-time business?
- Would you want platforms to use sales volume to flag accounts, knowing it could catch people having a big clear-out?
- If the business account were cheaper or easier, would more people switch?
- As a buyer, do you check whether a seller is private or a business before you buy?
