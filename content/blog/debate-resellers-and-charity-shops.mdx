Few arguments come up more often in reselling circles than this one. Some shoppers and volunteers say resellers sweep through charity shops, take everything worth having and leave the rails bare for people who need cheap clothes. Resellers reply that they pay whatever the shop asks, buy more than almost anyone else and turn stock over faster. The argument matters to anyone who sources from charity shops, because it shapes how shops price, how volunteers treat regular buyers and whether you are welcome. This post sets out what is known, puts both sides as their supporters would, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal or tax advice.

## The short version

- There are more than 9,900 charity shops in the UK, and the sector returned more than £300m in profit to charities in 2024/25, according to the Charity Retail Association.
- Charities have said publicly that the quality of donations has fallen, and they link that mainly to people selling their own things online rather than donating them.
- Resellers argue that the charity gets exactly the price it set, whoever buys the item.
- Critics argue that resellers take the best stock, and that shops respond by pricing from online sold prices, which pushes prices up for everyone.
- Many charities now sell their better donations online themselves, on their own shops and on the big marketplaces.
- Buying from a charity shop to resell is legal. Once you do it for profit, platform rules and tax rules treat you as a trader.

## What is happening

The [Charity Retail Association's statistics page](https://www.charityretail.org.uk/charity-shop-stats) puts the sector at more than 9,900 shops, more than 24,900 full-time equivalent staff and more than 223,500 volunteers, with more than £300m in profit going to parent charities in 2024/25. It gives an average transaction of £7.51 for April to June 2026, from a sample of 35 charities, and median prices of £5.21 for womenswear and £6 for menswear.

On pricing, the association says decisions are often left to shop and assistant managers, "often using pricing guides supplied by the parent charity", and that more valuable and unique items such as antiques, jewellery, art and musical instruments are often sold online to reach the widest audience ([how charity shops work](https://www.charityretail.org.uk/how-do-charity-shops-work)). Textiles that do not sell usually go to textile recyclers.

Charities have become sellers on the same platforms resellers use. Oxfam says it runs more than 500 shops plus a "fast-growing online charity shop" ([Oxfam: shop with us](https://www.oxfam.org.uk/get-involved/shop-with-us/)), and that its eBay shop has been running for 17 years, with eBay sales up by over 200% since eBay for Charity began supporting Second Hand September in 2019 ([Oxfam and eBay for Charity](https://www.oxfam.org.uk/get-involved/how-companies-can-partner-oxfam/our-corporate-partners/oxfam-and-ebay-for-charity/)). A guest post on the association's own site, looking back at 2025, describes charity retailers selling across eBay, Vinted, Vestiaire Collective and Depop ([Charity Retail Association news](https://charityretail.org.uk/news/what-2025-taught-charity-retailers-about-e-commerce)).

The strongest recent evidence on donations comes from research commissioned by the wealth manager Rathbones in July 2026, reported by [UK Fundraising](https://fundraising.co.uk/2026/09/09/resale-sites-hit-charity-shop-donations/). Of 100 senior executives at charities that run shops, 38% said donation quality had fallen since the rise of Vinted, eBay and Depop, 42% saw no change and 18% said items had improved. Of 1,000 UK adults, 21% said they keep their better items back to sell online, and 9% said they had stopped donating and now sell online instead. Note what that research measures: people selling their own things, not resellers buying from shops.

## The case against resellers

Critics, among them shoppers and some volunteers, make these points.

**The best stock goes first, and to the same few people.** A charity shop is meant to offer affordable second-hand goods to the local community. When a small number of regular buyers visit several shops a day and take every branded or collectable item, ordinary shoppers see only what is left.

**Resellers push prices up.** Shops that feel they are being picked over start checking eBay and Vinted before pricing. That can mean higher prices across the board, which hits the customers who rely on charity shops to dress their families cheaply. The association's own figures show that 73% of people surveyed in November 2024 agree charity shops provide affordable goods ([statistics](https://www.charityretail.org.uk/charity-shop-stats)); critics say that reputation is what is at stake.

**The charity leaves money on the table.** If an item sells for £4 in the shop and £30 online, critics say the £26 difference should be going to the cause, not to a trader.

**It changes the feel of the shop.** Volunteers give their time to a charity, not to support other people's businesses. Some find it uncomfortable to see the same buyers every morning scanning barcodes on their phones.

## The case for resellers

Resellers, and some in charity retail, argue the opposite.

**The charity gets exactly what it asked for.** A charity shop sets its own prices. When a reseller pays the ticket price, the charity receives the same money as it would from any other shopper. Resellers point out that it is the shop's choice to price an item at £4 rather than research it.

**Stock moves, and space costs money.** Items that sit on the rail take up space that newer donations could use. Resellers say they buy things other shoppers pass over, including items that would otherwise go to textile recyclers, and that fast turnover is good for any shop.

**The real pressure is on donations, not sales.** The public statements from charities point mainly at donors. The Charity Retail Association told The Telegraph in April 2025 that many members say they no longer get the high-quality items they used to, and Sue Ryder and Oxfam both linked the change to people selling their own items online ([The Telegraph, via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/vinted-killed-charity-shop-120000360.html)). Resellers argue they are being blamed for a donations problem they did not create.

**Charities are resellers too.** Many charities sell their better donations online at market prices. Resellers ask why the same activity is fine when a charity's e-commerce team does it and suspect when an individual does.

**Items get a second life.** Something bought in a charity shop and sold to a buyer who wanted exactly that item has been reused, not landfilled or shipped abroad as bulk textiles.

## What the rules and the law say

Buying from a charity shop to resell is legal. There is no law that stops you buying as many items as a shop will sell you. A shop can set its own policy, for example on limits, reserving items or selling to traders, and it is entitled to refuse a sale.

Once you buy to resell, platform rules treat you as a business seller. Vinted's [Catalogue Rules](https://www.vinted.co.uk/catalog-rules) say standard accounts cannot be used to list items "you've specifically bought or sourced to resell"; that is what [Vinted Pro](/guides/vinted-pro-and-selling-as-a-business) is for. eBay has its own tests, covered in [when eBay says you are a business seller](/blog/when-ebay-says-you-are-a-business-seller).

For tax, buying goods to sell at a profit is likely to be trading, and if your trading income is over £1,000 in a tax year you need to tell HMRC ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-tell-hmrc-about-your-income-from-online-platforms)). Platform reporting to HMRC does not create a new tax; it reports sales that were already taxable. See [HMRC and your Vinted sales](/blog/hmrc-and-your-vinted-sales-what-is-true).

The practical side, including how to behave in a shop and why haggling takes money from the charity, is in our [charity shop sourcing guide](/guides/charity-shop-sourcing).

## Questions to think about

1. If a charity prices an item at £4 and you sell it for £30, has the charity lost anything, or has it simply chosen not to do the research?
2. Should charity shops price from eBay and Vinted sold prices, even if that makes them less affordable for regular shoppers?
3. Is there a difference between one reseller buying a few items a week and several buyers clearing the rails every morning?
4. If donors are keeping their best things to sell online themselves, are resellers the problem or a convenient target?
5. Would you tell a volunteer you resell if they asked, and should shops ask?
