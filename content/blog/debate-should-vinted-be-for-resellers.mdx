Vinted began as a place to sell clothes you no longer wear, and much of its appeal still rests on that. It is also where a great many resellers now sell charity shop, car boot and clearance finds. People clearing their wardrobes sometimes feel crowded out by sellers with hundreds of listings; small second-hand businesses feel they are doing exactly what Vinted's buyers want. Vinted's own rules draw a clear line, and its new terms take effect in October 2026. This post sets out what the rules say, the strongest case on each side, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal or tax advice.

## The short version

- Vinted's rules say standard accounts are for private, non-commercial use. If you buy or source items to resell, Vinted says you need a Vinted Pro account.
- Vinted Pro is free to join, has no listing limit for second-hand items, and comes with business duties such as a 14-day right for buyers to withdraw from a sale.
- Selling on a standard account is free; the buyer pays a Vinted fee on top of the item price.
- People selling their own clothes argue resellers crowd the feed and change the feel of the app.
- Resellers argue that buyers want the stock, and that a second-hand item is second-hand whoever sells it.
- Vinted's updated UK terms apply from 8 October 2026 and must be accepted to keep using the app.

## What is happening

Vinted's [Catalogue Rules](https://www.vinted.co.uk/catalog-rules) say that "Standard Vinted accounts are for private, non-commercial use only". A standard seller cannot use Vinted to list items bought or sourced to resell, to earn a regular income through reselling, or to run a reselling business, even one that is legally registered. The [commercial selling help page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/4/1120-commercial-selling) repeats that standard accounts "can't be used to sell items professionally", including "buying or sourcing items and reselling them at a profit".

For businesses, Vinted offers Vinted Pro. Its [Pro page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/pro) says that if you want to sell "a high volume of items on Vinted (more than just clearing out your closet)", you need to sign up as a professional seller, and that Pro is free, with unlimited second-hand listings. Some things are not allowed on any account: Vinted does not allow bulk inventory resale, listing new items as second-hand, or, for Pro sellers, reselling cosmetic and beauty items.

The fee model is the same idea on both sides. Vinted's [How it works page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/how_it_works) says there are zero selling fees. The buyer pays a fee at checkout; Vinted's [fee help page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/1654) calls it the Vinted fee and says it is usually 3% to 8% of the item or bundle price plus £0.30 to £0.80, varying by item and order. So Vinted earns from buyers on every sale, whoever the seller is.

Vinted's [Pro guide](https://www.vinted.co.uk/pro-guide) says a professional "must declare" themselves as a Pro seller, and that Vinted can ask you to create a Pro account if it has information showing you meet the criteria. Its [account blocks page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/92-why-was-my-account-blocked) lists commercial selling on a standard account as a reason for a temporary ban, and repeated commercial selling as a reason for a permanent one, after which Vinted will require you to convert to Pro within six months of the ban notice.

The [new UK terms from 8 October 2026](/blog/vinted-terms-october-2026) rename the Buyer Protection fee as the Vinted fee and tighten the counterfeit rules, giving sellers 48 hours instead of 24 to prove an item is genuine when Vinted has strong evidence it is fake. They do not change the split between standard and Pro accounts.

## The case for keeping Vinted for people clearing wardrobes

People who sell their own things make these arguments.

**That is what Vinted says it is.** The rules are clear that standard accounts are for personal use. Members who follow them feel they are competing with people who do not, and they ask why the rules are not enforced more firmly.

**Resellers crowd the feed.** A reseller with hundreds of listings, relisting often, can take up a lot of space in search. Someone listing a dozen of their own items finds it harder to be seen.

**Prices creep up.** Resellers price from sold data to make a margin. Private sellers say that changes buyers' expectations and makes the whole app feel less like a clear-out and more like a shop.

**Buyers deserve to know who they are dealing with.** A business seller owes buyers more under consumer law, including a right to cancel. A reseller on a standard account gives buyers less protection than they should get. Vinted Pro exists so that buyers can see a "Pro" badge and know where they stand.

**It keeps things fair between sellers.** Pro sellers accept slower payouts, published business details and a 14-day withdrawal right. A reseller who avoids Pro gets the benefits of a business without the duties.

## The case for welcoming resellers

Resellers, and many buyers, argue the other way.

**Second-hand is second-hand.** A pre-loved jacket is the same jacket whether it came from someone's wardrobe or a charity shop rail. Buyers come to Vinted for the stock and the prices, not to check the seller's reasons.

**Resellers bring good stock.** Small second-hand businesses find, clean, photograph and describe items well, often better than a private seller clearing a cupboard. Buyers benefit from the choice and the quality.

**It keeps items in use.** Resellers rescue items that might otherwise be sold as bulk textiles or thrown away. That fits Vinted's own second-hand message.

**Vinted Pro is the answer, not a ban.** Resellers point out that Vinted does not say businesses are unwelcome; it says they belong on Pro. Registering, showing your business details and honouring buyers' rights is the fair route, and many small sellers are glad to do it.

**Vinted earns from their sales.** The buyer's fee is charged on every order, including those from business sellers. Resellers argue that a platform earning from their sales should be glad of them.

## What the rules and the law say

The platform rules are set out above: buying to resell on a standard account breaks Vinted's rules, and Vinted can ban accounts that do it and require them to move to Pro. The practical steps, from registering to the 14-day withdrawal right, are in our guide to [Vinted Pro and selling as a business](/guides/vinted-pro-and-selling-as-a-business).

The law sits behind the rules. Vinted's Pro guide says failing to declare yourself as a professional can lead to sanctions under the [Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents). A business selling to consumers must give buyers certain information and a right to cancel most distance sales within 14 days ([GOV.UK: accepting returns](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds)).

For tax, buying goods to sell at a profit is likely to be trading, and trading income over £1,000 in a tax year must be reported to HMRC ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-tell-hmrc-about-your-income-from-online-platforms)). Platform reporting does not create a new tax. See [HMRC and your Vinted sales](/blog/hmrc-and-your-vinted-sales-what-is-true).

If your account has already been restricted, [banned on Vinted: how to appeal](/blog/banned-on-vinted-how-to-appeal) covers the process, and [what Trading Standards says about Vinted](/blog/what-trading-standards-says-about-vinted) covers the consumer law side.

Our view: the rules are clear. If you buy to resell on Vinted, you should be on Vinted Pro.
