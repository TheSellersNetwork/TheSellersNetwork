Most resellers have seen it: an item on eBay or a small web shop at three times the price of the identical thing on Temu or AliExpress. Some call it arbitrage, the same thing retailers have always done. Others call it misleading, especially when the listing hints at "handmade", "UK stock" or "fast delivery" that is not true. The argument matters because it touches platform rules, product safety law and how buyers see second-hand and small sellers in general. This post sets out both sides at their strongest, the rules that apply, and ends with a poll. It does not name any seller. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Buying low and selling higher is legal in the UK. The questions are about how it is done and described.
- eBay allows dropshipping from a wholesale supplier, but not listing an item and then buying it from another retailer or marketplace that ships straight to your buyer.
- Etsy only allows items made, designed, handpicked or sourced by the seller. Reselling ready-to-use new goods is not allowed, though genuine craft supplies are.
- Under the UK General Product Safety Regulations 2005, a UK business that brings in goods from outside the UK can count as the "producer" if the manufacturer has no UK presence, with the safety duties that go with it.
- Goods sold by a trader must match their description, and false or misleading information about a product is an unfair commercial practice.

## What is happening

Overseas shopping apps sell very cheap goods direct to UK consumers. Resellers buy from them for two different reasons, and it helps to keep them apart.

1. **Buy, hold and resell.** The reseller orders stock, receives it in the UK, checks it, photographs it and sells it on eBay, Etsy, Vinted Pro, Facebook or their own site, usually at a mark-up.
2. **List first, order later.** The reseller lists an item, and only when it sells orders it from the app with the buyer's address as the delivery address.

The second is what most platforms mean by "retail dropshipping", and it is the one the platform rules deal with most directly. Both raise questions about product safety and descriptions.

## The case for: it is just retail

Resellers who defend the practice make these points.

**Every shop marks up.** High street retailers buy from manufacturers and wholesalers, often overseas, and sell for more. A reseller who buys from an app, pays for the stock, takes the risk it will not sell, checks the quality and handles the customer service is doing the same job at a smaller scale.

**Buyers are paying for something real.** Supporters argue that UK stock arriving in a few days, a seller who answers messages in English, easy returns under UK consumer rules and a checked item are all worth paying for. Not every buyer wants to install another app, wait for delivery from abroad or risk getting the wrong thing.

**Curation has value.** Finding the one good product among thousands of listings, testing it and photographing it properly takes time. Supporters say that is no different from a charity shop reseller picking out the good stock.

**It is honest if the listing is honest.** Nothing in UK law requires a seller to tell a buyer where they bought their stock or what they paid. Supporters say the line is crossed by false claims, not by the mark-up itself.

## The case against: it trades on buyers not knowing

Critics, including many resellers who do not do it, argue the opposite.

**The mark-up relies on an information gap.** Critics say the model only works because the buyer does not know the same item is a few clicks away for a fraction of the price. It may be legal, but they see it as trading on ignorance rather than adding value.

**Listings drift into untrue claims.** The real problem, critics argue, is that these listings often borrow words that are not true: "handmade", "designed by me", "small UK business", "vintage", or product photos lifted from the app. On Etsy in particular, a mass-produced item presented as handmade misleads buyers and undercuts genuine makers.

**Delivery promises get broken.** With list-first dropshipping, the seller has no stock and no control over when the parcel leaves. Buyers shown a next-day or two-day estimate may wait weeks, and the tracking often does not match the item location in the listing.

**Nobody has checked it is safe.** Critics point to electricals, chargers, toys, cosmetics and children's items. If the reseller never handles the product, nobody in the UK has checked it before it reaches a child's bedroom. Even if they do handle it, few small sellers can tell whether a charger meets UK safety standards.

**It damages trust in small sellers.** Every buyer who discovers they paid triple for an app item, critics say, is a little less willing to buy from the next small seller.

## What the rules and the law say

### eBay

- **Dropshipping.** eBay's [dropshipping policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/drop-shipping-policy?id=4176) says dropshipping "where you fulfil orders directly from a wholesale supplier" is allowed, and that you remain responsible for delivery and the buyer's satisfaction. It also says: "Listing an item on eBay and then purchasing the item from another retailer or marketplace that ships directly to your customer is not allowed on eBay." Ordering from a consumer shopping app to the buyer's address falls into that second category.
- **Item location.** eBay's [item location policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/listing-policies/item-location-policy?id=4244) requires the postcode, city and country the item will post from, consistent across the title, description and item specifics, and a tracking number that matches the location in the listing. "UK stock" on an item posted from abroad breaks this.
- **Delivery estimates.** eBay's [estimated delivery dates page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/posting-items/estimated-delivery-dates-sellers?id=4086) says the date shown to buyers is worked out from your dispatch time, the delivery service, your past delivery performance, the item's location and the buyer's address, and that missing your dispatch time can affect your late delivery rate.
- **Business sellers.** Buying to resell makes you a business seller on eBay. See [when eBay says you are a business seller](/blog/when-ebay-says-you-are-a-business-seller).

### Etsy

Etsy's [Creativity Standards](https://www.etsy.com/uk/legal/creativity) allow four types of item: made, designed, handpicked or sourced by the seller. Vintage must be at least 20 years old. Craft supplies and party supplies for a specific event can be resold, but ready-to-use goods such as blank t-shirts, generic household items and ready-to-wear clothing cannot. So a bag of beads bought in bulk from an app may be allowed as a craft supply; a finished necklace bought from the same app and listed as handmade is not. Our guide to [Etsy's vintage rules](/guides/etsy-vintage-rules) and our note on the [Creativity Standards](/blog/etsy-creativity-standards) cover the detail. Etsy's pages change, so check the current version.

### Product safety

In Great Britain, the [General Product Safety Regulations 2005](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/general-product-safety-regulations-2005/general-product-safety-regulations-2005-great-britain) require products placed on the market to be safe. The regulations separate producers and distributors:

- A **distributor** is a professional in the supply chain whose activity does not affect the product's safety. Distributors must act with due care to help make sure only safe products are supplied.
- A **producer** includes the manufacturer, but where the manufacturer is not established in the UK and has no representative here, it includes "the person established in the United Kingdom that places a product from a country outside the United Kingdom on the market" ([regulation 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1803/regulation/2)).

A UK reseller who brings goods in from an overseas app and sells them on as a business may therefore carry the producer's duties, including making sure the product is safe, giving safety information and making it traceable. Whether that applies to you depends on the facts, so check the GOV.UK guidance or ask Trading Standards.

For sales into Northern Ireland and the EU, the EU General Product Safety Regulation applies instead, with its own listing requirements. See [GPSR for Northern Ireland and the EU](/blog/gpsr-product-safety-ni-eu).

The rules are changing. The [Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/20/contents) gives the government powers to write new product safety rules, and a [GOV.UK consultation on a new product safety framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/product-regulation-the-uks-new-product-safety-framework) to replace the 2005 regulations ran from 31 March to 23 June 2026. At the time of writing the outcome has not been published.

### Honest descriptions

- Under the [Consumer Rights Act 2015, section 11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/11), goods supplied by a trader by description must match that description.
- Under the [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/section/226), giving false or misleading information about a product that is likely to change a consumer's decision is a misleading action, and unfair commercial practices are prohibited ([section 225](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/section/225)).

Marking up is not illegal. Calling a mass-produced item handmade, or claiming UK stock when it is not, can be.

## Questions to think about

- Is a mark-up fair if the buyer could easily find the same item cheaper, or is that simply how retail works?
- Should sellers have to say where their stock comes from, even when the law does not require it?
- If you resell electricals or children's items from overseas apps, how do you check they are safe?
- Is holding stock in the UK and checking it before sale enough to make this practice acceptable?
- Have you lost sales to listings you believe are dropshipped from an app?
