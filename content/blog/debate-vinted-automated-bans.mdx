Few things worry Vinted sellers more than logging in to find the account restricted or banned. In August 2026 the BBC reported sellers saying they had been wrongly suspended or banned, with some suspecting automated systems were getting fraud and counterfeit decisions wrong. Vinted's answer is that it combines people and tools, and that appeals are reviewed by people. The argument matters to every reseller on the platform: too little enforcement lets scammers and bots run free, too much catches honest sellers who rely on the income. This post sets out both sides and what UK data protection law says about automated decisions, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Vinted's terms say it may use automated tools to detect and act on rule breaches, and that appeals are reviewed under the supervision of qualified staff.
- The BBC reported in August 2026 that sellers said they had been wrongly suspended or banned. Vinted told the BBC that human review teams assess appeals.
- Nobody outside Vinted knows how many bans are wrong. Vinted does not publish an error rate for its UK moderation.
- Online purchase scams are a large and growing problem, and banks' trade body has called on marketplaces to do more to verify sellers.
- UK data protection law gives safeguards where a significant decision is made solely by automated means, including the right to obtain human intervention and to contest the decision.

## What is happening

### What Vinted's rules say

Section 7 of Vinted's [terms](https://www.vinted.co.uk/terms-and-conditions), in the version that applies from 8 October 2026, says: "We may sometimes use automated tools to detect and act on these issues." It says Vinted will tell you in writing when it takes a corrective action, that the notice will tell you about your right to appeal, and that "We'll review your appeal as soon as we can under the supervision of qualified staff."

Vinted's [ban and appeal help page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/92) says temporary bans lift automatically after 7 to 30 days, depending on the reason. The reasons it lists include offensive behaviour, repeatedly selling prohibited items, counterfeit goods, and selling commercially without Pro status. Permanent bans are for more serious breaches, such as fraud and multiple accounts. You can appeal "only once, and within 6 months" of the moderation notice.

On its [trust and safety page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/safety), Vinted says it runs systems that "work to automatically block suspicious content in messages", much like email spam filters, and uses "predictive analysis to try to detect suspicious activities, announcements, or orders".

### What was reported

In August 2026 the BBC published a report headlined "Banned over a pair of £2 sandals" about sellers complaining that they had been suspended or banned without a clear explanation ([BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2kwnrn5zpo)). Sellers told the BBC they suspected automated systems were wrongly flagging items, including second-hand branded clothing, as counterfeit. The report said a campaign website set up by an affected seller had collected more than 2,600 reports of bans or suspensions. Those reports are self-submitted and have not been independently checked.

Vinted told the BBC that its moderation combines trained teams, member reports and detection tools, that users can appeal decisions, and that human review teams assess appeals.

We cannot say how many bans are wrong, and neither can anyone outside Vinted.

### The problem Vinted is trying to stop

Marketplace fraud is not imaginary. UK Finance, the banking trade body, said in June 2026 that purchase scams made up 71% of authorised push payment fraud cases in 2025, with losses up 20% to £118.1 million ([UK Finance](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/press-release/fraud-report-2026-press-release)). It called on "online marketplaces to verify their sellers and use secure payment mechanisms". Those figures cover purchase scams across all platforms and payment types, not Vinted in particular.

Vinted's terms also ban bots, scrapers and other outside software. Our guide to [Vinted relisting and automation rules](/guides/vinted-relisting-and-automation-rules) sets out what they say.

## The case for: automation is the only way to keep a big marketplace safe

Supporters of Vinted's approach, including many buyers and plenty of sellers, argue that scale leaves no choice. A platform with millions of listings cannot have a person check every one. Scammers use scripts, fake accounts and stolen photos, and they move fast. Only automated systems can spot patterns across thousands of accounts in time to stop them.

They say the cost of being too slow is paid by ordinary members. A buyer who loses money to a fake listing, or a seller who ships to a fraudster, suffers real harm. Every counterfeit that gets through makes buyers less willing to pay fair prices for genuine branded items, which hurts honest sellers too.

They also point out that the system has checks built in. Vinted says people review appeals. Temporary bans lift on their own. The terms require a written notice with an appeal route. Supporters argue that a system which occasionally gets it wrong but offers a way back is better than one that lets fraud through to avoid inconveniencing anyone.

Finally, they note that some bans that feel unfair are simply the rules being applied. Vinted's own rules say standard accounts are not for buying to resell, and that bots and outside software tools are banned. A seller who breaks those rules and is caught by an automated check has still broken the rules.

## The case against: honest sellers pay for the system's mistakes

Critics, including sellers who say they were wrongly banned and the campaigners who took their stories to the BBC, argue that the system is too quick to act and too slow to correct itself. In their words: an account can be closed in seconds, but getting a real answer can take weeks, and in the meantime listings vanish and orders are cancelled.

They say the explanation is often too thin to appeal against. A notice that names a broad category, such as fraud or counterfeits, does not tell a seller which item, message or action triggered it. With only one appeal allowed, sellers argue they are being asked to disprove an accusation they cannot see.

They also argue that the stakes are high. For many people, Vinted is a meaningful part of their income. Losing an account, and possibly access to a balance where there is a fraud risk, is not a minor inconvenience. Critics say decisions with that kind of effect should involve a person before the ban, not only after it.

Finally, they question the lack of numbers. Vinted does not publish how many UK accounts it restricts, how many appeals succeed, or how often its systems are wrong. Without that, critics say, "human review teams assess appeals" is a reassurance nobody can check.

## What the rules and the law say

**Your contract with Vinted.** The terms allow automated detection, require a written notice with an appeal route in most cases, and say appeals are reviewed under the supervision of qualified staff. They also allow only one account, so opening a new one while banned breaks the terms. Our guide on [being banned on Vinted and how to appeal](/blog/banned-on-vinted-how-to-appeal) walks through the process.

**UK data protection law.** The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) says people have protection against decisions based solely on automated processing where the decision affects their legal rights or other equally important matters ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/your-rights-relating-to-decisions-being-made-about-you-without-human-involvement/)). The ICO notes that guidance is under review because of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. Its [summary of the changes](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/legislation-we-cover/data-use-and-access-act-2025/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-summary-of-the-changes/data-protection/) says organisations can now make solely automated decisions in a wider range of situations, as long as they provide safeguards: information about the decision, a way to make representations, a way to obtain human intervention, and a way to contest the decision. It also says a decision is solely automated "if there is no meaningful human involvement in taking it".

Two limits are worth knowing. The safeguards apply only to decisions with no meaningful human involvement, so if a person genuinely reviewed a ban, they do not apply. And whether a marketplace ban counts as a "significant" decision has not, as far as we know, been ruled on by the ICO for marketplaces specifically.

**Myths.** A lot of what circulates about why accounts get flagged is guesswork. Our post on [Vinted algorithm myths](/blog/vinted-algorithm-myths) tests the common claims against what Vinted actually says.

## Questions to think about

- Should a person always look at a permanent ban before it takes effect, even if that slows down action against fraud?
- Would you accept a few wrong bans if it meant far fewer scams?
- Should Vinted publish UK figures on bans, appeals and reversals?
- Is one appeal enough, or should there be a second, independent step?
- If you rely on Vinted for income, does that change what you think is fair? Our earlier debate on [private sellers who are really businesses](/blog/debate-private-sellers-who-are-businesses) touches on the same question from another angle.
