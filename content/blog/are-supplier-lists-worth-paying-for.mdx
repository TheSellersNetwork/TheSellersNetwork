This is for resellers who have seen a "hidden supplier list", "verified wholesaler database" or "my personal suppliers" offered for a fee and are wondering whether to buy. It explains what those lists usually are, how genuine wholesale buying works in the UK, and a checking routine you can run on any supplier before you pay. The short answer is that a list is rarely worth money, and checking a supplier properly is free.

## The short version

- Most paid supplier lists are collections of publicly findable businesses: trade directories, brand distributors and liquidation sellers. The work you need to do (opening an account, negotiating, checking stock) is still yours.
- A supplier that will sell to anyone who bought the same list is, by definition, not giving anyone an edge.
- Real wholesalers want to know who you are, usually need business details, set minimum orders and give you prices after you apply.
- Check every supplier: Companies House, VAT number, contact details, references and a small first order. Report Fraud (which replaced Action Fraud) publishes a [supplier checking guide](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/know-your-suppliers/) that is worth reading in full.
- Never pay a new supplier by bank transfer for a large first order on trust.

## What paid supplier lists usually are

Lists sold to resellers tend to fall into a few types. None of them is automatically fraudulent, but none of them is what the word "hidden" implies.

| What is on the list | What it really is | What it is worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big-name wholesalers and cash and carries | Businesses that advertise openly and want new trade customers | Nothing you could not find with a search |
| "Brand direct" contacts | General contact addresses for brands or their UK distributors | A starting point. Whether they sell to you depends on your business, not the list |
| Liquidation and returns pallet sellers | Companies selling mixed or returned stock, often advertised openly | Useful if you know how to value a pallet. Risky if you do not |
| Overseas factories and marketplaces | Listings from large business-to-business platforms | Available free on the platforms themselves |
| Unnamed "trusted contacts" | Individuals selling stock through messages or social media groups | The highest risk category. Check everything below before paying |

The seller of the list usually earns once, from you. The suppliers on it do not pay to be there, may not know they are on it, and have no obligation to give you the terms the list promised. Some lists are also sold with a "members only" discount code or referral link, which means the list seller is paid a second time when you buy.

### The logic problem

If a supplier really sold branded stock well below market price to anyone who asked, every reseller would already be buying from them and the market price would fall to meet them. A list sold to hundreds of buyers turns any real advantage into shared stock with shared prices. Our post on [why leads lists stop working](/blog/why-leads-lists-stop-working) walks through the maths.

## How real wholesalers work

Genuine trade suppliers in the UK tend to follow a similar pattern. Terms vary, so treat this as what to expect rather than a rule.

1. **You apply for a trade account.** Expect to give your business name, address and contact details. Many ask for a company number or VAT number, and some will only deal with VAT-registered or limited company buyers.
2. **They check you.** Some want evidence that you are a real business, such as a website, shop or selling account. Brands may want to know where and how you sell, to protect their pricing and image.
3. **You get a price list.** Trade prices are usually shown only to approved accounts. Prices may be tiered by volume.
4. **There is a minimum order.** Either a minimum spend or minimum quantities per line.
5. **Payment starts on proforma.** New accounts usually pay in advance. Credit terms come later, if at all.
6. **You get invoices.** A proper invoice with the supplier's legal name and address, and a VAT number if they charge VAT. You need these for your records and, on Amazon, for [ungating](/guides/amazon-ungating-uk) and authenticity checks.

What this means for you: a supplier who asks nothing about you, has no trade account process, and is happy to sell a single pallet of big-brand stock at a fraction of retail is not behaving like a normal wholesaler. That does not prove fraud, but it is the pattern to be careful with.

For more on buying wholesale, see [Amazon wholesale and private label, honestly](/guides/amazon-wholesale-and-private-label-honestly) and [sourcing for resale](/guides/sourcing-for-resale).

## How to check a supplier

Report Fraud's [know your suppliers](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/know-your-suppliers/) guidance is written for businesses of any size. Its main points: do your research, check that the supplier is who they say they are, check you get what you pay for, and keep checking over time. The steps below turn that into a routine.

### Before you contact them

- **Search the name with "scam", "complaints" or "fraud".** Report Fraud suggests exactly this.
- **Check Companies House.** Use the free [company search](https://www.gov.uk/get-information-about-a-company) to confirm the company exists, is active, and that its name, number, registered address and directors match what the supplier tells you. A company that was set up last month, or that has overdue accounts, is worth a closer look.
- **Check the VAT number** if they give one, using HMRC's [VAT number checker](https://www.gov.uk/check-uk-vat-number). The name it returns should match the business you are dealing with.
- **Look at their contact details.** A landline, a trading address you can check and a company email domain are signs of a real business. Free email accounts and mobile-only contact are not proof of anything, but add them to the picture.

### Before you pay

- **Ask for references** from other trade customers, and contact them yourself. Report Fraud also suggests references from industry contacts.
- **Ask for a sample or a small first order.** A genuine wholesaler will usually accept a smaller opening order to build a relationship, even if the per-unit price is higher.
- **Ask about the stock's origin** for branded goods. If they will not say where it came from, you cannot show it is genuine. Selling counterfeits puts your selling accounts at risk; see [avoiding VeRO and suspensions](/guides/avoiding-vero-and-suspensions).
- **Pay in a way that gives you protection.** A card payment may give you a chargeback route, and credit card payments can have extra protection; the [Financial Ombudsman Service](https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/consumers/complaints-can-help/credit-borrowing-money/goods-services-bought-credit) explains how both work, including that Section 75 does not cover debit cards. If you are buying as a business, or on a business card, ask your card provider which of these protections apply.

### After you start buying

- **Set one point of contact.** Report Fraud recommends contacting the supplier through their verified company switchboard and agreeing a single point of contact for changes.
- **Never change bank details from an email.** Report Fraud describes a common fraud where someone impersonates a known supplier and asks for payments to go to a new account, sometimes after building a relationship for months. Always confirm by phone using a number you already had.
- **Check deliveries against invoices.** Count, inspect and photograph stock on arrival.
- **Watch for change.** Report Fraud notes that slipping service standards can be a sign of financial pressure, and that a supplier heading for insolvency may take orders it knows it cannot fulfil.

## Red flags for a supplier

| Red flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Prices far below any other trade source for branded goods | The most common sign of counterfeit, stolen or non-existent stock |
| Bank transfer only, to a personal account | No card protection, and the account holder may not be the business |
| Pressure to pay today to "secure the pallet" | Stops you checking |
| No company number, or details that do not match Companies House | You cannot tell who you are dealing with |
| Stock photos only, no photos of the actual goods | You have no idea what you are buying |
| Refusal to invoice properly | You cannot prove authenticity or claim costs in your records |
| Found only through a paid list or private group | No public reputation to check |

## So, is a list ever worth it?

Rarely. A list can save a beginner a few hours of searching, but the value is the searching method, not the names. You can build your own list for free with directories, trade shows, brand websites and the checks above, and you will know how each supplier was checked. That knowledge is what protects your money.

If you do buy a list, treat every name on it as unverified and run the full checking routine. Never treat inclusion on a paid list as a recommendation.

## If a supplier lets you down

- **Contact your bank or card provider straight away** if you paid and the goods did not arrive or were not as described.
- **Report fraud** to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/), the national fraud reporting service for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. In Scotland, contact Police Scotland on 101.
- **Report the list seller** to Trading Standards through the [Citizens Advice consumer service](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/) if the list was misdescribed, and to the [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html) if it was advertised with misleading claims.

This is general information, not legal or financial advice.

## What goes wrong

- **Trusting the list instead of checking.** The list seller is not responsible for the supplier.
- **A large first order.** Start small, even if the price per unit is worse.
- **Paying by bank transfer to a new supplier.** It is the hardest payment to get back.
- **Skipping invoices.** Without them you cannot prove what you bought or where it came from.
- **Following a change of bank details by email.** Always call.
