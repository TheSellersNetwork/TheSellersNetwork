Anyone who has sold at a busy car boot sale knows the scene: you pull on to the field before it is light, and before the engine is off there are people with torches at the windows asking what is in the boxes. For resellers this is often where the best buys happen. For the person trying to unpack a car, it can feel like being mobbed. This post sets out what organisers' published rules say, puts the case for and against early buying as each side would, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal advice, and every sale sets its own rules.

## The short version

- Car boot sales are run by private organisers, councils, schools and charities, and each one sets its own times and rules.
- Several organisers publish a clear time before which no buying is allowed, and some sell a paid early entry for buyers.
- Resellers say early buying is how car boots have always worked, and that sellers are free to say no.
- Sellers and some organisers say buying before a pitch is set up is rude, pressures people into bad prices and can be unsafe.
- Nothing in UK law bans buying early; it is a matter of the organiser's rules and ordinary manners.
- If you break an organiser's rules, they can remove you from the site or ban you.

## What is happening

Most car boot sales let sellers in first to park and set up, then open to buyers later. In between, buyers who arrive early often walk the rows as sellers arrive, sometimes with torches if it is still dark, to get first pick. Some organisers accept this and charge for it. Others ban it outright.

Here is what a few organisers publish. These are examples, not a survey, and rules change, so check the sale you are going to.

| Sale | What its published rules say |
| --- | --- |
| Chiswick Car Boot Sale, London | Sellers' vehicles are let in from about 5.30 to 5.45am. Main sales hours are 6.30am to 12.30pm, and "no buyers are allowed on site before 6.30am" ([sellers page](https://chiswickcarbootsale.com/sellers)) |
| Shepperton Car Boot Sale, Surrey | Sellers are not let in before 6.30am. Early bird buyers are let in at around 7.30am and there is no buying before then, including by other sellers. The organiser says it holds early buyers back "to give sellers time to set up without being bothered by buyers" ([guidelines](https://www.sheppertoncarboot.co.uk/guidelines)) |
| Stubbings, Berkshire | In its 2024 seller terms: sellers enter from 8am, early buyers from 9am at £2 a person, general admission free from 10am, and "selling is not permitted before 9am. Please focus on setting up your own stall" ([seller terms, PDF](https://stubbingsnursery.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/CB-Sellers-Terms.pdf)) |
| Armagh City, Banbridge and Craigavon Borough Council | The site opens to traders only at 7am; public entry commences at 8am, and "entry will not be permitted before this time". The council can bar anyone who does not follow the rules ([customer agreement, PDF](https://www.armaghbanbridgecraigavon.gov.uk/download/192/car-boot-sale-customer-agreement/16549/car-boot-sale-customer-agreement.pdf)) |

Two patterns stand out. First, the organisers who write rules about this tend to protect set-up time, and some apply the same ban to sellers buying from each other. Second, where early buying is allowed, it is often turned into a paid early entry, so the organiser rather than the crowd decides when buying starts.

Guidance for school and charity organisers from [PTA+](https://www.pta.co.uk/events/step-by-step/step-by-step-car-boot-sale/) suggests planning how and when sellers and buyers arrive, using separate gates if possible, and giving sellers an advice sheet when they register.

## The case for early buying

Resellers who buy early make these arguments.

**Early is how car boots work.** The best items go in the first hour, and everyone knows it. Sellers who arrive at a busy sale know there will be buyers waiting, and many come expecting to sell some of their load before they have even unpacked.

**Sellers are free to say no.** Nobody is forced to sell from the boot. A seller who wants to set up first can say so, lock the car, or ask people to come back later. Early buyers say most take no for an answer.

**It helps plenty of sellers.** A quick sale of a box of items at the car door means less to unpack, less to carry home and cash in hand early. Some sellers, especially regular traders, welcome it.

**The torch is practical.** In autumn and winter the sale opens in the dark. Buyers argue a torch is simply the only way to see what is on offer, not a sign of bad intent.

**Pay the early fee and you are within the rules.** Where an organiser sells early entry, the early buyer has paid for exactly this, and the organiser has decided it is acceptable.

## The case against early buying

Sellers, and organisers who ban it, argue the other way.

**It is rude and it is pressure.** Being surrounded before you have opened the boot, with people reaching into boxes and asking prices in the dark, is stressful, especially for someone clearing out their own home for the first time. Sellers say it pushes them into prices they would not accept in daylight.

**It breaks the organiser's rules.** Where a sale says no buying before a set time, buying early is not a grey area. The organiser has made the rule to protect sellers, as Shepperton says in terms, and early buyers who ignore it risk the sale for everyone.

**It is unsafe.** Vehicles are moving on to the field in the dark. People walking between cars with torches, sometimes crowding a car that is still parking, create an obvious risk.

**It takes the fairness out of it.** Other buyers wait at the gate as instructed. If a handful of people are already on the field buying, the rule-followers lose out.

**It is hard to keep an eye on your things.** Sellers worry that while they are crowded during unloading, they cannot watch everything at once.

## What the rules and the law say

There is no UK law against buying from a seller before a car boot sale officially opens. The rules that apply are the organiser's, and they are a condition of being on the site. Organisers can refuse entry, remove people and ban them from future sales; the Armagh council agreement says so in terms, and most private organisers reserve the same right.

Trading rules still apply to what happens at the pitch. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's [Business Companion guide to car boot sales](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/car-boot-sales) says there is no hard and fast rule on who counts as a trader, but buying goods to resell and selling regularly are signs of trading. If you are trading, the goods you sell must be safe, as described and of satisfactory quality. Buying from a private seller gives you far fewer rights, so check items before you pay, which is harder in the dark.

Selling counterfeit goods is a criminal offence wherever it happens ([GOV.UK: IP crime and enforcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses)), and several organisers list counterfeit or copied goods as banned from their sites ([Stubbings seller terms](https://stubbingsnursery.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/CB-Sellers-Terms.pdf)).

## Questions to think about

1. If a sale does not publish any rule on early buying, is it fair game, or should ordinary manners fill the gap?
2. Is a paid early entry a fair answer, or does it just let those who can pay jump the queue?
3. Should the same rules apply to sellers buying from each other while everyone sets up?
4. Where is the line between asking "anything in the boot?" and crowding someone who is trying to unpack?
5. If you sell at boot sales as well as buy, does your view change depending on which side of the car you are on?
