Resellers hear two things about their work. One is that buying and selling second-hand is one of the most useful things an ordinary person can do for the environment. The other is that it is just more vans, more plastic bags and more stuff, moved around faster. Both get said with confidence, and both lean on figures that are easy to misquote. This post sets out what reputable sources actually say, puts the strongest case on each side, and ends with a poll so you can say where you stand. It is general information, not a verdict: where a figure comes from a platform's own report, we say so.

## The short version

- UK government policy ranks preventing waste first and preparing things for re-use second, above recycling, in the legal "waste hierarchy".
- WRAP, the UK waste charity, says keeping clothes in use for just nine months longer could cut their carbon, water and waste footprints by up to 20%.
- WRAP also found that about half of used textiles in the UK still end up in the bin.
- Vinted and eBay both publish large "avoided emissions" figures. These are the companies' own claims, built on their own methods and surveys.
- Vinted says 94% of the emissions from its sales and running its business are delivery-related.
- The honest answer probably turns on one question: does a resale replace a new purchase, or add to it?

## What is happening

### The law puts reuse near the top

In England and Wales, the [Waste (England and Wales) Regulations 2011](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2011/988/regulation/12) set out the waste hierarchy as a priority order: prevention, then preparing for re-use, then recycling, then other recovery such as energy recovery, and disposal last. Selling on something that still works sits close to the top of that list. Recycling, which gets most of the public attention, sits below it.

### What WRAP says about clothes

WRAP runs the UK Textiles Pact (formerly Textiles 2030) with retailers, charities and reuse organisations. Two of its findings come up again and again in this argument.

- On lifespan, WRAP says [extending the life of clothing by nine months could reduce carbon, water and waste footprints by up to 20%](https://www.wrap.ngo/resources/case-study/extending-product-lifetimes-wraps-work-clothing-durability).
- On waste, WRAP's [April 2024 report on the UK textile market](https://www.wrap.ngo/media-centre/press-releases/price-our-addiction-cheap-fast-fashion-pressure-builds-uk-second-hand) found that 49% of used textiles end up in rubbish bins, about 711,000 tonnes a year, or around 35 items per person. It said the UK bought 1.42 million tonnes of textiles in 2022 and generated 1.45 million tonnes of used textiles the same year. Of the textiles thrown away in household waste in England, 84% was incinerated with energy recovery and 11% went to landfill.

The same report found that prices paid per tonne for used textiles had fallen over ten years, by 57.5% for textile banks and 41% for charity shops, and WRAP's chief executive said "We're all buying too many new items". That last point matters for both sides of this debate.

### What the platforms say about themselves

Both big second-hand marketplaces publish impact figures. They are not independent audits, and each uses its own model.

- **Vinted** says that in 2025 its members [avoided 1,607 kilotonnes of CO2e](https://company.vinted.com/sustainability) by buying second-hand, that 76% of orders avoided the purchase of a new item, and that each second-hand item avoided 2.39kg of CO2e on average. It says the data comes from a survey of over 64,000 members and its internal climate model. On the same page, Vinted says: "Vinted sales and running our business create emissions. 94% of those are delivery-related".
- An earlier Vinted report, covering 2023 and produced with the climate tech company Vaayu, gave [679 kilotonnes avoided and a 40% share](https://company.vinted.com/newsroom/impact-report) of transactions that replaced a new purchase. The two reports use different surveys and methods, so the numbers should not be compared as a trend.
- **eBay** says in its [2025 Impact Report](https://www.ebayinc.com/stories/news/ebay-publishes-2025-impact-report/), published in May 2026, that recommerce on its site in 2025 helped avoid 1.6 million metric tons of carbon emissions and divert more than 71,000 metric tons of waste from landfill. The announcement does not set out how those figures are calculated.

### Parcels and packaging

Every online resale usually means at least one parcel. Carriers publish their own emissions figures, and they vary by operator, vehicle and route, so there is no single number for "a parcel". Packaging is also part of the picture. Large businesses are now covered by packaging extended producer responsibility, but GOV.UK says it only applies to organisations with a turnover of £1 million or more that supplied more than 25 tonnes of packaging in the previous year ([GOV.UK: EPR for packaging](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/extended-producer-responsibility-for-packaging-who-is-affected-and-what-to-do)). Almost every small reseller falls below that, so what goes in the post is down to your own choices.

## The case for: resale is one of the greenest things you can do

Resellers who see their work as good for the planet usually make these points.

**Reuse beats making new.** Most of a garment's or gadget's footprint is in making it, not moving it. The waste hierarchy puts re-use above recycling for that reason, and WRAP's nine-month finding shows how much a longer life saves. A reseller who finds a jacket at a car boot and gets it to someone who wears it for another two years is doing exactly what WRAP describes.

**Resellers rescue things from the bin.** WRAP says about half of used textiles still go into general waste. Resellers argue they are one of the few groups with a financial reason to find, clean, mend and relist items that would otherwise go to incineration or landfill, including stock charity shops cannot sell. Whether resellers help or hurt charity shops is its own argument, covered in [are resellers emptying charity shops?](/blog/debate-resellers-and-charity-shops).

**The platforms' own numbers are big.** Even allowing that they are self-reported, Vinted and eBay both say most or many second-hand purchases replace new ones, and both put the avoided emissions in the hundreds of thousands to over a million tonnes a year.

**Delivery is the part you can fix.** Supporters accept that parcels have a footprint, but say it is the easiest part to reduce. Reusing boxes and mailing bags, sending to lockers and drop-off points, and bundling orders all cut it, and our guide to [packaging cheaply](/guides/packaging-cheaply) shows the cheap option and the lower-waste option are often the same one.

## The case against: it is still more stuff, more vans and more plastic

Sceptics, including some resellers, push back like this.

**It only helps if it replaces something new.** Every avoided-emissions figure rests on an assumption about what the buyer would otherwise have done. If cheap second-hand makes shopping feel guilt-free, people may simply buy more. WRAP's own line, that we are all buying too much, cuts both ways: a busy resale market can sit alongside rising new sales rather than replacing them.

**The big numbers are marketing.** Sceptics point out that the platforms' figures are produced by the platforms, rely partly on members' survey answers about what they would have bought instead, and are not independently audited in the way a regulator's statistics would be. Two Vinted reports with different methods gave very different replacement rates. That does not make the figures wrong, but it does mean they should be treated as claims, not facts.

**Delivery is the biggest part of the platforms' own footprint.** Vinted itself says 94% of the emissions from its sales and operations are delivery-related. A single item bought at a charity shop and carried home on foot has almost none of that. A £3 top sent across the country in a new plastic mailer, and sometimes sent back again, is a different story.

**Returns pallets and liquidation stock are not really reuse.** Some resellers buy [returns pallets and liquidation stock](/guides/returns-pallets-and-liquidation-stock). Sceptics argue that this is mostly new stock being moved again, sometimes several times, with a share that turns out to be broken or unsellable and ends up binned by the reseller instead of the retailer.

**Fast turnover encourages throwaway buying.** If a reseller's model depends on cheap fast-fashion stock bought in bulk, the argument goes, it is part of the same cycle WRAP describes, not a way out of it.

## What the rules and the law say

- **The waste hierarchy is law, but it binds waste handling, not shoppers.** Regulation 12 of the [2011 regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2011/988/regulation/12) sets the priority order for anyone dealing with waste. It does not say any particular resale business is good for the environment.
- **Green claims in your own listings must be true.** The Competition and Markets Authority's [Green Claims Code](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-claims-code-making-environmental-claims/environmental-claims-on-goods-and-services) applies to businesses making environmental claims. Its six principles include that claims must be truthful and accurate, must consider the full life cycle of the product, and must be substantiated. If you call your shop "sustainable", you need to be able to back that up. The platforms' figures are their claims about their whole marketplace, not evidence about your items.
- **Packaging EPR is for larger businesses.** GOV.UK says organisations under £1 million turnover, or handling under 25 tonnes of packaging, have no obligations under packaging EPR ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/extended-producer-responsibility-for-packaging-who-is-affected-and-what-to-do)). Check the current page if your business grows.
- **Selling items for re-use is covered by the usual consumer rules.** If you sell as a business, your buyers have the same rights as with any other trader. See [eBay private sellers, returns and the law](/blog/ebay-private-sellers-returns-and-the-law) for how that differs from selling your own things.

## Questions to think about

- When you buy stock, how much of it do you think your buyers would otherwise have bought new?
- Should platforms' impact figures be independently checked before they are used in adverts?
- Does selling returns and liquidation stock count as reuse, or is it just retail with extra steps?
- What have you changed about your own packaging or postage to cut waste, and did it cost you anything?
- If a buyer asked you whether your shop is "green", what would you honestly say?
