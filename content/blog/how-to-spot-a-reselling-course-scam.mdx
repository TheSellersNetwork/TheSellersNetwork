This is for anyone being sold a course, mentorship or "done-for-you" store that promises reselling income. It gives you a checklist of warning signs, the UK rules each one runs into, and the US cases where the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) took ecommerce schemes to court. Not every paid course is a scam, but the bad ones follow a pattern, and you can check for it in ten minutes. This is general information, not legal or financial advice.

## The short version

- Big earnings screenshots, lifestyle videos and "my students made £X" claims are the most common sign. UK advertising rules say income claims must be representative of what most people get, not the best result.
- A "done-for-you" store that someone else runs on your behalf is the model behind several of the largest US enforcement cases.
- Countdown timers, "only three places left" and "free for 48 hours" are banned in the UK when they are false.
- If the price is only revealed on a sales call, or a "free" training leads straight to a payment, slow down.
- If you are pushed towards a loan or finance to pay for it, stop.
- If a creator recommending a course is paid for that recommendation and does not say so, that is itself a breach of the rules.

## Why the US cases matter here

Most of the big, documented cases about ecommerce money-making schemes come from the US, because the FTC has sued several of them and published its complaints and court orders. These are **US cases under US law**. They are useful because they show, on the official record, what these schemes promised and how they worked. The UK has its own rules, covered below and in our post on [what the ASA says about earnings claims](/blog/what-the-asa-says-about-earnings-claims).

Where a case was settled, the defendants agreed to a court order; settlement is not the same as a finding at trial, and the descriptions below are the FTC's allegations and the terms of the orders, as the FTC published them.

| Case (US) | What the FTC alleged | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Automators AI](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/02/ftc-action-leads-ban-owners-automators-ai-e-commerce-money-making-scheme) | Promised "passive investment income" from AI-powered Amazon and Walmart stores; most clients did not make the promised earnings; Amazon and Walmart suspended or terminated stores for policy breaches; contract clauses stopped customers posting negative reviews | February 2024: monetary judgment of $21.8 million (partly suspended), permanent ban on selling ecommerce business opportunities or coaching for most defendants |
| [Ecommerce Empire Builders](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/05/ftc-action-ends-ecommerce-empire-builders-online-business-opportunity-scam) | Training costing nearly $2,000 and "done for you" storefronts costing up to $35,000, with promises of large monthly profits that did not materialise | May 2025: ban on selling business opportunities, assets handed over for refunds |
| [Ascend Ecom](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/06/ftc-case-leads-order-banning-ascend-ecom-its-owners-business-opportunity-marketing) | Charged tens of thousands of dollars for stores on major marketplaces plus tens of thousands more for inventory; claimed AI tools would bring passive income; tried to stop customers complaining or posting reviews; at least $25 million in consumer losses | June 2025: proposed order banning the owners from business opportunity and coaching sales, $25 million judgment (partly suspended) |
| [Ecom Genie and related companies](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/07/ftc-action-against-e-commerce-business-opportunity-scam-results-permanent-bans-owner-his-companies) | Promised "$100K+ per month" in sales and "million-dollar" stores; most customers lost tens of thousands of dollars and many took on substantial debt | July 2025: permanent bans, judgment of nearly $14 million (partly suspended) |
| [Click Profit](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-case-against-e-commerce-business-opportunity-scheme-its-operators-results-permanent-ban-industry) | Promised guaranteed "passive income"; falsely claimed advanced AI and exclusive partnerships with major brands; threatened customers and used contract clauses to suppress negative reviews | August 2025: permanent bans, judgments of $13.6 million and $7.3 million (partly suspended) |

The FTC also described a case against [FBA Machine, also known as Passive Scaling](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes), which it said promised "7-figure businesses", falsely guaranteed refunds and cost consumers more than $15.9 million.

Read the pattern, not the brand names. None of these schemes sold "a course" in the plain sense. They sold the idea that paying enough money would make the income happen for you.

## The red-flag checklist

Work through this before you pay anything. One flag is a reason to ask questions. Three or more is a reason to walk away.

| Red flag | What it looks like | The rule it runs into |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earnings screenshots | Dashboards, bank balances, "£20k months", students' best results | Earnings must be representative ([CAP Code 20.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html)); results must not be implied as typical ([ASA, April 2026](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/money-making-courses-stick-to-the-facts.html)) |
| Done-for-you stores | "We build and run your Amazon store, you collect profit" | The model behind most of the FTC cases above |
| Countdowns and scarcity | Timers that reset, "doors close tonight", "last two spots" | False time limits are a banned practice ([DMCC Act, Schedule 20, paragraph 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20)) and break [CAP Code 3.30](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| Price only on a call | "Book a call to see if you qualify" with no price anywhere | Business opportunity marketing must state the investor's financial commitment ([CAP Code 20.8](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html)) |
| "Free" that is not free | Free webinar or guide that only works if you buy the full programme | Calling something free when you must pay for it is banned ([DMCC Act, Schedule 20, paragraph 23](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20)) |
| Finance pressure | "Most students use finance", a loan link on the call, "you can't afford not to" | The FTC recorded customers left with substantial debt ([Ecom Genie](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/07/ftc-action-against-e-commerce-business-opportunity-scam-results-permanent-bans-owner-his-companies)) |
| Undisclosed affiliates | Creators "honestly recommending" a course they earn commission on | Ads must be obviously identifiable ([ASA affiliate guidance](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/affiliate-marketing.html)) |
| Review gagging | Contract terms that stop you posting a negative review | Named in several FTC orders; suppressing genuine negative reviews is covered by [CMA fake reviews guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews) |

### Earnings screenshots

A screenshot of a sales dashboard tells you revenue, not profit. It says nothing about stock cost, fees, returns, refunds or the months that went badly. It also says nothing about the hundreds of buyers who did not get that result.

UK advertising rules deal with this directly. Business opportunity ads must not contain "unrepresentative or overstated earnings figures" ([CAP Code 20.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html)), and the [ASA's advice on earnings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recruitment-and-business-opportunities-earnings-claims.html) says the figure should reflect what an average respondent gets, not a few high performers. A small "results not typical" line does not fix it: the ASA ruled in 2025 that such a disclaimer contradicted the headline claim rather than clarifying it ([Self Made Girl Boss Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/self-made-girl-boss-ltd-a25-1293166-self-made-girl-boss-ltd.html)).

**What to ask:** "What did the median buyer earn in their first twelve months, after stock and fees, and can I see the evidence?" A seller with honest numbers can answer. If the reply is another screenshot, that is your answer.

### Done-for-you stores

The pitch is that you pay a large fee, someone else builds and runs a store in your name, and profit arrives. In the FTC cases above, this is where the biggest losses were. In one case the FTC said the marketplaces themselves suspended or terminated the stores for policy breaches ([Automators AI](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/02/ftc-action-leads-ban-owners-automators-ai-e-commerce-money-making-scheme)).

Think about what you would be handing over. The selling account is in your name, so the policy risk, the tax and the customer complaints are yours. Our guides on [Amazon account health](/guides/amazon-account-health) and [avoiding VeRO and suspensions](/guides/avoiding-vero-and-suspensions) explain how quickly an account can be lost when someone else decides what to list.

### Countdowns and "limited places"

Pressure to decide now is the point. In the UK, falsely saying a product or its terms are available only for a limited time, to rush your decision, is one of the practices banned outright by the [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20). The ASA upheld exactly this against a course advertiser whose "FREE for 48 hours" offer was still available after 48 hours ([Charlie Johnson](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/charlie-johnson-a25-1293121-charlie-johnson.html)).

**What to do:** screenshot the timer, close the page, come back tomorrow in a private browser window. If the deadline has moved, you know how the rest of the pitch works.

### Price only on a sales call

A price you can only hear on a call is a price designed around you: your savings, your credit limit, how badly you want out of your job. The CAP Code says business opportunity marketing must give "a statement of the extent of investors' commitments, including any financial investment or outlay", either in the ad or the follow-up literature ([CAP Code 20.8](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html)).

**What to do:** ask for the full price, what is included, the refund terms and the cancellation terms in writing before the call. If they will not send it, do not book the call.

### "Free" that needs a purchase

A free webinar or guide is fine if it is genuinely useful on its own. It is a problem when the "free" part is a teaser for a paid product, or when the ad implies the free material delivers the results. The ASA upheld complaints on this in two 2025 course rulings: one ad implied a paid £390 course was free ([Self Made Girl Boss Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/self-made-girl-boss-ltd-a25-1293166-self-made-girl-boss-ltd.html)), and another implied the advertised results could come from a free training video alone ([Jessica Crane Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/jessica-crane-ltd-a25-1293031-jessica-crane-ltd.html)).

### Finance pressure

Some sellers suggest a loan, credit card or instalment plan to pay for the course, sometimes arranged on the call. If you are told the course will "pay for itself", ask what happens to the repayments if it does not. The FTC said most customers of one US scheme lost tens of thousands of dollars and many ended up in substantial debt ([Ecom Genie](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/07/ftc-action-against-e-commerce-business-opportunity-scam-results-permanent-bans-owner-his-companies)).

If you are offered credit, check the lender on the [FCA Financial Services Register](https://register.fca.org.uk/s/) before you sign anything. Paying by credit card can also give you more protection than other methods; see [refund rights for online courses](/blog/refund-rights-for-online-courses).

### Undisclosed affiliates and paid endorsements

Many "honest reviews" of courses are written by people who earn a commission on each sale. That is allowed if it is clear. The [ASA's affiliate guidance](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/affiliate-marketing.html) says affiliate content must be obviously identifiable as advertising, and that a vague line saying the author "may" receive a commission is unlikely to be acceptable. Reviews that hide the fact they were paid for are also banned under consumer law; our post on [fake reviews](/blog/fake-reviews-are-illegal) explains how.

**What to check:** look for "Ad" at the start of the post, or a clear statement that the creator is paid. If a creator recommends a course in every video and never says whether they earn from it, treat the recommendation as an advert.

### Contracts that stop you complaining

Several of the FTC orders above ban the defendants from using contract terms that stop customers posting reviews or complaints. If a course contract says you cannot "disparage" the company or must take down negative comments to get a refund, read it as a warning about how refunds will go.

## What a reasonable course looks like

Paid education is not a scam in itself. A course or mentor worth considering usually:

- shows the full price, what you get, and the refund and cancellation terms before you pay;
- talks about typical results, or says plainly that most people earn little or nothing, and backs any figure with evidence;
- teaches a method you can check for yourself, such as reading [sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps) or working out fees, rather than selling access to a list or a system;
- tells you about the costs and risks: stock you cannot sell, fees, account suspensions, tax;
- does not rush you, and does not push credit.

Most of what these courses teach is also available free, including in our [guides](/guides). Read those first, then decide whether you are paying for something new.

## If you think you have been caught

1. **Stop further payments.** Cancel any subscription or instalment plan in writing and keep the confirmation.
2. **Check your cancellation and refund rights.** They depend on how and when you bought; see [refund rights for online courses](/blog/refund-rights-for-online-courses).
3. **Talk to your card provider** about a chargeback or, for credit card payments, a Section 75 claim, as early as you can. The [Financial Ombudsman Service](https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/consumers/complaints-can-help/credit-borrowing-money/goods-services-bought-credit) explains both.
4. **Report the ad** to the [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html). Scam ads in paid online space have their own [reporting form](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint/report-an-online-scam-ad.html).
5. **Report the business** to Trading Standards through the [Citizens Advice consumer service](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/).
6. **If you have lost money to fraud,** report it to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/), which has replaced Action Fraud (in Scotland, contact Police Scotland on 101).

Keep every ad, message, contract, invoice and screenshot. The order in which things were promised matters.

## What goes wrong

- **Treating a refund guarantee as proof of honesty.** The FTC said one scheme falsely guaranteed refunds ([FBA Machine](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes)). Read the conditions before you rely on it.
- **Paying more to fix a bad result.** "Your store needs the advanced package" is how a loss grows.
- **Waiting too long to act.** Chargebacks have time limits, usually around 120 days according to the [Financial Ombudsman Service](https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/consumers/complaints-can-help/credit-borrowing-money/goods-services-bought-credit), and the clock may already be running.
- **Staying quiet out of embarrassment.** These schemes rely on it. A report to the ASA or Trading Standards helps the next person.
