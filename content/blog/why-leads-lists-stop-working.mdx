This is for resellers who pay for, or are thinking of paying for, a daily or weekly list of "profitable leads": products to buy from a shop or website and resell on Amazon, eBay or elsewhere. It explains why those leads lose their profit once they are shared, shows the arithmetic, and sets out what to learn instead so you can find your own. It is not about any particular list; it is about how shared leads behave.

## The short version

- A lead is only profitable because few people are acting on it. Sharing it with every subscriber removes that condition.
- When many sellers buy the same item and list it on the same product page, each seller's share of sales falls and the time to sell rises.
- Sellers who want their cash back cut prices. The price falls until the margin that made it a lead has gone.
- The source often sells out or reprices too, so late buyers pay more and sell for less.
- The skill that produced the lead (finding a price gap, checking demand, working out fees) is learnable and does not stop working when others learn it, because everyone applies it to different stock.

## What a leads list is

A leads list is a regular message, spreadsheet or group post saying, in effect: "Item X is on sale at shop Y for £A. It sells on marketplace Z for £B. Profit after fees is £C." Some lists add sales estimates or charts. Subscribers pay a monthly fee to receive them.

There is nothing wrong with the idea. Someone has done real work to find a price gap. The problem is what happens after they send it to everyone at once.

## Where the profit comes from

A price gap exists because the product is cheaper in one place than the price it reliably sells for in another. That selling price is not fixed. It is set by how many units buyers want and how many units sellers are offering.

On a marketplace where many sellers share one product page, such as Amazon, buyers see one product page with one featured offer, and sellers compete to be that offer (see [seller feedback and the Buy Box](/guides/amazon-seller-feedback-and-buy-box)). On platforms where each seller has their own listing, such as eBay or Vinted, buyers see many copies of the same item side by side and pick the cheapest one in good condition. Either way, more sellers means each gets a smaller share, and the cheapest offer tends to win.

## The maths of many buyers on one listing

The figures below are an **illustration** to show the mechanism, not data about any real product.

Say a product sells about 60 units a month on a marketplace at £25. Three sellers are on the listing, so each might expect roughly 20 sales a month at that price. Your cost is £12 and fees and postage come to about £8, leaving £5 a unit.

Now a leads list with a few hundred subscribers sends it out. Suppose 30 of them buy 10 units each.

| | Before the lead | After the lead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sellers on the listing | 3 | 33 |
| Units in stock across sellers | about 60 | about 360 |
| Monthly demand | about 60 | still about 60 |
| Months of stock at current demand | about 1 | about 6 |
| Your likely monthly sales | about 20 | about 2 |

Demand did not change. Supply went up six times. At the old price, it would take about six months to sell the stock that has just arrived, and most of the 33 sellers do not want to wait six months for their money.

So somebody drops the price by 50p. Then somebody else matches and goes 50p lower. Each step makes sense for that seller, because a quick sale at a smaller profit beats a slow one. Together they drive the price down until the margin is gone, or below it, as sellers with the most cash tied up start to sell at a loss.

| Selling price | Fees and postage (illustrative) | Cost | Profit per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| £25.00 | £8.00 | £12.00 | £5.00 |
| £23.00 | £7.60 | £12.00 | £3.40 |
| £21.00 | £7.20 | £12.00 | £1.80 |
| £19.50 | £6.90 | £12.00 | £0.60 |

Fees in the table are made up to show the shape. Real fees depend on the platform, category and size of the item, so always work them out from the platform's own fee page before you buy. Our guides on [where the money leaks on Amazon](/guides/amazon-where-the-money-leaks) and [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps) show how.

### The source side moves too

The same subscribers are all trying to buy from the same shop or website at the same time. Several things happen:

- **Stock sells out.** Early subscribers buy it; later ones find nothing, or buy the last few at full price.
- **Purchase limits appear.** Retailers often limit quantities when they see unusual demand.
- **The deal ends or the price changes.** A clearance line or discount code does not last.

The result is that the people who act last pay the most and sell into the most crowded market. On a list with hundreds of subscribers, most people are not first.

### Returns and slow sellers

Once a listing is crowded, some sellers return unsold stock to the shop if they can, which puts it back into the source for the next wave. Others end up with stock that sits for months. That is where a lead turns into a [stale stock](/guides/stale-stock) problem, and the cost of storage and your time eats what profit was left.

## Why it looks like it works at first

Leads lists often show screenshots of early subscribers selling out on day one. That is real, and it is the point: the first few sellers do get the old price, because the new supply has not landed yet. By the time the rest of the stock arrives, the price has started to fall. A seller who was early once can be late the next ten times.

It is also why a list's own results rarely tell you much. The members who did well post about it. The ones who sold at a loss usually do not.

## What to learn instead

The value in a lead is the method behind it. Learn the method and you can find leads that nobody else has been sent.

### 1. How to check demand

Before buying anything, check how often it actually sells and at what price. On eBay, the [Research tab in Seller Hub](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/product-research?id=4853) shows sold prices and a sell-through rate for recent periods. On Amazon, look at the sales history and the number of offers on the listing. Our guide on [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps) sets out a routine.

### 2. How to count the competition

Look at how many sellers are on the listing now, how much stock they appear to hold, and whether that number has jumped recently. A sudden rise in offers is often a sign that a lead has been shared.

### 3. How to work out true profit

Work out every cost: purchase price, platform fees, postage or fulfilment fees, packaging, returns and your time. Do it from the official fee pages, not from someone else's calculator. If there is no margin left after a modest price drop, the product is not worth buying.

### 4. Where to look that others are not

Shared lists concentrate everyone on the same big retailers and the same clearance events. Your own local shops, niche categories you know well, and sources that need knowledge or effort to use are where your advantage lasts, because they do not scale to hundreds of subscribers. See [sourcing for resale](/guides/sourcing-for-resale), [retail arbitrage](/guides/amazon-retail-arbitrage-uk), [online arbitrage](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage) and [Amazon to Amazon arbitrage](/guides/amazon-to-amazon-arbitrage).

### 5. How much to buy

Size your purchase to the demand you measured, not the stock you could buy. If a product sells 20 a month across all sellers, buying 30 is a bet that everyone else will stop.

## If you already pay for a list

- **Use leads as research, not instructions.** A lead is a hint that a category or retailer is worth looking at. Look at the related products that were not on the list.
- **Check the listing before buying.** If the number of offers has jumped since the lead went out, skip it.
- **Buy small.** Test with a few units, see the real price and the real speed, then decide.
- **Track your own results.** Record every lead you acted on, what you paid and what you sold for. After a few months you will know whether the subscription pays for itself.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying the full quantity the lead suggests.** Everyone else did too.
- **Trusting the profit figure in the lead.** It was calculated at the old price, before the listing got crowded.
- **Selling at a loss out of panic.** Sometimes waiting for other sellers to clear their stock is cheaper. Know your floor price before you buy.
- **Holding stock forever.** The opposite mistake. Set a date to review it and follow your [stale stock](/guides/stale-stock) routine.
- **Paying for a list and a course that sells the list.** Read [how to spot a reselling course scam](/blog/how-to-spot-a-reselling-course-scam) before you pay for either.
