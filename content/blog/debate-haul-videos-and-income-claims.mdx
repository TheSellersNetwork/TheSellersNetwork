Haul videos, "sold this week" roundups and monthly income posts are a big part of how people discover reselling. Plenty of sellers say a video showed them what sells and gave them the push to start. Others say the same videos left them expecting money that never came, or led them to a paid course. This post sets out the argument at its strongest on each side, what the advertising rules say about earnings claims and paid content, and ends with a poll. It does not name any creator, and the point is to weigh the format, not the people. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Haul and income content is not regulated as such. Most of it is ordinary social media posting.
- It becomes advertising when the creator is paid, gets free stuff, earns commission through an affiliate link, or is promoting their own course or product. Then the CAP Code applies.
- Ads must be obviously identifiable. The ASA says "Ad" or "#ad" is the minimum label, and words like "gifted", "sp" or "aff" are not enough.
- Business opportunity ads must not contain unrepresentative or overstated earnings figures, and the ASA says figures should reflect an average person, not the top performers.
- Supporters say the content teaches and motivates. Critics say it shows winners only and leaves out costs.

## What is happening

The formats are familiar. A table of items from a car boot, charity shop or returns pallet, with the price paid and a guess at what each will sell for. A "what sold this week" video. A screenshot of a sales dashboard with a monthly total. Some creators do it for fun and to share what they have learned. Some earn money from views. Some use it to sell courses, mentoring or tools.

We have tested the arithmetic behind two of the most common claims before: see [car boot haul videos versus real margins](/blog/car-boot-haul-videos-versus-real-margins) and [Amazon course claims tested](/blog/amazon-course-claims-tested). This debate is about the wider question: taken as a whole, is this content good for resellers?

## The case for: it teaches, motivates and shows what sells

Resellers who value haul and income content make these points.

**It shows what actually sells.** Supporters say you can learn more about which brands, eras and categories are worth picking up from an hour of haul videos than from most written guides. Seeing someone handle an item, check a label and explain why they bought it is practical teaching.

**It is free education.** Most of this content costs nothing to watch. For someone who cannot afford a course, or does not want one, videos from people doing the job are a way to learn without paying.

**It motivates.** Reselling can be lonely and slow. Supporters say seeing someone else's good week, or a steady monthly total, keeps people going through the bad weeks, and shows that small, regular sales add up.

**Honest creators do show the costs.** Plenty of creators show their fees, their unsold stock and their bad months. Supporters argue it is unfair to judge the whole format by its worst examples.

**Viewers are adults.** Most people, supporters say, understand that a video shows the highlights. It is up to the viewer to do their own sums.

## The case against: it shows the winners and hides the costs

Critics, including many experienced resellers, argue the opposite.

**Survivorship.** The people posting good months are, by definition, the ones having good months. Nobody makes a video about quitting after losing money on stock. Critics say this gives new sellers a picture built entirely from the successful few.

**The costs are missing.** A haul video shows the price paid and the hoped-for sale price. It rarely shows platform fees, postage, packaging, travel, returns, unsold stock or hours worked. A dashboard screenshot shows sales, not profit. Our [car boot worked example](/blog/car-boot-haul-videos-versus-real-margins) shows how large that gap can be.

**The content is often a funnel.** Critics say that some income posts exist mainly to sell a course, a mentoring programme, a sourcing list or a tool, and that the "look what I made" message is the advert. When the real income comes from selling the course, the reselling results may not be the point.

**Paid links are not always labelled.** Affiliate links to tools and apps, free products from brands and paid partnerships are common in this space. Critics say viewers often cannot tell when a recommendation is paid.

**It pushes people to spend before they are ready.** A viewer who believes the numbers may buy stock in bulk, sign up for tools, or pay for a course before they know whether reselling works for them.

## What the rules and the law say

### When a video is an advert

Not every haul video is an ad. The ASA's guidance on [recognising ads on social media](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html) says content needs to be obviously identifiable as advertising when:

- a brand gives the creator a payment or any other incentive, requested or unsolicited, and the content features or refers to that brand;
- the content refers to a product for which there is also an affiliate link or code, because the creator earns commission;
- the creator is promoting their own brand or products, such as their own course, which falls within the CAP Code as marketing.

The CAP Code's [rule 2.1](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/02.html) says marketing communications "must be obviously identifiable as such", and rule 2.3 says they must make their commercial intent clear if it is not obvious from the context.

### How it must be labelled

The ASA and the Competition and Markets Authority publish an [influencers' guide to making clear that ads are ads](https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/influencers-guide.html); the third edition was published in March 2023. The ASA's [social media guidance](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html) lists labels that work, such as "Ad", "Advert" and "Advertisement feature", and labels that are unlikely to be enough on their own, including "Sponsored", "Gifted", "Supported by", "aff", "sp", "spon", a discount code alone, or simply tagging the brand.

### Earnings claims

Where a video or post promotes a course, coaching or scheme sold on the promise that it will help you make money, the business opportunity rules apply.

- [CAP Code rule 20.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html) says such marketing "must neither contain unrepresentative or overstated earnings figures nor exaggerate the support available to investors".
- Rule 20.8 requires a clear description of the work involved and a statement of the investor's commitments, including any money they must put in.
- The ASA's [advice on earnings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recruitment-and-business-opportunities-earnings-claims.html) says marketers should state the level likely to be reached by an average respondent, not one based on a minority of high performers, and should avoid averages skewed by high earners.
- In its April 2026 article, [Money making courses: stick to the facts](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/money-making-courses-stick-to-the-facts.html), the ASA said disclaimers were judged insufficient when they contradicted the headline claim rather than clarifying it.

Our post on [what the ASA says about earnings claims](/blog/what-the-asa-says-about-earnings-claims) covers the 2025 rulings and how to complain.

### What the rules do not cover

A creator sharing their own hauls and results, with no payment, no free products, no affiliate links and nothing of their own to sell, is generally not advertising. The rules above do not make that content wrong or require it to show costs. Whether it is fair to viewers is the heart of this debate.

## Questions to think about

- Did a haul or income video help you start, and did your results match what you expected?
- Should creators who show sales totals also show fees, stock costs and unsold items, even when no rule requires it?
- Can you usually tell when a video recommendation is paid or affiliate-linked?
- Is a creator who later sells a course responsible for how their earlier free videos were read?
- What would a genuinely useful, honest haul video look like?
