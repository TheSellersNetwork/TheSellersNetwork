This is for UK Amazon sellers who have heard they "need insurance" and want to know when that is true, what kind, and why. By the end you will know the exact trigger in Amazon's agreement, the criteria Amazon sets for an acceptable policy, what product liability and public liability insurance are for, how they relate to UK product safety law, and what to ask before you buy a policy. We do not recommend any insurer or broker. This is general information, not legal, insurance or financial advice; an insurance broker or adviser can look at your own situation.

## The short version

- Amazon's agreement says that if your gross proceeds exceed the **Insurance Threshold in any month**, you must have liability insurance in place **within 30 days** and keep it for as long as you sell. For the UK store the threshold is **£4,000** ([Amazon Services Europe Business Solutions Agreement](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201190440?locale=en-GB)).
- The required cover is at least **£400,000** per occurrence and in aggregate, including products liability and bodily injury. Amazon can also ask any seller to get cover, whatever their sales.
- Amazon's [insurance policy page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G200386300?locale=en-GB) adds criteria: occurrence based in most cases, an excess of no more than £10,000, all your Amazon products covered, your legal entity name on the policy, and an insurer with a minimum financial rating.
- Since 1 January 2025 you no longer need to name Amazon as an additional insured ([Amazon announcement](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/seller-forums/discussions/t/330aebc0-1962-457b-a33b-553eed2b4a08)).
- Insurance is not a substitute for selling safe products. UK product safety law puts duties on distributors and producers, and UK importers can be treated as producers.
- As a reseller of branded goods, being able to name the manufacturer, brand owner or importer matters, both under UK law and in Amazon's own claims process.

## What Amazon's agreement actually says

Section 8 of the [Amazon Services Europe Business Solutions Agreement](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201190440?locale=en-GB), which governs UK selling accounts (last updated 30 June 2025 when we read it), says:

> If the gross proceeds from Your Transactions exceed the applicable Insurance Threshold during any month, or otherwise if requested by us, then within thirty (30) days thereafter, you will maintain at your expense throughout the remainder of the Term, public third party liability insurance ... including products liability and bodily injury.

The definitions in the same agreement set the UK figures:

| Term | UK Amazon site |
| --- | --- |
| Insurance Threshold | Four Thousand British Pounds (£4,000) |
| Insurance Limits | Four Hundred Thousand British Pounds (£400,000), per occurrence and in aggregate |

Three points people miss:

- **It is gross proceeds, not profit.** The agreement defines Sales Proceeds as the gross amount buyers pay, including the price, postage and other charges, and taxes.
- **One month is enough.** It is "during any month", not an average. A strong December can trigger it.
- **Amazon can ask regardless.** "Or otherwise if requested by us" means a seller under £4,000 a month can still be required to get cover, and you must provide certificates of insurance when Amazon asks.

### A worked example

Say your amazon.co.uk orders in November come to £4,300, including VAT and postage charged to buyers. That month's gross proceeds exceed £4,000, so under section 8 you have 30 days to have a qualifying policy in place, and you must keep it for as long as you remain on the agreement, even if December sales fall. If November came to £3,800, the threshold was not crossed that month, unless Amazon asks you directly.

## Amazon's criteria for an acceptable policy

Amazon's [Commercial Liability Insurance Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G200386300?locale=en-GB) sets out what the policy must look like, unless the law requires otherwise:

- **Type:** commercial general liability, or public and product liability.
- **Occurrence based,** except for certain product categories listed in Amazon's FAQ.
- **Limits:** at least the Insurance Limits above, covering products, products/completed operations and bodily injury.
- **Excess:** no more than GBP/EUR 10,000, and it must be shown on the certificate.
- **Scope:** all products you list on Amazon. It is your job to tell the insurer about your whole product range and any changes.
- **Name:** the insured name must match the legal entity name in your Account Info.
- **Insurer:** global claim handling capability and a financial rating of S&P A- and/or AM Best A- or better (or a local equivalent).
- **Notice:** you must tell Amazon immediately if the policy is cancelled, changed or not renewed.

Amazon says it will consider exceptions case by case if you cannot get a policy that meets these rules.

The [FAQ](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GT89995FFL4KH22J?locale=en-GB) adds more detail. Claims made policies are only accepted for certain higher risk products, such as supplements, ingestibles, chemicals and critical car parts, and then need cover for at least three years after you stop selling them. If you sell in several Amazon stores, one policy should cover all of them. The FAQ names brokers who may help; you are free to use any insurer that meets the criteria, and we do not suggest any.

The additional insured requirement is gone. Amazon's announcement said it would "remove the requirement that insurance policies name Amazon and its assignees as additional insureds" from 1 January 2025, and that the duty to tell Amazon about cancellation or non-renewal now rests with you, not the insurer.

## What the cover is for

Put simply, and subject to the wording of any policy you buy:

- **Product liability** cover responds when a product you sold is said to have caused injury to someone or damage to their property.
- **Public liability** cover responds when your business activities, rather than a product, are said to have caused injury or damage to a member of the public, for example a visitor at your premises or someone hurt while you are out collecting stock.

Amazon's requirement is about third party claims, including products liability and bodily injury. It does not cover your own stock, your own premises, or lost income. Those are separate policies.

## How it relates to product safety and liability law

Insurance pays for claims. The law is about who is responsible in the first place.

**Product safety.** In Great Britain the [General Product Safety Regulations 2005](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/general-product-safety-regulations-2005/general-product-safety-regulations-2005-great-britain) require consumer products to be safe. A reseller whose activity does not affect a product's safety is a "distributor" and must act with due care, not supply products it knows or should presume are dangerous, keep records that trace where products came from, and cooperate with enforcement authorities ([regulation 8](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1803/regulation/8)). The definition of "producer" in [regulation 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1803/regulation/2) includes the person in the UK who places a product from outside the UK on the market, where the manufacturer is not established here. If you import stock yourself, you may carry producer duties. Sales into Northern Ireland and the EU fall under the EU rules instead; see our note on [GPSR for Northern Ireland and the EU](/blog/gpsr-product-safety-ni-eu).

**Product liability.** Under [section 2 of the Consumer Protection Act 1987](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1987/43/section/2), where damage is caused by a defective product, the producer, anyone presenting themselves as the producer by putting their name or brand on it, and anyone who imported it into the UK to supply it in the course of business can be liable. A supplier can also become liable if asked to identify the producer or its own supplier and fails to do so within a reasonable time.

That last point lines up with Amazon's own process. Its FAQ says that when a claim comes in, you must provide your certificate of insurance unless you can give the details of the importer, brand owner or manufacturer of the defective product, and Amazon will then work with that producer. For a reseller of branded goods, good purchase records are part of your protection. Our guide to [product safety and listing accuracy](/guides/amazon-product-safety-and-listing-accuracy) covers what to check before you buy stock.

## Questions to ask an insurer or broker

Take this list with you. Write down the answers.

1. Does the policy include both public liability and products liability, and is products liability on an occurrence basis?
2. Is the limit at least £400,000 per occurrence and in aggregate? Is there a separate aggregate for products?
3. What is the excess, and is it shown on the certificate? Amazon's maximum is £10,000.
4. Does it cover online sales through Amazon and every Amazon store I sell in, including any EU stores?
5. Does it cover goods I did not make, bought from UK shops and wholesalers? Does it cover goods I import myself?
6. Are any of my product types excluded? Ask specifically about anything with a battery, children's products, cosmetics, food and supplements, electricals and second hand goods.
7. Does it cover claims brought in other countries, including the US, if I sell or ship there?
8. Is the insured name exactly my legal entity name as it appears in Seller Central?
9. What is the insurer's financial rating, and does it meet Amazon's S&P or AM Best minimum?
10. Is there a sunset clause, and what happens to cover for past sales if I stop selling?
11. What do I need to tell you when I add new product types, and how quickly?
12. Can you issue a certificate of insurance in PDF that shows the limits, the excess and the period?

## What goes wrong

- **Waiting for Amazon to ask.** The duty starts when you cross the threshold in a month, not when Amazon writes to you.
- **Buying cover that excludes what you sell.** A cheap policy that excludes lithium batteries is no help to a seller of electricals.
- **Name mismatch.** A policy in your personal name when your account is a limited company, or the other way round.
- **Letting it lapse.** Amazon's policy puts the duty on you to tell it about cancellation or non-renewal.
- **Thinking insurance makes an unsafe product acceptable.** It does not. Safety law applies either way.
