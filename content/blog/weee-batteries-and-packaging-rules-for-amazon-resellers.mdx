This is for small resellers on Amazon, eBay or both who sell electricals, batteries or anything in packaging, which is nearly everyone. By the end you will know the difference between being a distributor and being a producer under the UK waste rules, what a distributor has to do about take-back, when a reseller crosses into producer territory, the packaging thresholds as GOV.UK publishes them, and what the marketplaces take on for you and what they leave with you. We only state thresholds we could check on GOV.UK or legislation.gov.uk. The rules are detailed and change; this is general information, not legal advice, and the GOV.UK pages linked below are the ones to rely on.

## The short version

- If you sell new electrical or electronic equipment (EEE) to UK households, you are a **distributor** and must offer free like-for-like take-back of the customer's old item. Sellers who only sell online, or sell less than £100,000 of EEE a year, can join the **Distributor Take Back Scheme** instead ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/electricalwaste-producer-supplier-responsibilities)).
- You must also publish take-back and recycling information on your website or listings.
- If you sell or supply **32kg or more of portable batteries a year**, you must offer a battery collection point, including if you sell online ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/battery-waste-supplier-reponsibilities)).
- You become a **producer** if, for example, you import goods commercially or sell them under your own brand. Producers have to register.
- Under packaging EPR you are only an **obligated producer** if you supplied or imported more than 25 tonnes of packaging and had a turnover of £1 million or more ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-must-comply-with-epr-for-packaging--2)). Most small resellers are below that.
- Marketplaces take on some producer costs for **non-UK** sellers. If you are established in the UK, your obligations stay with you.

## Distributor or producer

Most resellers are **distributors**: you make products available to customers. A smaller number are **producers**: roughly, the first business to put the product on the UK market, or the brand on it. The two roles carry different duties, and the sections below cover each.

## Electricals: the WEEE rules

### Take-back as a distributor

GOV.UK's [retailer and distributor guide](https://www.gov.uk/electricalwaste-producer-supplier-responsibilities) says that if you sell EEE you must give customers a way to dispose of their old household item when you sell them a new version of the same item. That applies online too. The take-back has to be free, and covers items of an equivalent type that did the same job, whatever the brand; GOV.UK's example is taking back an old kettle when a customer buys a new one.

You must do one of these:

1. **Offer free take-back in store**, giving customers at least 28 days to bring the old item back.
2. **Set up an alternative free take-back service**, such as a designated collection facility, on your own or with other distributors.
3. **Join the Distributor Take Back Scheme (DTS)**, which GOV.UK says you can do instead if your business sells less than £100,000 of EEE a year, or you only sell online. GOV.UK says the current fee covers your obligations until 31 December 2026 and depends on your size, whether you only sell online and how much EEE you sell.

For almost every online-only reseller, option 3 is the practical one. GOV.UK notes an exception: vape sellers must take back waste vapes in store or through an alternative collection point.

### Information you must give

Whatever option you choose, you must give customers free written information on which take-back service you provide, how they can reuse and recycle electricals, why this waste must be kept separate, the damage caused by not recycling, and the meaning of the crossed-out wheeled bin symbol. GOV.UK says online retailers must publish this on their website, and DTS members can get the material from the scheme. On a marketplace, put it where your buyers will see it, such as your seller profile or policies page. Keep a record of what you told customers.

### Used and refurbished electricals

The wording of the regulations matters here. Regulation 42 of the [WEEE Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3113/regulation/42) puts the take-back duty on a distributor "who supplies new EEE", and the information duty in [regulation 44](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3113/regulation/44) is worded the same way. If you only sell second-hand electricals, those two duties are worded for new equipment. If you sell a mix, which is common for arbitrage sellers, the new stock brings you in. If you are unsure, ask the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), which enforces these rules.

### When you become an EEE producer

GOV.UK's [producer guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/electrical-and-electronic-equipment-eee-producer-responsibility) says you are an EEE producer if you:

- make and sell EEE under your own brand in the UK;
- resell equipment made by someone else under your own brand (if the maker's brand is on it, the maker is the producer);
- import EEE on a commercial basis into the UK;
- are based outside the UK and sell EEE directly to UK customers at a distance;
- run an online marketplace supplying EEE to UK households from non-UK suppliers.

Reselling a branded item bought in the UK does not make you the producer. Importing stock yourself can.

Producers must register every year. If you place less than 5 tonnes of EEE on the UK market in a year, you can register directly with your environmental regulator as a small producer, by 31 January or within 28 days of first placing EEE on the market. Above 5 tonnes, you must join a producer compliance scheme. Producers must also mark products with the crossed-out wheeled bin symbol and a date mark, and keep records for at least four years.

## Batteries

### Take-back as a distributor

GOV.UK's [battery retailer guide](https://www.gov.uk/battery-waste-supplier-reponsibilities) says you must offer free take-back of waste portable batteries if you sell or supply **32kg or more** of portable batteries a year. GOV.UK says one pack of four AA batteries a day is about 32kg a year.

If you are over that line, you must have a collection point at all premises you supply from, and this applies to online and mail order sellers too. You must take back any sealed battery an average person can carry, not just the types you sell, and let people know you collect them, for example on your website. GOV.UK says a battery compliance scheme will collect the batteries free of charge.

Below 32kg a year, you do not have to take back batteries. The GOV.UK page talks about the batteries you sell or supply; it does not say how batteries built into products count towards the 32kg. If most of your stock has batteries inside, ask OPSS rather than guessing.

### When you become a battery producer

GOV.UK's [battery producer guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/waste-batteries-producer-responsibility) says you are a producer if you have a UK business presence and are the first in your selling chain, including as an importer, to make batteries available in the UK. That includes batteries inside products you import. For portable batteries:

- **More than 1 tonne a year:** join a battery compliance scheme by 15 October before the compliance year.
- **1 tonne or less:** register with your regulator on the National Packaging Waste Database, report tonnage and chemistry by 31 January each year, and pay an annual registration charge.

If you import electricals with batteries, you report the equipment under the WEEE rules and the batteries under the battery rules, separately.

## Packaging: extended producer responsibility

GOV.UK's [check if you must comply](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-must-comply-with-epr-for-packaging--2) page lists the packaging activities that make an organisation a producer, including supplying filled packaging under your own brand, packing goods or filling packaging, importing goods in packaging, and **selling filled packaging to an end user**. That last one covers every reseller, but being a producer is not the same as having obligations.

You are an **obligated producer** only if you do one of those activities and all of these apply:

- you are established in the UK (including a business run from home);
- you supplied or imported **more than 25 tonnes** of packaging in the UK in the previous year;
- your annual worldwide turnover was **£1 million or more**.

GOV.UK's table:

| Packaging weight | £1 million or less turnover | £1 million to £2 million | More than £2 million |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Less than 25 tonnes | No obligation | No obligation | No obligation |
| 25 to 50 tonnes | No obligation | Small producer | Small producer |
| More than 50 tonnes | No obligation | Small producer | Large producer |

Small producers must register with their regulator, report packaging data every year and keep data for seven years. Large producers have more, including fees. For 2026 reporting, GOV.UK says to count packaging supplied or imported from 1 January to 31 December 2025, and to use turnover from the last accounts available before 7 April 2026. Count each piece of packaging only once, even if you do more than one activity with it.

**A worked example.** Say your turnover is £300,000. You are below the £1 million test, so GOV.UK's table shows no obligation whatever your packaging weight. At £1.2 million, you would add up a year's packaging (on the products, plus your mailers, boxes and fill); only above 25 tonnes would you be an obligated producer, in the small producer group.

## What the marketplaces take on

**For non-UK sellers.** Since 12 August 2025, the definition of an EEE producer includes an online marketplace supplying EEE to UK households from non-UK suppliers ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/electrical-and-electronic-equipment-eee-producer-responsibility)). Packaging EPR similarly lists owning an online marketplace where packaging is supplied into the UK from outside the UK. Amazon has told sellers it assumes the EPR obligations for packaging and WEEE on Amazon.co.uk sales by sellers not established in the UK, uses the registered business address in Seller Central to decide, and enrols those sellers in a Pay on Behalf service with an annual service fee of £20 per EPR category ([Amazon seller forums announcement](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/seller-forums/discussions/t/5024cd60-6842-4b43-927c-b4fdd026b209)).

**For UK sellers.** None of that moves your distributor duties, or any producer duties you have, onto the marketplace. Amazon's own customer help page on recycling electricals tells buyers who bought from a Marketplace seller to contact that seller about disposing of their old equipment ([Amazon help](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GZ8UKWT6JBYRDJJA)). So a buyer's question about take-back will come to you. eBay's [electronic equipment policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/prohibited-restricted-items/electrical-electronic-equipment-policy?id=4302) points sellers to the WEEE Regulations 2013 for more information.

**What Amazon asks for.** Amazon's [EPR compliance page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/extended-producer-responsibility-compliance) explains producer duties in general and says that non-compliant listings may be deactivated, or Amazon may comply on your behalf. The registration numbers it describes collecting are for European countries such as Germany and France. We could not find a public Amazon page setting out a UK registration number requirement for UK-established sellers, so check the compliance notices in your own Seller Central account. For battery-powered products, Amazon's own dangerous goods paperwork is covered in our [product safety and listing accuracy guide](/guides/amazon-product-safety-and-listing-accuracy).

## What goes wrong

- **Assuming the marketplace handles it.** For UK sellers, it does not.
- **Forgetting the information duty.** Take-back without the customer information still falls short.
- **Importing a small batch and not registering.** Importing EEE on a commercial basis, or being first in the UK chain for batteries, makes you a producer whatever the quantity; the tonnage decides how you register, not whether you must.
- **Guessing at thresholds from old articles.** Use the current GOV.UK page, which was last updated in August 2026, not a figure you remember.
