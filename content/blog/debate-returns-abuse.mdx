Sellers talk about clothes that come back worn, items swapped for cheaper ones, and "not as described" claims on listings that were accurate. Buyers talk about faults that were never mentioned, stains hidden by the photos, and sellers who stop replying. Both happen. The argument is about where the balance should sit, and whether platforms protect buyers at sellers' expense. This post sets out both sides, what eBay, Vinted and Amazon's own rules say, and the consumer law, and ends with a poll. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- eBay's rules ban returning a different item, using or damaging an item and then returning it, and claiming an accurately described item was not as described.
- eBay lets sellers deduct up to 50% from a refund when a return comes back used or damaged. Amazon has a claims route (SAFE-T) for seller-fulfilled orders; Vinted reviews evidence from both sides when a return is disputed.
- UK consumer law gives buyers from businesses a 14 day right to cancel and a 30 day right to reject faulty goods. Businesses can reduce a refund where handling went beyond what a shop would allow.
- Buyers from private sellers have fewer rights, but items must still be as described.
- Retail bodies say refund fraud is growing, but the published figures cover retail as a whole, not marketplace resellers.

## What is happening

### The size of the problem

There are no official figures for returns abuse on eBay, Vinted or Amazon's marketplace. The best published sources cover retail generally. In May 2026 the British Retail Consortium (BRC) reported on a Home Office summit on fraud against business, where discussion focused on "the rise of first-party fraud, refund abuse, chargeback fraud" ([BRC](https://brc.org.uk/news-and-events/news/finance/2026/ungated/brc-attends-the-home-office-fraud-against-business-summit/)). The BRC reported that Cifas, the fraud prevention service, warned of "the growing normalisation of customer fraud, with refund fraud and false non-delivery claims becoming increasingly common, particularly among younger demographics". It also reported that University of Portsmouth research presented at the summit estimated UK retail losses linked to refund fraud at a median of £2.3 billion, potentially more than £5 billion.

Those are retail-wide estimates. They tell you the problem is real, not how often it happens to a Vinted seller.

### What the platforms say

**eBay.** The [abusive buyer policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/rules-policies-buyers/abusive-buyer-policy?id=4374) lists "returning an item other than the original item received", "using or damaging an item and then returning it" and "returning an item with the reason that it was not as described when it was described accurately". Consequences can include warnings, loss of eBay Money Back Guarantee cover, blocks on returns and claims, and suspension. The [condition of returned items policy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/member-behavior-policies/condition-returned-items-policy?id=4763) says returns should come back in the condition received, with everything that was in the parcel, and that sellers can deduct up to 50% for loss in value when an item is returned damaged or used. eBay's [seller protections](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/seller-protections?id=4345) say it will remove related negative or neutral feedback when it confirms a buyer's "not as described" claim was false.

On the other side, the [eBay Money Back Guarantee](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/ebay-money-back-guarantee-policy/ebay-money-back-guarantee-policy?id=4210) covers items that do not arrive, are faulty or damaged, or do not match the listing. If a seller offers returns, a buyer can return for any reason, including a change of mind.

**Vinted.** Vinted's [refund policy](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/465-the-vinted-refund-policy) covers items that are lost, damaged or significantly not as described, and says items that match the description but do not fit or suit the buyer are "not eligible for a return or refund". Its [definition of significantly not as described](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/1090-significantly-not-as-described-items-at-vinted) includes wrong colour or size, undisclosed damage and functionality not as described, but not minor differences or wear shown in the photos. If a seller says the returned item is not the one sent, or is in a different condition, Vinted's [returns page](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/272-returning-an-item) says Vinted reviews evidence from both parties.

**Amazon.** For seller-fulfilled orders refunded by Amazon with a prepaid return label, [SAFE-T](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G202175000?locale=en-GB) lets sellers claim within 60 days where the customer misused the returns policy, returned an item damaged or used, or returned something materially different or incomplete. Reimbursements exclude VAT and fees, and may be limited to a percentage of the order value. For FBA, Amazon's [returns reimbursement policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G9N934L7Y4SFWPJ4?locale=en-GB) reimburses automatically if a customer returns a non-authentic item or a completely different product, but says Amazon "does not accept responsibility nor reimburse for returned items that are damaged by a customer".

Our comparison of [seller protection across platforms](/blog/seller-protection-compared) sets these side by side.

## The case for sellers: the system rewards the dishonest

Resellers argue that the rules on paper are not the rules in practice. In their words: the buyer's account of what happened is accepted by default, the refund goes out first, and the seller is left to prove a negative. When a phone comes back as a different model or a coat comes back worn, the seller is out of pocket on the item, the postage both ways and their time, and a successful claim, where there is one, may not cover everything. Amazon's own SAFE-T page says reimbursements exclude VAT and fees.

They say "not as described" has become a free returns route. On platforms where change of mind is not covered, a buyer who simply does not like an item can claim it is different from the listing. Sellers argue that without a video of packing, it is hard to show otherwise, and that they have had to turn their homes into evidence rooms to protect themselves.

They point to the scale in retail generally. If refund fraud is being "normalised", as the BRC reported Cifas warning, sellers with no fraud team and no margin to spare feel it most.

Finally, they argue that tougher enforcement would protect honest buyers too. Every abused return adds to the cost of selling, and those costs end up in prices.

## The case for buyers: most returns are genuine, and sellers get it wrong too

Buyers and consumer advocates argue that the protections exist because sellers are not always right either. In their words: items arrive with faults that were not mentioned, photos hide stains, sizes are wrong, and some sellers do not respond. For a buyer on a second-hand marketplace, the platform's guarantee is often the only realistic route to getting money back.

They say most people returning things are not fraudsters. The retail-wide estimates cover every kind of refund fraud, and there is no published evidence of how common abuse is among buyers from resellers specifically. Treating every return with suspicion punishes the majority.

They also point out that sellers have remedies. eBay lets sellers deduct up to 50% for used or damaged returns and bans abusive buyers; Amazon has SAFE-T; Vinted reviews evidence from both sides. Those tools work best for sellers who describe items fully and keep good records.

Finally, they argue that the law exists for a reason. The right to cancel online purchases reflects the fact that buyers cannot see or handle goods before paying. Weakening that to deal with a minority of abusers would take away a protection everyone relies on.

## What the rules and the law say

**Buying from a business.** Under the [Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/contents), consumers can cancel most online purchases within 14 days of receiving the goods and must be refunded within 14 days of the business receiving the goods back ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds)). But [regulation 34](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/regulation/34) lets a business recover the amount by which goods have lost value because of handling "beyond what is necessary to establish the nature, characteristics and functioning of the goods", which it describes as going beyond "the sort of handling that might reasonably be allowed in a shop". That deduction is not available if the business failed to tell the consumer about the right to cancel. Separately, the [Consumer Rights Act 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/22) gives a 30 day short-term right to reject goods that are faulty or not as described.

**Buying from a private seller.** Citizens Advice describes the position as "buyer beware": a private seller cannot misdescribe an item but can leave information out ([Citizens Advice, 2018](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/about-us/media-centre/press-releases/citizens-advice-warns-buyers-need-to-beware-as-thousands-of-people-report-being-ripped-off-on-online-marketplaces/)). There is no legal right to cancel, though a platform's own policy may offer more. Our earlier debate on [private sellers who are really businesses](/blog/debate-private-sellers-who-are-businesses) looks at why that line matters.

**Fraud.** Deliberately sending back a different item to get a refund is dishonest and can be a crime. Victims can report fraud to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/), which replaced Action Fraud.

**Evidence.** Whichever side of the debate you are on, good records decide most disputes. Our guides to [switched or damaged eBay returns](/blog/ebay-returns-switched-or-damaged), [sealed parcel videos and evidence](/blog/sealed-parcel-videos-and-evidence) and [Amazon damaged returns and SAFE-T](/guides/amazon-damaged-returns-and-safe-t) explain what to keep.

## Questions to think about

- Should platforms hold the refund until a returned item has been checked, or refund first and sort it out later?
- Is a 50% deduction for used returns on eBay too much, too little, or about right?
- Should buyers with a pattern of returns be shown to sellers before they buy?
- How much of the problem would clearer, more detailed listings solve?
- Have you ever been on the buying side of a dispute you thought the seller got wrong?
