This is for anyone watching adverts for Amazon FBA courses, mentoring or "done-for-you" services and trying to work out which parts are true. It takes seven claims that appear in these adverts over and over, says what each one implies, and tests it against what Amazon itself publishes and what the UK advertising rules say. It does not name any course or creator: the point is to test the claim, wherever you see it. Some paid training is honest and useful. This is general information, not legal or financial advice.

## The short version

- **"Amazon does all the work."** Amazon's own FBA page lists the steps you still do: buying, listing, prepping, labelling and shipping stock in. It is less work than posting orders yourself. It is not passive.
- **"£X an hour in a supermarket."** An hourly figure depends on how many profitable items you find per hour, which nobody can promise. Work out your own with real fees and real minutes.
- **"Start with £150."** The Professional plan alone is £30 a month with VAT. What is left buys very little stock, and the money is held until after delivery.
- **"25 to 40% ROI every cycle."** Fees differ by product and change during the year. Returns, storage and price drops are not in a headline percentage.
- **"£5,000 a month by month 12."** UK advertising rules say earnings figures must be representative of what an average person gets, not the best.
- **"Guaranteed ungating" and "guaranteed reinstatement."** Only Amazon decides these. Nobody outside Amazon can guarantee Amazon's decision.

## The rules these adverts have to follow

UK adverts are covered by the CAP Code, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA). Four rules matter here:

- **Rule 20.7:** ads for business opportunities "must neither contain unrepresentative or overstated earnings figures nor exaggerate the support available to investors" ([CAP Code section 20](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html)).
- **Rule 20.8:** before you commit, the marketer must give you "a clear description of the work involved" and "a statement of the extent of investors' commitments, including any financial investment or outlay".
- **Rule 3.7:** marketers must hold documentary evidence for objective claims before the ad runs ([CAP Code section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html)).
- **Rule 3.11:** ads "must not mislead consumers by exaggerating the capability or performance of a product".

The ASA's [advice on earnings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recruitment-and-business-opportunities-earnings-claims.html) says a figure should "state the level likely to be attained by an average respondent, not a level based on a minority of unrepresentative high performers". In April 2026 it summarised its rulings on money-making courses: advertisers were "unable to demonstrate that the highlighted results reflected what most consumers were likely to achieve" ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/money-making-courses-stick-to-the-facts.html)). Our post on [what the ASA says about earnings claims](/blog/what-the-asa-says-about-earnings-claims) covers the rulings in full.

## Claim 1: "Amazon does all the work, it's passive income"

**What it implies:** you buy stock, Amazon does everything else, and money arrives.

**What Amazon says:** Amazon describes FBA as a service where it takes care of "storing, packing and shipping your products, handling customer support and returns" ([Amazon FBA](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/fulfilment-by-amazon)). The same page lists what you do: create or convert listings, prepare stock following Amazon's packaging requirements, "pack and ship your products in bulk" to a fulfilment centre, and manage inventory in Seller Central. Before any of that, you find and buy the stock, which is most of the work and all of the risk.

Then there is the account. Prices move, other sellers join your listings, stock ages and starts attracting surcharges, and returns come back needing a decision. Our guide to [where the money leaks on Amazon](/guides/amazon-where-the-money-leaks) lists the checks that need doing every month.

**The pattern to watch:** in the US, the Federal Trade Commission said one scheme promised "passive investment income" from online stores, and that "Amazon and Walmart routinely suspended, blocked, or terminated the stores" it ran for clients ([FTC, February 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/02/ftc-action-leads-ban-owners-automators-ai-e-commerce-money-making-scheme)). That is a US case under US law, but the promise is the same one you will see in UK adverts.

## Claim 2: "£X an hour sourcing in a supermarket"

**What it implies:** walk into a shop, scan products, and earn a set hourly rate.

**What the numbers show:** an hourly figure is (profit per item x items found per hour) minus your costs. The profit per item comes after Amazon's fees, which are not small. In our [worked example of every Amazon fee on one sale](/blog/every-amazon-fee-on-one-sale), a £24.99 toy sold through FBA pays about £8.54 in fees in September, leaving £16.45. Say you paid £10 for it in a shop: that is £6.45 before prep, labels, shipping it to Amazon and your petrol.

Now the hard part: how many of those do you find in an hour? It depends on the shop, the day, what other sellers have already bought, and whether the brand is one you are allowed to sell. An advert that gives one hourly figure has picked a number for that, and the ASA's advice says it should be what an average person achieves, not a good day.

**Test it yourself:** our [worth my time calculator](/tools/worth-it) adds up the minutes an item really takes, from the drive to the drop-off, and shows your real hourly rate.

## Claim 3: "Start with £150"

**What it implies:** a tiny sum is enough to build a business.

**What Amazon's pricing shows:** the Professional plan is £25 a month excluding VAT ([Amazon pricing](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing)), and Amazon charges VAT on its fees to UK sellers, so £30 a month if you are not VAT registered. From £150 that leaves £120 for stock, labels, supplies and shipping to Amazon, before any tool.

Then the money is held. Amazon's default reserve holds sale proceeds until seven days after delivery, and can hold them longer ([Amazon on reserves](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/seller-forums/discussions/t/aac4b8e9-c776-418e-8373-9484fbf8baa1)). So your £120 of stock has to be bought, prepped, shipped in, received, sold and delivered, and then wait another week, before it can buy the next lot. Meanwhile the next month's plan fee is due.

It is possible to start small. It is not the same thing as starting a business that pays you. Our [first-month budget for £1,000](/blog/what-1000-pounds-buys-on-amazon-fba) shows the lines in full.

## Claim 4: "25 to 40% ROI every cycle"

**What it implies:** a steady, predictable return you can reinvest over and over.

**What the fee schedule shows:** every product pays a different mix of fees. The referral fee runs from 5% to 45% by category and price band, though Amazon says most are between 8% and 15%; the fulfilment fee depends on size tier and weight, and both change on set dates. On Amazon's [July 2026 rate card](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/02/sell/images/260630-FBA-Rate-Card-EN1.pdf), UK fulfilment fees rise for peak season from 15 October, storage roughly doubles from October to December, stock older than 240 days pays an aged inventory surcharge, and some returns pay a returns processing fee. None of that fits in one percentage.

**The arithmetic:** if you really could make 30% on your money every month and reinvest all of it, £1,000 would grow to about £23,300 in a year. That is what "every cycle" implies. Real stock does not sell on a timetable: some sells in a week, some in three months, some has to be sold at a loss.

A percentage return on one good purchase can be real. A promise that every purchase will return it is a claim about the future that nobody can evidence, and rule 3.7 says the evidence has to exist before the ad runs.

## Claim 5: "£5,000 a month by month 12"

**What it implies:** a ladder of income targets, month by month, that anyone following the method will climb.

**What the rules say:** this is exactly what rule 20.7 and the ASA's advice on earnings claims are about. The figure must be what an average person achieves, and it must be backed by evidence. A screenshot of one student's month is not that. The ASA has also ruled that a small "results not typical" line does not rescue a headline that says the opposite.

**What to ask:** "What did the median buyer of this course earn, as profit after stock and fees, in their first twelve months, and how many buyers does that figure cover?" If the answer is another screenshot, you have your answer. It is also worth asking whether "£5,000" means sales or profit, because on Amazon those are very different numbers.

## Claim 6: "Guaranteed ungating"

**What it implies:** pay us and you will be approved to sell a restricted brand or category.

**What is true:** approval to sell a gated brand or category is Amazon's decision, made on Amazon's criteria. A third party can help you prepare an application. It cannot guarantee Amazon's answer. Our guide to [ungating in the UK](/guides/amazon-ungating-uk) explains how applications work and why the invoice matters, and it is blunt about the risk: the application is not guaranteed.

**What the rules say:** CAP rule 3.55 says ads "must not use the word 'guarantee' in a way that could cause confusion about a consumer's rights", rule 3.56 says ads must not mislead by leaving out significant limits on a guarantee, and rule 3.57 says a valid claim under a money-back guarantee must be refunded promptly ([CAP Code section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html)). If a guarantee is offered, read what it actually promises. "Guaranteed or your money back" is a refund promise, not a promise that Amazon will approve you.

## Claim 7: "Guaranteed reinstatement"

**What it implies:** if Amazon suspends your account, pay us and it will come back.

**What is true:** reinstatement is Amazon's decision after reviewing your appeal. Amazon's own staff describe even routine reserves as something that "may be extended following an assessment of your overall risk and historical performance", which gives a sense of how much sits with Amazon. A good appeal addresses the specific reason Amazon gave, with evidence. Our guide to [Amazon account health](/guides/amazon-account-health) covers what the notices mean and how to respond.

A person offering a guarantee here is promising something they do not control. At best it is a refund offer. At worst you pay for an appeal that makes things worse, at a moment when you can least afford it.

## How to use this

When you see one of these claims, run three questions:

1. **What exactly is being promised?** Sales or profit? Every month or one month? Guaranteed by whom?
2. **Where is the evidence?** Typical results, for most buyers, over a stated period.
3. **What does Amazon's own page say?** The fee schedule and the FBA page are public. Check them before you check the advert.

For a wider checklist, including countdown timers, prices only given on a call and finance pressure, read [how to spot a reselling course scam](/blog/how-to-spot-a-reselling-course-scam). If an advert crosses the line, you can complain to the [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html). If you have lost money to fraud, report it to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/).
