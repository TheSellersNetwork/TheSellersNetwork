This is for resellers who see ads for courses, mentoring and "systems" that promise big monthly incomes, and want to know what UK advertisers are actually allowed to say. It covers the Advertising Standards Authority's (ASA) 2025 rulings on money-making courses, the guidance it published in April 2026, the CAP Code rules behind them, and how to complain. If you sell your own knowledge, it also tells you what honest advertising looks like. This is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- In October 2025 the ASA ruled against several course advertisers as part of a project on ads from "online personalities and companies promoting courses that purport to help people make money and expand their business".
- The problems it found: income claims implied to be typical, disclaimers that contradicted the headline, "free" offers that were not free, and false deadlines.
- In April 2026 the ASA summarised the lessons in [Money making courses: stick to the facts](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/money-making-courses-stick-to-the-facts.html).
- The CAP Code has specific rules for business opportunities. Earnings figures must be representative and the ad must say what you will have to commit, including money.
- Anyone can complain to the ASA online. It does not get you a refund, but it can stop an ad running.

## Who the ASA is and what it covers

The ASA is the UK's independent advertising regulator. It applies the UK Code of Non-broadcast Advertising and Direct & Promotional Marketing, known as the [CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/codes-and-rulings/advertising-codes/non-broadcast-code.html), to ads in most media, including paid social media ads, influencer posts, websites and emails.

The ASA deals with what an ad says. It does not deal with whether a course was any good, whether you are owed a refund, or payment disputes; the ASA's [complaints FAQ](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint/complaints-faq.html) sends those to Citizens Advice. For your money, see [refund rights for online courses](/blog/refund-rights-for-online-courses).

## The 2025 rulings

The ASA published the rulings on 15 October 2025. Each advertiser was told the ad must not appear again in the form investigated. The table uses the rule numbers from each ruling.

| Ruling | What the ad said, in short | What the ASA found | CAP Code rules breached |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Self Made Girl Boss Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/self-made-girl-boss-ltd-a25-1293166-self-made-girl-boss-ltd.html) | Paid Instagram ad: a course that led to £20,000 to £30,000 a month working four hours a day | Implied the results were typical; the "results aren't typical" line contradicted the claim; implied a £390 course was free | 3.1, 3.9, 3.11 |
| [Charlie Johnson](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/charlie-johnson-a25-1293121-charlie-johnson.html) | Paid Facebook and Instagram ads for a coaching course with claims of £33k to £100k a month | Implied results were typical regardless of experience; a "FREE for 48 hours" offer stayed available after 48 hours; referred to CAP's Compliance team | 1.7, 1.7.1, 3.1, 3.11, 3.30 |
| [Jessica Crane Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/jessica-crane-ltd-a25-1293031-jessica-crane-ltd.html) | Ads for a coaching system for salon owners, referring to what "the top 1%" of owners know | Implied lifestyle and earnings were typical; implied a free video alone would deliver the results; no evidence that top performers used the system | 3.1, 3.7, 3.11 |

The April 2026 article also cites a ruling on [Robbins Research International Inc](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/robbins-research-international-inc-a25-1293005-robbins-research-international-inc.html), whose ad claimed businesses could grow "overnight 30 to 130 percent". The ASA found it implied the results were typical and breached rules 3.1 and 3.11.

None of these rulings concerned a reselling course specifically. They matter because they set out how the ASA treats income claims in any course ad, and reselling courses make exactly the same kinds of claims.

## The April 2026 guidance

The ASA's [April 2026 article](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/money-making-courses-stick-to-the-facts.html) groups the problems into four themes:

1. **Inflated income claims.** Advertisers could not show that the highlighted results reflected what most consumers were likely to achieve.
2. **Testimonials and transformations.** Success stories gave an unrealistic impression, and disclaimers were judged insufficient because they contradicted rather than clarified the headline.
3. **"Free" offers.** The line between free and paid content was often unclear.
4. **Urgency.** Some time-limited promotions were not really time-limited and put undue pressure on people to sign up.

Its advice to advertisers is to avoid exaggeration, not to imply that exceptional success is typical, and to hold robust evidence for claims that could influence someone's decision.

## The CAP Code rules that apply

### General rules on misleading ads

These apply to every ad, and they are the ones cited in the rulings. The wording is from [section 3 of the CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html).

| Rule | What it says, in short |
| --- | --- |
| 3.1 | Ads must not materially mislead or be likely to |
| 3.7 | Advertisers must hold documentary evidence for objective claims before the ad runs |
| 3.9 | Qualifications may clarify but must not contradict the claim they qualify |
| 3.11 | Ads must not exaggerate what a product can do |
| 3.30 | Ads must not falsely say a product or its terms are available only for a limited time |
| 3.45 | Ads must make clear where consumer reviews have been incentivised |
| 3.47 | Advertisers must hold evidence that a testimonial is genuine, and contact details for the person who gave it |

### Rules on business opportunities

[Section 20 of the CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/20.html) covers employment, homework schemes and business opportunities. A course sold on the promise that it will start you in a money-making business is likely to be treated as a business opportunity, and two rules matter most:

- **Rule 20.7:** ads for business opportunities "must neither contain unrepresentative or overstated earnings figures nor exaggerate the support available to investors".
- **Rule 20.8:** the ad or the follow-up literature must give the advertiser's full name and geographical address, "a clear description of the work involved", and "a statement of the extent of investors' commitments, including any financial investment or outlay".

The ASA's [advice on earnings claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recruitment-and-business-opportunities-earnings-claims.html) adds that figures should reflect what an average respondent earns, not a few high performers, and that a mean average skewed by top earners should not be used unless the basis is made clear.

### Affiliates and influencers

A creator who earns commission on a course must make that obvious. The [ASA's affiliate marketing advice](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/affiliate-marketing.html) says affiliate content must be obviously identifiable as advertising, usually with "Ad" at the start, and that a vague line saying the author "may" earn commission is unlikely to be enough.

### Consumer law sits alongside the Code

Some of the same conduct is also illegal under consumer law. Since 6 April 2025, the [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20) bans outright, among other things, false limited-time claims (paragraph 7 of Schedule 20) and describing something as free when the consumer has to pay for more than the unavoidable cost of responding and delivery (paragraph 23). Those are enforced by Trading Standards and the Competition and Markets Authority, not the ASA.

## What honest advertising looks like

If you sell a course, coaching or a guide yourself, this is the practical reading of the rules and the rulings. It is also a useful checklist when you are the one being sold to.

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "My students make £10k a month" | What a typical buyer earned over a stated period, how you know, and how many buyers that covers. If you do not know, do not give a figure |
| One student's best month, with "results not typical" in small print | The typical result as the main message. An exceptional result can be shown only if it is clearly presented as exceptional and does not contradict the rest of the ad |
| "Free training" that is mainly a sales pitch | Say it is a free introduction to a paid course, and give the price |
| "Doors close at midnight" | A real deadline, kept. If the offer comes back next week, do not claim it is ending |
| "Book a call to find out more" with no price | The price, what is included, and your refund and cancellation terms |
| "Anyone can do this" | Who it suits, what it takes (time, money for stock, fees), and the risks |
| Testimonials from friends, or paid for | Genuine testimonials you can evidence, with any incentive disclosed |

Keep the evidence behind every number. Rule 3.7 says you need it before the ad runs, not after someone complains.

## How to complain to the ASA

You can complain about an ad whether or not you bought anything. The ASA's [make a complaint page](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html) sets out the steps:

1. **Check it is an ad.** Paid social posts, sponsored influencer content, website sales pages and marketing emails usually are.
2. **Capture it.** Take screenshots or a screen recording showing the ad, the account that posted it, and the date. Ads disappear quickly.
3. **Note where and when you saw it.** Platform, date, and a link if there is one.
4. **Explain what is wrong.** The ASA asks you to limit a complaint to three issues. For course ads, the usual ones are "implies these earnings are typical", "claims a free offer that is not free" and "false deadline".
5. **Use the online form.** The ASA does not take complaints by email. If you cannot use the form, the ASA gives a phone number on its [complaints page](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint.html).

You must give your name and contact details, because the ASA does not accept anonymous complaints. According to [how the ASA handles complaints](https://www.asa.org.uk/about-asa-and-cap/the-work-we-do/how-we-handle-complaints.html), it protects the anonymity of members of the public who complain unless there is a good reason to reveal their details and it has their permission.

If the ad is a scam in paid online space, such as a social media ad, use the separate [scam ad reporting form](https://www.asa.org.uk/make-a-complaint/report-an-online-scam-ad.html).

### What happens next

Some complaints are resolved informally: the ASA tells the advertiser what is wrong and the ad is changed, with no published ruling. Serious cases get a formal investigation and a ruling published on the ASA website. Advertisers who keep breaking the rules can be referred to Trading Standards.

The ASA does not get refunds or compensation. If you have lost money, also contact the [Citizens Advice consumer service](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/), which can pass your report to Trading Standards, and your card provider.

## What goes wrong

- **Complaining about the course rather than the ad.** The ASA can act on what the ad claimed, not on whether the content was poor.
- **No evidence.** An ad you did not screenshot may have gone by the time the ASA looks.
- **Too many issues.** Pick the clearest three.
- **Expecting a refund from the ASA.** It does not do that. Run your refund claim separately.
- **Assuming a disclaimer makes it fine.** The 2025 rulings say the opposite when the disclaimer contradicts the headline.
