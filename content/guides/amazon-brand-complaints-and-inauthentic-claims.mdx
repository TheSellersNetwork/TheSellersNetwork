For Amazon sellers who buy genuine stock and then get a notice saying a brand has complained, or a buyer has called the item fake. After reading you will know which complaint you face, which of Amazon's documented responses fits it, what evidence Amazon accepts, and how to buy so the evidence exists before anyone asks. The Account Health page and plans of action are covered in the [account health guide](/guides/amazon-account-health); this guide is about the proof underneath.

Every rule here is from Amazon's help pages or GOV.UK and is linked where used. Amazon rewrites these pages without notice, so check the linked page before relying on a detail.

## Two different accusations

| | Intellectual property complaint | Authenticity complaint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it says | Your listing or product infringes a trade mark, copyright, patent or design | Your product is not genuine |
| Policy | [Intellectual Property Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201361070?locale=en-GB) | [Anti-Counterfeiting Policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201165970?locale=en-GB) |
| What resolves it | A retraction from the rights owner, or an appeal showing a mistake was made | Invoices showing a verifiable supply chain for the units sold |

Both are among the [policies](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GQ5DSES264XVXNX7?locale=en-GB) that feed the Account Health Rating. Amazon's [rating policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G200205250?locale=en-GB) says repeat violations of the same policy cost more points each time, and that reaching the maximum repeats for one policy within 180 days can put the account at immediate risk of deactivation, with the limit as low as two for higher severity violations. One complaint is a problem to solve. A second under the same policy within six months is far more dangerous.

The Anti-Counterfeiting Policy is the harsher of the two. It says Amazon may suspend or terminate the account and related accounts, dispose of inventory at your expense, and withhold payments. Not knowing is no defence: the IP policy says Amazon will still act against a seller who infringes without knowledge. Treat authenticity notices as the most serious thing on the page.

## What a reseller can and cannot prove

**You can prove** that you bought a specific product, in a specific quantity, on a specific date, from a specific business, if that business gave you a proper invoice with your name on it. That is evidence the goods are genuine and came through a supply chain Amazon can check.

**You usually cannot prove** that the brand consented to the goods being on the UK or EEA market. A shop invoice shows where you bought them, not where the shop bought them. Amazon's policy says that if a rights owner challenges your sales as parallel imports, you will have to prove the goods were imported or sold in the UK or EEA with the rights owner's consent.

**You cannot prove anything** with a card slip, a till receipt that says "HEALTH & BEAUTY £12.00", a dispatch email, or a spreadsheet you made yourself.

**You cannot make a brand like you.** Being right is not the same as being left alone. A brand that objects to anyone outside its distribution may keep filing complaints, and each one has to be dealt with.

### Invoices against receipts

Amazon's [invoice requirements for appeals](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GDQ9K277NYP6WNEW?locale=en-GB) define an invoice as a document issued by the supplier that itemises the goods and records a completed transaction. For a policy violation appeal the invoice must show:

| Requirement | What Amazon says |
| --- | --- |
| Date | An issue date within the 365 days before you received the notification |
| Quantity | Enough to cover your sales and in-stock inventory of each cited ASIN over the past 365 days |
| Products | Identifiers that are easy to understand: product name, model number or barcode that can be checked online |
| Supplier | Name, phone number, address and website. Amazon says it may contact the supplier and keeps the details confidential |
| Status | A completed and fulfilled transaction |
| Format | PDF, JPG, PNG or GIF |

You may remove prices, but everything else must be visible and unaltered, and the supplier should be verifiable through government records such as tax registration numbers or credible third parties such as brand websites. Amazon will not accept quotes or pro formas, self-issued invoices or ones where the supplier looks like the same business as the buyer, or invoices that are manipulated or in an editable format such as Excel or Word.

Read the 365 days and the quantity rule together. If you sold 40 units of an ASIN over the year and can show invoices for 24, the appeal is weak however good those 24 are. Ungating has its own rules, set out in the [ungating guide](/guides/amazon-ungating-uk#what-amazon-requires-from-an-invoice).

A till receipt is not an invoice in this sense: it rarely names you, often does not identify the product, and gives a store address at best. The [receipt problem](/guides/amazon-retail-arbitrage-uk#the-receipt-problem) explains what to ask the shop for instead, and [what Amazon needs](/guides/amazon-vat-invoices-and-business-accounts#what-amazon-needs) covers the name and address rules.

### Letters of authorisation

Some notices ask for a letter of authorisation from the brand. Amazon's [letter of authorisation page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GUF78PCXAMHDY2A6?locale=en-GB) treats it as a licence naming the licensor, licensee, what is granted, the territory and the term, on the brand's letterhead with an authorised signatory or from an email on the brand's own domain. It lists what does not count: invoices, distribution or reseller agreements, receipts from online or physical retailers, order confirmations and packing slips.

A reseller buying from retailers will not get one. If a notice accepts only that, you will not win it on documents; talk to the brand or remove the product.

## Responding to an intellectual property complaint

Amazon's [IP policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G201361070?locale=en-GB) sets out the routes. Pick the one that fits the facts, not the one that feels quickest.

### First, read what is actually alleged

Work out from the notice which kind of right is claimed:

- **Trade mark.** Your listing uses a brand name or logo. If the goods are genuine and not an unlawful parallel import, Amazon's policy says you can use the trade mark to market that product. Complaints still come when the brand doubts the goods, or when the listing misuses the mark: a bundle presented as the brand's own, or "similar to" wording.
- **Copyright.** Usually images or text. If you built the listing with photos from the brand's website, the complaint may be correct.
- **Patent or design.** Aimed at the product itself rather than the listing, and rare for resellers of genuine branded goods.
- **Parallel import.** The rights owner says the goods came from outside the UK or EEA without consent.

### The four documented routes

1. **You never listed the product.** Reply to the notification and say so. Amazon says it will investigate whether an error occurred.
2. **You have a relationship with the rights owner** (a licence, manufacturing or distribution agreement). Contact them and ask for a retraction. The rights owner's contact details are in the notice. If Amazon receives a retraction from the rights owner, your content may be reinstated.
3. **You believe the rights owner or Amazon made a mistake.** Reply to the notification with specific reasons and, where appropriate, invoices or Order IDs that show the product is authentic. Amazon says it will re-evaluate the notice.
4. **You have received several warnings and believe the goods are genuine.** Appeal through Seller Central with a list of the ASINs and at least one of: invoices proving authenticity, Order IDs demonstrating authenticity, or an authorisation letter from the rights owner that is not a forwarded email.

For parallel import notices the policy is more specific: contact the rights owner first to seek a retraction, and appeal to Amazon only if they do not respond, with evidence that the goods were placed on the UK or EEA market with consent. Invoices from an authorised distributor are one form of that evidence.

### Contacting the rights owner

Keep it short, factual and polite: what you sold, where you bought it, that you believe it is genuine, and whether they will retract. Do not threaten or argue law. Amazon's seller help does not set out how a rights owner submits a retraction, so ask them to send it to Amazon themselves rather than forwarding it to you. If there is no reply in a reasonable time, use route three and say in the appeal that you tried.

### When the complaint looks like extortion

Amazon's IP policy says it may refuse to act on abusive notices, such as a trade mark registered without real commercial intent followed by payment demands. If you are asked for money for a retraction, do not pay. Appeal through the notification with screenshots of the demand and any evidence you used the name before the mark was registered. The policy says direct evidence of bad faith payment demands will result in reinstatement.

### When to delete instead

If a low-value ASIN with no stock draws a complaint from a brand that plainly wants resellers gone, removing the offer is usually the right commercial answer; the [account health guide](/guides/amazon-account-health#acknowledging-and-appealing) covers when deleting or acknowledging resolves it. Fight where you have good invoices and meaningful sales.

## Responding to an authenticity complaint

Authenticity notices are resolved on documents, against the [invoice requirements](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GDQ9K277NYP6WNEW?locale=en-GB) above.

1. **Stop selling the ASIN** if you have any doubt about the stock.
2. **Pull your sales** of each ASIN for the 365 days before the notification, plus current inventory.
3. **Match invoices to that total.** If you cannot cover the quantity, say so plainly in the appeal and explain the gap. Do not pad it with receipts from unrelated products.
4. **Attach PDFs named for what they are**, highlight the relevant line where an invoice lists many products, and keep the covering note to the facts. If a plan of action is required, write it as the [account health guide](/guides/amazon-account-health#writing-a-plan-of-action-that-works) describes.
5. **Inspect the returned unit** if a buyer complaint started it. A return that is not the item you sent is evidence in its own right; photograph it.

If Amazon rejects invoices, its page says the performance notification will give the reasons. Read them and fix that point, rather than resubmitting the same file.

## Where the right to resell comes from in the UK

This is general information, not legal advice. If a brand's solicitor writes to you, speak to a solicitor.

The principle is called exhaustion. The Intellectual Property Office's guidance on [exhaustion of IP rights and parallel trade](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exhaustion-of-ip-rights-and-parallel-trade) explains that once goods have been placed on the market in a territory by the rights holder, or with their consent, IP rights cannot be used to stop their further distribution or resale. The UK operates what the guidance calls a UK+ regime: goods placed on the UK or EEA market with consent are treated as exhausted in the UK.

For trade marks this is in [section 12 of the Trade Marks Act 1994](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/12), with two limits worth knowing:

- It covers goods put on the market in the UK or EEA **by the proprietor or with their consent**. Goods first sold outside both, for example grey imports from elsewhere, are not covered unless the brand consented.
- It does not apply where the brand has **legitimate reasons** to object, in particular where the condition of the goods has been changed or impaired. Amazon's policy gives the example of goods sold in cheap packaging that no longer gives the quality image the brand chose. Repackaging and splitting multipacks carry this risk.

So stock bought in UK or EEA shops is the easiest to defend. The other way round is weaker: GOV.UK warns that goods placed on the UK market may no longer be exhausted in the EEA, and Amazon's policy says a rights owner can stop sales of UK-sourced items in its European stores. If you [sell UK stock into Europe or bring EU stock into the UK](/guides/amazon-to-amazon-arbitrage#route-two-eu-to-uk), treat brand complaints as a known cost of that route.

Selling counterfeits is also a criminal matter; GOV.UK lists the [trade mark offences](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/intellectual-property-offences/intellectual-property-offences) and explains [who to contact](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses) if you find fakes in your supply chain, starting with Trading Standards.

## Prevention through sourcing paperwork

Most lost authenticity appeals were lost at the point of purchase. The fix is a buying habit, not a better appeal.

- **Buy on invoice, with your details on it.** Business name and billing address exactly as in Seller Central, from the first order. A till receipt is the weakest document you will own.
- **Vet the supplier before the first order**, the way Amazon will: a website, a company or VAT number you can check, a phone line that answers. Amazon may contact the supplier, and one that will not confirm a sale is no use for branded stock. The IPO's [supply chain toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/supply-chain-toolkit) covers the warning signs of counterfeits entering a supply chain.
- **Ask a new wholesaler where it buys.** A supplier that can tell you it buys from the brand's UK distributor gives you a stronger chain, and a better answer to a parallel import complaint, than one that will not say.
- **Keep a file per ASIN**, every invoice named by date and supplier, so the 365 day total takes minutes. Keep at least a year; HMRC's rules on [keeping VAT records](https://www.gov.uk/charge-reclaim-record-vat/keeping-vat-records) ask for longer if you are VAT registered.
- **Check brand behaviour before you buy deep.** Brands that complain about resellers tend to keep doing it. Buy small, or leave it.
- **Do not change the product.** No repackaging and no bundles presented as the brand's own.

## What goes wrong

- **Answering an authenticity notice with till receipts.** They do not meet the invoice definition.
- **Invoices that cover half the sales.** Amazon's quantity rule looks at a year of sales and stock, not the last box you bought.
- **A forwarded email from a sales rep as proof of authorisation.** Amazon's IP policy specifically asks for an authorisation letter that is not a forwarded email.
- **Editing an invoice.** Adding your name, changing a date, or sending a Word file. Amazon rejects manipulated or editable documents, and an altered one is far worse than a missing one.
- **Paying a complainant to go away.** Report it through the appeal instead; Amazon's policy treats payment demands as abuse.
- **Winning, then relisting the same brand at volume.** A second complaint on the same policy within 180 days costs more points than the first.
- **Opening a second account while the first is restricted.** See the [account health guide](/guides/amazon-account-health#acknowledging-and-appealing); it turns a document problem into a termination.
