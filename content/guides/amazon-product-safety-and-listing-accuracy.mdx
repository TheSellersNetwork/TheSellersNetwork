This is for arbitrage and wholesale sellers who put other people's products on Amazon. You did not make the product, but you are the one selling it, and in UK law and in Amazon's rules that carries duties. After reading this you will know which categories carry safety rules, what to check on the product before you buy, what Amazon can ask you to produce and when, how to tell a listing that does not match your item and what to do about it, and roughly what is changing.

The rules link to GOV.UK and to Amazon's help pages. Product regulation changes often and a major rewrite is under way, so check the current page before relying on anything here. Nothing in this guide is legal advice; where a product is borderline, ask your local Trading Standards service, which offers business advice, before you buy stock.

## Why a reseller is responsible

UK product safety law puts duties on everyone in the supply chain, not only the manufacturer. A distributor (which is what a reseller is) must not supply a product they know or should know is non-compliant, must act with due care, and must be able to say where they got it and who they sold it to. Trading Standards can prosecute for unsafe or counterfeit goods whether or not you knew, and the defence is showing you took reasonable steps: bought from a legitimate supplier, kept the invoice, checked the product for the marks and warnings it should carry, and stopped selling when something looked wrong.

Amazon's [product safety and compliance](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/GUH6FA4XSJ2LZFLY) pages say much the same in its own terms: you are responsible for making sure everything you list complies with the law, and Amazon can ask for evidence at any time and remove the listing or your selling privileges if you cannot provide it. The two sets of rules reinforce each other. The paperwork that satisfies Amazon is the paperwork that protects you in law, and it is the same paperwork you keep for brand approvals: supplier invoices with your name and address on them.

## Category by category

### Toys

A toy is anything designed or intended for play by a child under 14. The [Toys (Safety) Regulations 2011](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/toys-safety-regulations-2011) set the essential safety requirements for the Great Britain market. What you can check on the box:

- **A conformity mark.** UKCA or CE. The government now [recognises the CE mark indefinitely](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/using-the-ukca-marking) alongside UKCA for the Great Britain market across the product areas covered by both regimes, so either is acceptable in England, Scotland and Wales. In Northern Ireland the EU rules apply and CE is the mark that counts (with UKNI alongside it in some cases). A toy with neither mark is not a product to sell.
- **Manufacturer and importer name and address.** The regulations require the manufacturer's name and address on the toy or packaging, and if it was made outside the UK an importer's name and address in the UK as well. Retailer stock of well known brands carries this. Marketplace imports often do not.
- **Age warnings.** Toys not intended for children under 36 months must say so, with the reason (for example, small parts). The wording is set by the regulations and Amazon requires the same information in the listing. Activity, chemical and water toys carry their own warnings.
- **Instructions and warnings in English.**

Amazon's own [toy safety requirements](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/GJ42UXNKWUNT5BHA) page lists what it can ask a seller for: images of the product showing the marks and warnings, a Declaration of Conformity, and test reports against the relevant standards from an accredited laboratory. As a reseller you will not have test reports. What you can do is buy from suppliers whose products carry everything above, keep the invoice, and if asked, request the Declaration of Conformity from the brand or supplier. A brand that will not provide it is a brand to drop from your list.

### Food and grocery

Food sold in the UK must be labelled in line with the rules on [GOV.UK's food labelling pages](https://www.gov.uk/food-labelling-and-packaging): the name of the food, the ingredients list with allergens emphasised, the quantity, a best before or use by date, storage conditions where they matter, the business name and address, country of origin where required, and any instructions. Prepacked food from UK retailers has all of this. Imported food often does not, and a label in another language is not compliant.

Three practical points for FBA:

- **Dates.** Amazon requires dated stock to arrive with a minimum shelf life and disposes of it before the date; the current figures are in Amazon's expiry policy and summarised in [the manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk). Check the date on the product before you buy, and again on the invoice quantity, because a case of 12 with three months left is not a buy.
- **Allergens.** The listing must show the allergen information the label shows. If the detail page's ingredients differ from the pack (recipes change), do not sell on it until it is corrected. A customer with an allergy relying on a wrong listing is the worst version of a "not as described" complaint.
- **Storage.** Chilled and frozen food cannot go through standard FBA. Meltable products (chocolate, some sweets) are refused for FBA between dates Amazon sets each year; check before buying seasonal confectionery in spring. Amazon's grocery category also needs approval before you can list at all; see the [categories that need approval](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/200333160).

### Cosmetics

Cosmetics on the Great Britain market must have a UK based Responsible Person named on the label, and that person must have notified the product through the [Submit Cosmetic Product Notifications service](https://submit.cosmetic-product-notifications.service.gov.uk/) before it went on sale; the Office for Product Safety and Standards runs the service and [publishes figures](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-data-on-uk-cosmetic-product-safety-notifications) on notifications. The label must also carry the ingredients list, a batch number, and either a best before date or a period after opening symbol, with the function of the product where it is not obvious.

For a reseller the check is the label. A UK or EU brand bought from a UK retailer has a Responsible Person address. A product bought from an overseas marketplace, or grey imported, often has an EU address but no UK one, or none at all, and cannot legally be sold in Great Britain. Amazon can ask for the Responsible Person details and product safety information for cosmetics; if you cannot get them from your supplier, do not list. The beauty category is also gated on Amazon.

### Supplements

Food supplements are food in law and must meet the general food labelling rules plus [specific supplement rules](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-supplements-guidance/food-supplements-guidance): the words "food supplement", the nutrients and their amounts per daily dose, the recommended daily dose and a warning not to exceed it, a statement that supplements should not replace a varied diet, and a warning to keep out of reach of young children. Health claims on the label and in the listing are restricted to those on the authorised list, and it is easy to sell on a listing that makes a claim the product is not allowed to make.

Amazon treats supplements as a restricted category with its own compliance requirements and can ask for a certificate of analysis, product labels and the manufacturer's details. Some ingredients are restricted outright on Amazon and some are medicines in UK law rather than supplements. This is a category to enter carefully or not at all, and only with products from mainstream UK brands bought from mainstream UK retailers.

### Batteries and anything with one

Anything containing or sold with a lithium battery is a dangerous good for transport purposes and needs classification before FBA will accept it. Amazon's page on [lithium batteries and products containing them](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G200383420) and the [dangerous goods required information](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G201371860) page set out what it needs: a safety data sheet from the manufacturer, or where none exists, Amazon's own exemption sheet completed for the product, plus the battery's specification (chemistry, watt hours or lithium content). The listing must declare the battery accurately when you add the product. Apply to the [FBA dangerous goods programme](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/GZLZBQ7W6QZRKWWK) early; classification takes weeks and a product cannot ship until it is done.

In law, the seller of batteries and battery containing electricals also has obligations under the UK producer responsibility rules for waste batteries and electrical equipment (take back schemes and, above thresholds, registration). A small reseller of retailer stock is usually below the thresholds, but check the current GOV.UK guidance if electricals become a large part of what you sell.

### Everything else

Electrical goods need a conformity mark, a UK plug and instructions in English. Products for babies (bottles, teats, cots, car seats) have their own standards. Anything for children under three that is not a toy still has to be safe for a child to use. The general rule for all consumer products is the same: it must be safe, and the person who put it on the UK market must be identifiable. If a product has none of the marks or names it should have, that tells you what kind of supply chain it came from.

## What Amazon can ask you for

Amazon's compliance requests arrive as a notification against the ASIN, with a deadline and a list of documents. The common ones:

| Request | What it means | What a reseller can supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supplier invoice | Proof of where the stock came from | The invoice, with your business name and address matching Seller Central, dated within the period Amazon specifies |
| Product images | Show the marks and warnings on the actual product | Clear photos of every side of the packaging and the product, including the conformity mark, age warning and manufacturer address |
| Declaration of Conformity | The manufacturer's statement that the product meets the regulations | Request it from the brand or supplier; many publish it on their website |
| Test reports | Laboratory results against the standard | Only the manufacturer has these. Ask the supplier; if unavailable, remove the offer |
| Safety data sheet or exemption sheet | Transport classification for dangerous goods | From the manufacturer's website, or Amazon's exemption sheet completed from the product's specification |
| Responsible Person details | Who is legally responsible for the product in the UK (cosmetics, and increasingly other categories) | From the label; from the brand if not |
| Product labels | The full label as sold | Photos, with ingredients and warnings legible |

Two things about responding. Supply exactly what is asked, by the deadline, and nothing else. And if you cannot get a document, remove the offer yourself rather than let the deadline pass; a removed offer is a listing you can add again, a compliance suppression is a mark against the account. Keep the documents you gather; the same brand will be asked about again.

## Listings that do not match your item

This is the mistake that ends more arbitrage accounts than any safety rule. You buy a genuine product from a real shop, match it to a listing that is nearly right, and sell it. The customer receives something that differs from the detail page, complains, and Amazon treats it as a counterfeit or a not as described violation. The invoice does not help, because the invoice is for a different product from the one on the page.

### What "does not match" means

- **Different brand.** The listing's brand attribute is not the brand on your product, even though the title and images look the same. Check the brand field and the "Visit the store" link under the title against the name on the pack. A listing for a supermarket own label product that has been hijacked onto a brand name, or a generic listing carrying a brand's images, is a listing to leave alone.
- **Different pack count or size.** Two 250g packs are not a 500g pack. A "pack of 3" listing is not three singles. A 24 pack is not two 12 packs. The units per pack and the per unit size are listing attributes, and they must match your stock exactly. Amazon's fair pricing rules also mean a multipack can never cost more per unit than the single, and you cannot create your own multipack of a brand's product without the brand's own barcode or written permission.
- **Different variation.** Colour, scent, flavour, size and model year are variations. The parent listing may look right while the child you have attached to is wrong.
- **Different edition or generation.** Electronics, games and books change model and edition with the same name. The barcode is the check.
- **Different condition.** "New" means sealed, complete, in original packaging, without retailer stickers. Damaged packaging or a missing accessory is not new.
- **A listing that is itself wrong.** The title says one thing, the images another, the bullet points a third. Sellers have piled onto it and the reviews complain about receiving different things. This is a bad listing, and selling on it inherits its complaints.

### The check before you buy

Scan the barcode on the product and compare it with the barcode on the listing, then compare title, brand field, pack count, size and images against the product in your hand. Read the most recent reviews for "received the wrong item". If any of these disagrees, either find the right listing or do not buy. Add this to your buying checklist alongside the margin and the brand approval, because it is cheaper to check than to argue.

### Report it rather than sell on it

If a listing is wrong (wrong brand, wrong images, wrong pack count, unsafe product, a claim it should not make), report it. In Seller Central go to Account Health and use Report abuse, choose the type of violation, give the ASIN, and explain in plain terms what is on the page and what is wrong with it, with photos if you have them. Amazon does not tell you the outcome, and some reports come back with a template saying no violation was found. Report anyway. A report on record is evidence that you saw the problem and acted on it, which is exactly what "due care" looks like if Trading Standards or Amazon ask later. And then do not list on it.

If you are already selling on a listing that turns out to be wrong, remove your offer first and report second. Existing orders should be handled honestly: if the customer will receive something different from the page, contact them and refund rather than ship.

## Where the law is going

The [Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/20) received Royal Assent in July 2025. It does not itself change what you must do tomorrow. It gives the government the power to rewrite product safety law by regulation, and the government has started using it. In March 2026 it opened a consultation on [the UK's new product safety framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/product-regulation-the-uks-new-product-safety-framework/the-uks-new-product-safety-framework), which closed in June 2026. The direction of travel in that consultation:

- **Duties for online marketplaces.** Formal obligations on platforms to check who is selling, act on unsafe products and keep repeat offenders off. In practice this means Amazon asking for more documents from more sellers, more often.
- **A wider set of responsible businesses.** Fulfilment service providers and others in the chain would carry duties alongside manufacturers, importers and distributors.
- **Modern risks.** Product information provided digitally, and software and connected product safety.
- **New enforcement tools.** Civil penalties and undertakings alongside prosecution.

For a reseller the practical message is that the standard of proof is rising. The habits that already work (buy from suppliers who can be identified, keep every invoice, check the marks and names on the product, report wrong listings, drop products you cannot document) are the ones the new framework will expect. Sellers who build them now will not notice the change. Sellers who rely on the listing being someone else's problem will.

## What goes wrong

- **Matching to a nearly right listing.** Pack count, size, variation and brand field must all match. The invoice is no defence for the wrong product.
- **Buying toys with no mark and no address.** They cannot be sold legally, and Amazon will ask for documents you cannot get.
- **Selling imported food or cosmetics with no UK label or Responsible Person.** Not compliant in Great Britain, whatever the price.
- **Listing a product with a battery without declaring it.** It will be flagged, stranded and stuck until classified.
- **Letting a compliance deadline pass.** Remove the offer yourself if you cannot supply the document.
- **Ignoring a wrong listing because it is selling.** The complaints on it become yours.
- **Health claims in supplements listings.** Only authorised claims are allowed, and Amazon and Trading Standards both police them.

## Questions

**The product has a CE mark but not UKCA. Can I sell it?** In Great Britain, yes, for the product areas where the government recognises CE indefinitely, which includes toys. Check the [current UKCA guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/using-the-ukca-marking) for the list. In Northern Ireland, CE is the required mark.

**Amazon wants a Declaration of Conformity and the supplier will not give me one.** Remove the offer and stop buying that brand from that supplier. A legitimate supply chain can produce it.

**Can I report a listing that a competitor is on?** Yes, if it is wrong. Reports are about the page, not the seller, and a false or retaliatory report is itself a violation.

**Do these rules apply if I sell FBM rather than FBA?** Yes. The law applies to the seller, however the parcel gets there.
