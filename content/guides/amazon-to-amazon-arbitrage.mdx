This is for UK Amazon sellers who already run [online arbitrage](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage) and keep seeing leads where the supplier is Amazon itself: a product Amazon has dropped to a price below what third party sellers are getting on the same listing, or a product cheaper on a European Amazon store than on the UK one. It explains how each route works, what the rules are, what can go wrong and how to do the sums. Customs and VAT facts link to GOV.UK; check the current page before relying on any figure. None of this is tax or legal advice.

## The idea, and why it exists

Amazon's own retail prices move constantly. Automated pricing, promotions, overstocks, price matching against other retailers and clearance of lines it is dropping all push Amazon's price down, sometimes well below the price the same listing settles at when Amazon is out of stock. Amazon's clearance store, now called Amazon Resale (it was Amazon Warehouse), sells returned and open box units at further discounts. And each of Amazon's European stores prices independently, so the same product can be cheaper in Germany or Spain than in the UK.

A seller who buys at the low point and sells when the price recovers is doing arbitrage with Amazon as the supplier. It is popular because the supply is enormous, the listing you will sell on is the one you are buying from (so the match is exact), and every purchase comes with an invoice. It is also the most crowded form of arbitrage there is, for the same reasons.

## First, what Amazon's own terms say

Read this before anything else, because it shapes every decision below.

Amazon.co.uk's [Conditions of Use and Sale](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GLSBYFE9MGKKQXXM) say that Amazon sells products in quantities that correspond to the typical needs of an average household, that this applies both to a single order and to several orders of the same product, that purchases are not for resale or commercial use, and that Amazon reserves the right to refuse dealers and resellers. Its [quantity limit](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GVMU4A8KZ54WP4MP) page adds that limits are applied to some items and that larger quantities are what Amazon Business is for.

So a consumer account used to buy stock for resale is outside the spirit of the terms, and Amazon enforces that by cancelling orders, applying quantity limits and, in some cases, closing buying accounts. A separate Amazon Business account, registered to your trading entity, is the version of this Amazon has built for commercial buyers, and it is the only sensible way to do the UK to UK route. Even then, quantity limits apply, orders can be cancelled, and buying an item Amazon is clearing in order to sell it back on the same listing is something Amazon can see.

The one thing you must never do is use your Seller Central login to buy. Your buying account and your selling account should have different emails, different phone numbers and separate payment methods, so that a restriction on one cannot reach the other. The [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#staying-on-the-platform) says the same thing for the same reason.

## Route one: UK to UK

### Where the price differences come from

There are three prices on most listings worth watching.

**Amazon's retail price.** When Amazon holds the Buy Box it sets the market. When it drops its price for a promotion or to clear stock, third party sellers with a floor price stop selling, and when Amazon's stock runs out the price returns to whatever the remaining sellers want. The gap between the promotional price and the recovered price is the opportunity. The price history chart shows both, and it shows how many days the recovery took last time.

**Amazon Resale.** Returned and open box units graded Like New, Very Good, Good or Acceptable; Amazon's page on [Resale product conditions](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=202074290) describes each grade and the 30 day return right. Two facts decide whether these are stock. Under Amazon's own condition rules, a returned or opened unit is not new, so you can only relist it as used in the matching condition, and the used market on most listings is small and priced accordingly. And what you receive varies: sellers we know have had sealed, perfect units and units with missing parts under the same grade. Resale stock is for sellers who know the product and have a used market for it. It is not a beginner's route.

**Third party sellers.** Occasionally a seller misprices, or clears stock below the market, and their price sits under everyone else's for a day. Buying it out is legal and common. It is also how a seller with a repricer fault gets emptied by other sellers in an afternoon, so be sure the price is a mistake and not a signal that the product has a problem you have not spotted.

### Buying through a separate Amazon Business account

Amazon Business is Amazon's commercial buying platform. You register a business (sole traders and companies both qualify; the registration asks for business details and may ask for verification documents), and you get access to business only prices, tiered quantity discounts set by sellers and by Amazon, VAT exclusive price displays and downloadable VAT invoices. Amazon's [help page on business pricing](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=TdrCsyjGIPfdD96n9a) explains that business prices and quantity discounts are only shown to registered business customers, and its [invoice help](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=202119460) says invoices for items sold by Amazon and most sellers are available to download from Your Orders around 24 hours after dispatch, with items from other sellers requested from the seller.

Set it up as the manual describes: on a different email and phone from Seller Central, in the exact business name and address Amazon has for your seller account, with its own payment card. Do not open several business accounts to get around quantity limits; Amazon links accounts by address, payment method, device and network, and linked accounts inherit each other's problems. One properly registered business account, used sensibly, lasts. Five thin ones do not.

Quantity discounts deserve a word. Some listings show a lower per unit price when you buy, say, five or ten. The discount can turn a marginal product into a good one. The chart still decides whether the listing can absorb ten more units from you.

### Do Amazon's invoices work for ungating

Mostly no, and it is important to understand why. Amazon's brand and category applications ask for invoices from the manufacturer or an authorised distributor, showing your business details and theirs, at least ten units in total and dated within the last 180 days, and they say retail receipts and order confirmations are not accepted for approval. An invoice from Amazon Business is a proper VAT invoice from a retailer, not a distributor, so for approval purposes it sits in the same place as a supermarket's invoice. Sellers we know have occasionally had one accepted; none of them plan on it.

Where the Amazon invoice is genuinely useful is everywhere else: it is a clean VAT invoice for your books, it names the product and quantity, and it is convincing evidence of a legitimate source if a buyer or Amazon questions authenticity. That is worth having. It is not a route to approvals.

### The UK to UK checklist

1. The listing is the one you will sell on, and your account can sell it (brand, category, hazmat, Transparency).
2. The chart shows Amazon's price recovering after previous drops, and shows how long it took.
3. The current low price is Amazon clearing, not Amazon permanently repricing a product it will keep in stock. Twelve months of history helps here.
4. The numbers work at the recovered price, less 10% to 15% for the other sellers who saw the same drop, with every fee and VAT on fees included.
5. Quantity is what the listing sells in a fortnight divided by the sellers who will be on it, and within the quantity limit on the product page.
6. You are buying on the business account, to your business address, and the invoice will be downloadable.

## Route two: EU to UK

The same idea across a border. A product on a European Amazon store is priced lower than on Amazon.co.uk, sometimes by enough to cover delivery, currency and tax, sometimes because the UK listing has fewer sellers or a brand that is scarce here. Since the UK left the EU's VAT area and customs union, every parcel from an EU store into Great Britain is an import, and that changes the numbers and the paperwork.

### What happens at the border

The rules for goods sent to Great Britain from abroad are on GOV.UK's [tax and duty page](https://www.gov.uk/goods-sent-from-abroad/tax-and-duty). In summary:

- **VAT is due on everything** except gifts worth £39 or less.
- **Consignments worth £135 or less.** The seller (or the marketplace) charges UK VAT at the point of sale, so the price you pay at checkout includes it, and there is no customs duty on non excise goods at this value. GOV.UK's guidance for [overseas sellers of goods to UK customers](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/vat-and-overseas-goods-sold-directly-to-customers-in-the-uk) sets out the mechanism. If you give a UK VAT registration number, the sale can instead be zero rated with you accounting for the VAT under the reverse charge.
- **Consignments over £135.** Import VAT and, where it applies, customs duty are charged at the border and collected by the carrier before delivery. The carrier will contact you and typically hold the parcel for around three weeks awaiting payment. Some stores show and collect an estimated import charge at checkout instead.
- **Customs duty depends on origin, not on where you bought it.** Goods that originate in the EU can enter the UK at zero tariff under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement if they meet the rules of origin; GOV.UK explains [how to claim the preferential rate](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/claiming-preferential-rates-of-duty-between-the-uk-and-eu), including that consignments under £1,000 do not need a formal statement on origin. Goods made outside the EU (most consumer electronics, toys and homeware) do not qualify, and the duty rate for them is on the [UK Trade Tariff](https://www.gov.uk/trade-tariff).
- **Excise goods** (alcohol, tobacco) attract duty whatever the value.

The £135 figure is the value of the consignment, not of each item, and it is the value of the goods without delivery. A basket of five items at £30 is a £150 consignment.

### The business side

A business importing goods needs an EORI number starting with GB, which is free and issued by HMRC ([who needs one and how to apply](https://www.gov.uk/eori)), and someone has to make a customs declaration, which for a parcel from an Amazon store is done by the carrier. GOV.UK's [import goods step by step](https://www.gov.uk/import-goods-into-uk) sets out the sequence. Keep the commercial invoice and the customs paperwork with your records.

If you are VAT registered, import VAT is recoverable, either through your return using [postponed VAT accounting](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-when-you-can-account-for-import-vat-on-your-vat-return), if your VAT number is on the declaration, or by claiming it back with the import VAT certificate. If you are not VAT registered, the import VAT is simply a cost, and it is 20% of the value of the goods plus duty plus delivery. That one line is why the EU route is mostly a VAT registered seller's route.

Northern Ireland is different. Goods moving between the EU and Northern Ireland are not treated as imports in the same way, and a Northern Ireland business may need an EORI starting with XI; see the [EORI guidance](https://www.gov.uk/eori) and HMRC's Northern Ireland pages before assuming any of the above applies to you there.

### Who can realistically do it

Be honest about the conditions:

- **A separate buying account on the EU store,** with your business address and a payment card that does not charge foreign transaction fees, or the fee eats the margin. Some EU stores require address verification or refuse delivery to the UK on some items; check delivery to the UK on the product page before spending time on the chart.
- **VAT registration,** or margins fat enough to absorb 20% import VAT as a cost.
- **Basket discipline.** Keeping each consignment at or under £135 avoids duty and border delays but limits what you can buy in one go; going over it means budgeting for VAT, possible duty and the carrier's handling fee, and waiting.
- **A price history tool that covers the EU stores,** so you can compare the same product's chart on both sides.
- **Patience.** Delivery from an EU store to the UK is days to a couple of weeks, plus customs. A price recovery you were counting on can be over before your stock lands.
- **A returns plan.** Returning to an EU store means posting internationally, customs paperwork on the way back, and reclaiming VAT and duty you paid on the way in, which is possible but slow. Assume you will keep what you buy.

Most sellers we know who use this route are VAT registered, buy in small consignments, stick to products that originate in the EU (so no duty question), and treat it as a supplement to UK sourcing rather than the main event.

### UK to EU

Selling UK bought stock on European stores is a different business: EU VAT registration in at least one member state, EU product compliance and labelling, and shipping across a customs border to Amazon's EU warehouses or to customers. It is out of scope here. If you are curious, start with the FBA manual's advice to keep Europe switched off until you are established, and talk to an accountant who does cross border VAT before you list anything.

## The risks

**Your buying account.** Order cancellations, quantity limits, and, occasionally, a closed buying account are the price of doing something Amazon's consumer terms say not to. The business account reduces the risk; it does not remove it. Never let a buying account share an email, phone, card or device with Seller Central.

**Returns.** Amazon's returns are generous for consumers, and a business account gets similar rights, but returning fifteen units of the same product because the price collapsed is the sort of pattern that gets an account looked at. Buy what you can hold.

**Price collapses.** Everyone with an alert saw the same price drop. Amazon's price recovers, then twenty new sellers arrive with the stock they bought at the drop and the price falls again. The chart shows how this played out last time; assume it plays out worse this time.

**Amazon comes back.** If Amazon was clearing, fine. If Amazon was repricing and will restock at the same low price, you are holding stock you cannot sell at a profit. Twelve months of history and a look at whether Amazon has been in stock continuously will usually tell you which.

**Condition.** Amazon Resale units are used and must be sold as used. A "Like New" unit sold as new is a misdescribed item.

**Customs surprises.** A consignment that tips over £135, goods that turn out to be made in China rather than Germany, a carrier handling fee you did not price in. Each one turns a margin into a loss.

**Cash tied up.** Money spent on an EU order is out of reach for the delivery time, the customs time, the Amazon check in time and the selling time. Six weeks from card to bank is normal.

## The numbers

The arithmetic is the same as any arbitrage buy, with two extra lines for the EU route. Work it on a realistic sell price, which is where the chart spends most of its time after a recovery, not the peak.

| Line | UK to UK | EU to UK |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buy price | Amazon's price, less any business or quantity discount | The EU price converted at your card's real rate, including any foreign transaction fee |
| Delivery to you | Usually free over the store's threshold | The store's UK delivery charge, spread across units |
| VAT on purchase | Included in the price; reclaimable if you are registered | At or under £135: charged at checkout, reclaimable if registered. Over £135: import VAT at the border, reclaimable if registered, otherwise a cost |
| Customs duty | None | None on qualifying EU origin goods; the tariff rate on others |
| Carrier handling fee | None | Charged by the carrier on consignments where it collects tax; check the carrier's page |
| Amazon referral fee | On [Amazon's fee page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) by category | Same |
| FBA fulfilment and storage | By size and weight; higher October to December | Same |
| VAT on Amazon's fees | 20% on every fee; a cost if not registered | Same |
| Inbound to Amazon | Per box, spread across units | Same |
| Returns allowance | A few percent of sales | Same, and any return to the EU store is your problem |
| Price fall allowance | 10% to 15% off the recovered price | 15% or more, because your stock lands later |

A shape to have in your head for the UK route: a product Amazon dropped from £30 to £18 and which the chart shows recovering to £28 for weeks at a time after previous drops. Sell at £25 to allow for company. Referral fee at 15% is £3.75; a small standard FBA fee, storage and inbound might be around £4 together (look yours up); VAT on those fees, if you are not registered, adds another £1.55; a returns allowance of 3% is £0.75. That leaves roughly £15 against an £18 buy, which is a loss. The same product at a business price of £12 leaves a margin of about £3, or 25% on the money, before your time. The lesson is not the specific numbers, which are illustrative, but that a headline drop of 40% is often not enough once Amazon's own fees and the crowd have had their share.

For the EU route, add the import lines. A £120 consignment of EU made goods arrives with UK VAT already charged at checkout and no duty; a £180 consignment of Chinese made goods arrives with import VAT, a tariff and a carrier fee to pay before you can have it, and possibly a fortnight later. Build the basket to the rule you want to be under, and check origin before you rely on zero duty.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying on the Seller Central login.** A cancelled order becomes a selling account problem. Separate everything.
- **Several buying accounts.** Linked, limited, and eventually all closed together.
- **Expecting Amazon's invoice to ungate a brand.** It is a retailer's invoice. Plan approvals through distributors.
- **Selling Resale units as new.** They are used, and Amazon and the buyer both know it.
- **Reading the drop, not the recovery.** The chart's job is to show you what happened after the last three drops.
- **A £140 basket.** Five pounds over the line, and the parcel waits at the depot for VAT and a handling fee.
- **Origin assumed.** Bought in Germany does not mean made in Germany. The tariff applies to where it was made.
- **Import VAT as a surprise.** If you are not VAT registered, it is 20% of the landed cost you cannot get back.
- **Foreign transaction fees.** A card that adds a few percent to every EU purchase takes that off every margin.
- **Holding too long.** Amazon's price does not always recover. Set a date by which you will clear at whatever the market gives.
