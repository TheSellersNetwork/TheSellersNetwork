This is for resellers who post their own orders and want the packaging bill to stop creeping up. After reading it you will be able to choose between a bag and a box for any item, pack to the cheapest Royal Mail format that will still protect the item, buy packaging at the right price in the right quantity, reuse what comes in without breaking anyone's rules, and know at what size a business has to start reporting its packaging.

Packaging is where a lot of small sellers quietly lose their margin: a few pence of bag on every order, a box that tips a large letter into a small parcel, a breakage every fortnight. None of it shows up as one big number, which is why it is worth an hour of thought once and a routine afterwards. Nothing here is legal advice.

## What packaging has to do

In order of importance:

1. Get the item to the buyer in the condition you described. A damaged item is a return at your cost and a defect risk.
2. Keep the packed item inside the cheapest postage band it can safely fit. Royal Mail's formats are set by the outer dimensions of the packed item, not the item itself; the limits are on its [size and weight guide](https://help.royalmail.com/personal/s/article/Size-and-weight-guide).
3. Meet the carrier's conditions, so that if it is lost or damaged you can claim. Royal Mail's [compensation policy for damage](https://www.royalmail.com/retail-compensation-policy-damage) and its [policy for loss](https://www.royalmail.com/retail-compensation-policy-loss) both exclude items that were not packed in a strong enough container, or breakables that were not marked.
4. Look acceptable. Not branded, not beautiful; clean, secure, and not held together with a whole roll of tape.

Anything beyond those four is cost.

## Mailing bags versus boxes

The decision that drives everything else.

| | Mailing bag | Box |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | Clothing, textiles, soft goods, anything that cannot break or bend | Anything rigid, fragile, heavy, or with corners |
| Cost per unit in bulk | Pennies | Tens of pennies and up |
| Weight added | A few grams | Tens to hundreds of grams |
| Effect on format | Can keep a soft item inside large letter | Almost always a parcel |
| Storage at home | A drawer | A wall |
| Weakness | Crushing, punctures from sharp corners | Cost, weight, empty space that must be filled |

The rule most experienced sellers land on: a bag unless there is a reason for a box. The reasons for a box are that the item can break, that it can be bent or creased, that it is heavy enough to tear a bag, or that it has corners that will puncture one. A pair of jeans goes in a bag. A framed print, a mug, a games console, a stack of vinyl all go in a box, whatever the postage saving.

Between the two sits the padded envelope (bubble lined bag), which handles most small, semi-rigid things: cables, cosmetics, small electronics in their own box, books. It costs more than a plain bag and less than a box, and it is the workhorse of most reseller operations.

**Two types of mailing bag.** Standard polythene mailers with a peel and seal strip are the cheapest and the most common. Paper mailers and compostable film mailers cost more and appeal to some buyers; they are worth it if your buyers care and your prices carry it, and not otherwise. Whichever you use, buy bags with a permanent adhesive strip. A bag that opens in transit is a lost item with no claim.

## The large letter test

Royal Mail's large letter format is up to 35.3cm x 25cm x 2.5cm and 750g, or 1kg on Tracked services bought online. Its small parcel format starts where large letter ends. The price gap between them is the single largest saving in small item postage, and it comes purely from how you pack.

The test: pack the item exactly as you will send it, then pass it through a 2.5cm slot. Royal Mail's own size guide is the reference; a slot cut in stiff card is enough at home, and a plastic sizing gauge costs a few pounds if you post a lot. If it passes freely, it is a large letter. If you have to push, it is a parcel, and it will be charged as one at the counter or surcharged to the recipient if you have already labelled it.

What gets through:

- T shirts, blouses, thin jumpers, leggings, scarves, folded flat in a bag.
- Paperbacks, most hardbacks under about 2cm, magazines, comics in a board backed envelope.
- Trading cards, stamps, coins, jewellery and watches without their box, in a small padded bag.
- Cables, phone cases, cosmetics, small toys and parts in a padded bag with the air pushed out.
- DVDs, CDs and games in their cases (just), and out of their cases with a lot to spare.

What does not, whatever you do: shoes, mugs, anything in a rigid retail box thicker than about 2cm, most children's toys, anything you have padded generously.

Three habits make the test routine. Keep a slot gauge at the packing bench. Record the packed format against every product you sell more than once, so you never re-measure. And where a product sits on the line, change the packaging rather than the service: a slimmer bag, no bubble wrap on a thing that does not need it, the item out of a bulky retail box with the box flattened alongside.

## Protecting fragile items

Fragile items are where cheap packaging costs the most, because a breakage is a full refund, return postage, a defect risk and usually the loss of the item. Royal Mail's damage policy also makes compensation conditional on packing: breakables must be in a strong container with enough material to protect against pressure and knocks, and marked "FRAGILE HANDLE WITH CARE" in capitals above the address; things that can be bent must be in a container that prevents bending and marked "DO NOT BEND". If you skip the marking you can lose the claim as well as the item.

The method that works, in order:

1. **Wrap the item itself.** Bubble wrap or foam sheet, so nothing hard touches the item. Wrap each piece separately if there are several.
2. **Immobilise it.** The item should not be able to move inside the box. Fill every gap with paper, air pillows, loose fill or cut card. Shake the closed box; if you hear movement, open it and fill.
3. **Keep it off the walls.** At least a few centimetres of cushioning between the item and every face of the box. Corners are where boxes get dropped.
4. **Use a box that is strong enough.** Single wall corrugated card is fine up to a few kilograms. Double wall for anything heavy, ceramic or glass, or for anything over about 5kg.
5. **Box in box for the fragile and valuable.** The item in its own small box, cushioned, inside a larger box, cushioned again. It is the only method that reliably survives couriers.
6. **Tape all seams.** H pattern on top and bottom, and along the edges on heavy boxes. Packing tape, not parcel string, not masking tape.
7. **Mark it.** "FRAGILE" on at least two faces, above the address as Royal Mail requires. Do not expect it to change how it is handled; it changes whether you can claim.

Two things that seem like protection but are not: a "fragile" sticker on an underpacked box, and a mountain of bubble wrap around an item that can still move. Immobilising is what saves things.

For liquids, powders and anything that can leak, bag the item, then pack it. A leak that damages the outer packaging is an item Royal Mail can refuse to deliver, and a mess the buyer photographs.

## Where to buy packaging in bulk

The unit price of packaging falls hard with quantity, and the difference between a pack of ten from a stationer and a case of five hundred from a packaging supplier is often a factor of five or more. Types of supplier, and what each is good for:

**Online packaging wholesalers.** Companies that sell mailing bags, boxes, bubble wrap and tape by the case, direct to small businesses, with next day delivery and a trade account after a few orders. This is where most resellers should buy once they are posting more than a handful of items a week. Buy the standard sizes (they are the cheapest per unit), sample first (most sell sample packs), and compare on the per unit price including delivery, not the headline.

**Box manufacturers and corrugated suppliers.** Worth it if you send a lot of the same item and want a box that fits it exactly, or for double wall boxes in quantity. Minimum orders are higher, lead times longer, and the price per box is the lowest available.

**General online marketplaces.** Convenient and competitive on small quantities of bags and padded envelopes; less so on boxes because of the delivery cost. Useful for filling a gap, and for a new seller who does not yet know what sizes they need.

**Cash and carry and trade counters.** A local option for tape, bubble wrap on the roll and boxes without paying for delivery on bulky goods. Worth a visit if one is near.

**Local businesses discarding packaging.** Shops, warehouses and offices break down clean boxes every day and most will give them away. Covered under reuse below.

What to hold in stock: three or four sizes of polythene mailing bag covering large letter to small parcel; two sizes of padded envelope; three sizes of single wall box; a roll of bubble wrap; void fill (used paper is free); packing tape and a dispenser; a slot gauge; a scale that reads in grams up to a few kilograms. Standardise early so that everything you sell packs into one of a small number of formats you can price without thinking.

On cost, work out packaging per order for each format and put it in your listing margin next to postage. A polythene mailer plus a label is a few pence; a padded envelope is more; a box with fill and tape is often 50p to well over £1. Many sellers under price small parcel items because they only count the postage.

## Reusing packaging responsibly

Reuse is the cheapest packaging there is and buyers on resale platforms mostly expect it. It has rules, all of them practical.

- **Remove or fully cover every old label and barcode.** A parcel with two barcodes on it can be scanned to the wrong destination, and a visible old address is the fastest way to a "lost" parcel. Peel, black out, or cover with your new label and plain tape.
- **Only reuse packaging that is still strong.** A box that has been opened twice has creased corners and a crushed edge; it will not pass the "strong container" condition in a claim. Boxes once, bags never (a resealed polythene mailer is not sealed).
- **Clean, dry and odour free.** Smoke, damp and pet hair are returns waiting to happen, and a "not as described" for condition is a defect risk.
- **No retailer branding that misleads.** An item arriving in another shop's printed box is fine if the listing was honest, and an easy complaint if it looked like a different product.
- **Keep supplier packaging that came with your stock.** Retail cartons, dividers and inserts from wholesale deliveries are usually better than anything you would buy.
- **Fill with paper you already have.** Shredded or scrunched packing paper from incoming deliveries is free void fill. Do not use newspaper against anything pale; the ink transfers.

Reused packaging does not affect a Royal Mail claim as long as it meets the conditions above. A buyer cannot make a claim against you for the packaging being second hand if the item arrived as described.

## The packaging waste rules

The UK's packaging Extended Producer Responsibility scheme makes businesses that put packaging onto the market pay for it. It has thresholds, and most sole traders and small companies are well under them. GOV.UK's [check if you must comply](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-must-comply-with-epr-for-packaging--2) page sets them out.

You are an obligated producer only if all three apply:

- you are established in the UK,
- you supplied or imported more than 25 tonnes of packaging in the previous calendar year, and
- your annual turnover is £1 million or more.

Within that, a small producer (turnover between £1 million and £2 million with over 25 tonnes, or over £1 million with 25 to 50 tonnes) must register with the environmental regulator, report packaging data once a year and keep records for seven years. A large producer (over £2 million and over 50 tonnes) reports every six months, must obtain recycling evidence, and pays the disposal fees for household packaging. GOV.UK's [prepare for EPR](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/packaging-waste-prepare-for-extended-producer-responsibility) page covers the registration and fees.

To put 25 tonnes in perspective: a padded envelope weighs a few tens of grams and a small box a few hundred. A seller sending a few thousand parcels a year is nowhere near it. The threshold starts to matter for businesses turning over seven figures and sending tens of thousands of boxed parcels, and for anyone importing packaged goods in volume, because imported packaging counts as yours when you are the first UK business to handle it.

What a growing seller should do is keep the door open, not act now. Track packaging purchases by weight (suppliers show it on the invoice) alongside turnover, and if you are heading past £1 million and towards 25 tonnes, read the GOV.UK page properly and take advice. The obligation is assessed on the previous year's data, so you will see it coming.

Separately from EPR: packaging you sell on (for example, if you start selling packaging itself) and packaging you put around goods must meet the general requirement to be no more than necessary for safety, hygiene and acceptance by the buyer. In practice, do not overpack. That is also the cheapest option.

## Decisions, with our recommendation

**Bag or box as your default?** Bag. Box only for the reasons above. The postage and packaging saving pays for the odd extra padded envelope many times over.

**Branded or plain?** Plain, until you have a reason. Printed mailers cost more per unit and force a bigger minimum order. A printed thank you slip inside a plain bag does the same job for a fraction of the cost.

**Buy small and often, or a case at a time?** A case at a time for your two or three most used formats, once you know what they are. Small and often for everything else. Packaging that sits for a year is money on a shelf.

**Should I charge for packaging?** Never as a separate line; buyers hate it and eBay's fees apply to the total anyway. Put it into the item price or the postage price, whichever your category tolerates.

**Bubble wrap on everything?** No. It adds thickness (which changes the format), weight and cost. Soft goods do not need it. Rigid things that cannot break do not need it. Use it for the things it protects.

## What goes wrong

- **Packing an item to 2.6cm.** The whole saving evaporates. Use the gauge every time.
- **A box with room to move.** The item arrives broken and the claim is refused because the packing was inadequate.
- **No FRAGILE marking on a breakable.** Royal Mail's damage policy is explicit; it is a condition of compensation, not decoration.
- **Reusing a bag.** It is not sealed, whatever you do with tape.
- **Old barcodes left on a reused box.** Misrouted, delayed, or delivered to the previous address.
- **Buying packaging one pack at a time from a stationer.** Usually several times the case price.
- **Counting postage but not packaging in the margin.** A box, fill and tape can be the difference between profit and none on a £10 item.
- **Assuming the packaging waste rules apply, or assuming they never will.** Look at the thresholds once a year with your turnover figure to hand.

## Questions

**Is a padded envelope a large letter?** If the packed thickness is 2.5cm or under, yes. Most small padded envelopes with a thin item inside pass. Anything with a rigid box inside usually does not.

**Can I send a shoe box as it is?** You can, but it is a small parcel at best, the lid will come off, and shoe boxes are not strong. Put it in a mailing bag at minimum, or in an outer box for anything expensive.

**What weight does the packaging add?** Weigh it. As a rough guide, a polythene mailer is under 10g, a padded envelope 20g to 60g, a small single wall box 100g to 300g. Royal Mail's size guide has example weights for common items to check your scale against.
