This is for anyone who buys things to sell on eBay and wants to stop guessing the price. After reading it you will be able to filter sold listings so they tell the truth, read the spread of prices rather than the top one, adjust for condition and postage, pick a format, and work backwards through eBay's fees to a buy price that leaves you a margin. Fees change; every figure below links to the page it came from, and you should check the current page before relying on any of them. None of this is tax, legal or financial advice.

## Where the sold data lives

There are three places, and they answer different questions.

**The search results filter.** Search the item, then in the filters tick Sold items. Ticking Sold also turns on Completed items, which is the wider set: everything that ended, whether or not it sold. Sold is the number you price from; Completed tells you how many listings failed to sell at the prices people tried. The search filter only shows a recent window (in practice about the last three months), and it only shows listings, not the accepted amount when an item sold through Best Offer, which is displayed with a line through the asking price. eBay's own [pricing page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling/pricing-items?id=4133) points you at the Completed listings option in Advanced Search for the same data.

**The Research tab in Seller Hub.** eBay's [product research](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/product-research?id=4853) tool holds up to three years of sales and gives you average sold price, the sold price range, average postage, the share of listings with free postage, the number of sellers, and a sell-through rate for searches covering 90 days or less. It can filter by condition, date range, brand, buyer and seller location, and product code. For anything you plan to buy repeatedly, this is where you should be looking, because three months of search results is not enough to see seasonality.

**Your own orders.** In Seller Hub under Orders for the last two years ([finding your sold items](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/finding-sold-items?id=4108)). Your own history beats everyone else's because it includes your photos, your postage terms and your returns.

## Filtering so the comps tell the truth

A sold search done lazily gives you a number that is wrong in a direction you cannot see. Do these every time.

1. **Match the exact item.** Model number, size, edition, colourway, pack size. A search for a brand and a product type mixes three generations of the same thing. Put the distinguishing word in the search and use the minus sign to exclude words that pull in the wrong variant (for example the word for a different size or a bundle).
2. **Filter by condition.** New, new other, used, for parts. Used comps do not price a boxed item and boxed comps do not price a used one.
3. **Set location to the UK.** Comps from sellers overseas come with different postage, different buyer protection and different demand. In the search filters, set item location to UK only.
4. **Sort by ended most recently.** Prices move. The last thirty days matter more than the sixty before them, especially for anything seasonal, anything with a new model out, or anything that just appeared on a television programme.
5. **Open the ones you are going to price from.** Not every one; the eight or ten around the middle. You are checking whether the sale was a single item or a bundle, whether it was an auction that ended at three in the morning, whether the buyer paid postage on top, and whether the asking price has a line through it, which means the seller accepted an offer for less and you cannot see how much less.
6. **Ignore the outliers on both ends.** A signed copy, a mispriced listing that sold in a minute, a job lot that matched your keywords. They are real sales and useless comps.

## Reading the spread

You are not looking for the highest price; you are looking for the price at which the item reliably sells within the time you are prepared to wait. Take your filtered list and write down the sold prices, item only, from lowest to highest. Then read three numbers off it.

| Number | What it is | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low quarter | The price a quarter of the way up the list | Where the item goes when the seller wants it gone. Your liquidation floor. |
| Middle | The price halfway up the list | What the item is actually worth to buyers this month. Price here and expect to sell at the average speed. |
| High quarter | The price three quarters of the way up | What a well presented listing with a good seller can ask. Only price here if your photos, description and feedback are as good as the ones that got it. |

Then look at the gap. A tight spread (the high quarter within about 15% of the middle) means the market knows what the item is worth; price at the middle and move on. A wide spread means condition, completeness or presentation is doing the work, and the difference between the quarters is what you earn by grading honestly and photographing properly.

Read the Completed list next to it. If most completed listings did not sell, and the ones that did sold at the low end, the market is thin and the middle is optimistic. Finally, count active listings. Twenty sellers at the middle price with three sales a week is a queue; price below them or accept the wait.

## Condition and postage adjustments

The comps give you a price for the average example sold at the average postage. Yours is not that.

**Condition.** Grade against what the sold listings actually were, not against eBay's condition labels alone. A used item with the box and all accessories usually sits at the high quarter; the same item with a scuff and a missing cable sits at the low quarter. If the comps for "used" are mostly clean examples and yours is not, price off the low quarter and say why in the description, because a buyer who is surprised is a buyer who returns.

**Postage.** Buyers compare the total, so you must too. Turn every comp into item price plus the postage the buyer paid, and compare on that basis. If you plan to offer free postage, your item price has to absorb the label and still land at the same total. Use the actual cost of the service you will use, from [Royal Mail's UK price pages](https://www.royalmail.com/sending/uk) or your courier's price list, not a guess, and remember that a business seller's final value fee is charged on the postage amount as well as the item ([fees for business sellers](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/fees-business-sellers?id=4809)).

**Private sellers and postage.** Since 2025 private sellers on eligible items must use eBay's [Simple Delivery](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/posting-items/setting-postage-options/simple-delivery?id=5575), where eBay sets the label and the buyer pays at checkout or the seller offers it free and has the cost deducted. The postage a buyer sees on a private seller's listing is therefore eBay's rate, not a Royal Mail price the seller chose. When you compare your total against private seller comps, compare against what the buyer paid in total, which is what the sold listing shows.

**The Buyer Protection fee changes what buyers see.** When a buyer looks at a private seller's item, the price shown already includes the [Buyer Protection fee](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/buying/paying-items/buyer-protection-fee?id=5594): £0.10 plus 7% up to £20, then 4% up to £300, then 2% up to £4,000. The private seller still gets their list price ([information for sellers](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling/pricing-items/buyer-protection-information-sellers?id=5595)). On a £28 item that adds £1.82, so the buyer sees £29.82. If you are a business seller, your £28 shows as £28, so you are already cheaper on screen than a private seller listing at the same figure. If you are still selling as a private seller, a displayed price of £28 means listing at about £26.25. This matters when you read comps: eBay says the price buyers see includes the fee, so a private seller's sold price as you see it in the results may not be the amount that seller received. Treat it as what the buyer paid and work backwards if you need the seller's figure.

## Auction or Buy It Now

The comps answer this. If the sold list is mostly auctions ending across a wide range, the item has an active bidding market and a low start auction can reach the middle or better. If the sold list is mostly fixed price at a tight spread, the market knows the price and an auction only invites people to pay less.

| Situation | What we would do | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tight spread, plenty of sold fixed price comps | Buy It Now at the middle, Best Offer on with a floor | The price is known; the offer setting captures the buyers who want to feel they negotiated. See [Best Offer strategy](/guides/best-offer-strategy). |
| Wide spread, many sold auctions, collectable or hard to grade | Auction, seven days, ending on a Sunday evening, start at your floor or use a reserve | Bidders find the price; you protect the downside. A reserve is a paid upgrade ([reserve prices](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/reserve-prices?id=4143)); check the current fee. |
| Thin market, most completed listings unsold | Buy It Now at the low quarter, or hold the stock for the season it sells in | An auction with no bidders ends at the start price or not at all. |
| You need the cash this week | Auction from a start price you can live with | Speed costs money; decide the floor and accept it. |

Auction rules to know before you commit. Durations are 1, 3, 5, 7 or 10 days ([selling through auctions](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/selling-auctions?id=4110)). A Buy It Now on an auction must be at least 40% above the start and vanishes at the first bid in most categories. Once an auction has a bid and there is no unmet reserve, you cannot end it in the last 12 hours, and ending it earlier with bids costs a private seller 5% of the highest bid up to £20, and a business seller a final value fee on the highest bid ([ending a listing](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/cancelling-listing?id=4146)). Best Offer is not available on an auction with a Buy It Now price ([Best Offer help](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/selling-buy-now/adding-best-offer-listing?id=4144)). A 99p start is a decision to sell at whatever two people will pay; only do it when the comps show the bidding reliably gets there.

## Fees: what comes off the comp price

### Private sellers

UK based private sellers pay no final value fee and no regulatory operating fee on anything except vehicles, and get 300 free listings a month ([fees for private sellers](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/selling-fees?id=4822)). This started on 1 October 2024. Since February 2025 the buyer pays a Buyer Protection fee on top, shown inside the price, and private sellers on eligible items use Simple Delivery. A private seller's margin is therefore sale price minus label minus packaging, which is why undercutting private sellers by a few pence rarely works: they have no fee to cover and can go lower.

### Business sellers

Business sellers pay one final value fee per sale: a category percentage of the total sale (item, postage and any tax) plus a per-order fee of 30p on orders up to £10 and 40p above ([fees for business sellers](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/fees-business-sellers?id=4809)), plus a regulatory operating fee of 0.35% of the total, plus 30p per listing if you have no Shop, plus VAT at 20% on all of it. The percentages by category on the current page include, for example, 11.9% for Clothes, Shoes and Accessories, 9.9% for Books, Cameras, Computers and Mobile Phones, 10.9% for Antiques and Art, 12.5% for Business, Office and Industrial, and 14.9% up to £1,000 for Jewellery and Watches, with many categories dropping to a lower percentage above a price threshold. The page is the source; look up your own category there each time you set a floor, because the [February 2026 rate card change](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/news/2026-january/rate-card-change) moved several of them and the per-order fee, and it will move again.

Promoted Listings are on top of all this: a general ad charges your chosen ad rate (from 2% upwards) on the total sale amount if the item sells within 30 days of a click ([promoted listings](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/listing-tips/promoted-listings?id=4164)). If you promote, the ad rate is part of your fee line.

### A worked example

Say the comps for a used jacket put the middle at £28 with the buyer paying £3.50 postage, and you are a business seller with no Shop in Clothes, Shoes and Accessories, not VAT registered, not promoting. Figures for the label, packaging and returns are illustrations; use your own.

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Total sale (item £28 plus £3.50 postage) | £31.50 |
| Final value fee, 11.9% of £31.50 | £3.75 |
| Per-order fee (order over £10) | £0.40 |
| Regulatory operating fee, 0.35% of £31.50 | £0.11 |
| Listing fee, no Shop | £0.30 |
| Fees before VAT | £4.56 |
| VAT at 20% on fees (a cost if you are not registered) | £0.91 |
| Fees after VAT | £5.47 |
| Label you actually buy (illustration) | £3.20 |
| Packaging (illustration) | £0.40 |
| Returns allowance, 3% of the item price (illustration) | £0.84 |
| What you keep | £21.59 |

So a £28 comp is worth £21.59 to you before you have paid for the jacket. If you want a third of the sale price as profit, £9.33, the most you can pay for the jacket is about £12.26. If it is a £15 jacket at the boot sale, walk on. The whole discipline of sold comps is that last line: the buy price is the output, not the input.

Two adjustments. If you are VAT registered, the VAT on fees is reclaimable, but you owe VAT on the sale itself under whichever scheme you use; run it with your accountant's method. If you promote at 5%, add £1.58 on the £31.50 and ask whether the item would have sold without it.

## Making it a habit

Do the example above in a spreadsheet with the fee percentage, per-order fee and VAT as cells you can change when the rate card does. One row per item: comp middle, postage, fees, label, packaging, returns allowance, what is left, maximum buy price. Write the floor into the listing's custom label field so that offers and later markdowns start from a number you calculated rather than one you feel. Keep the sheet; after a few months it is your own comp history, and it is better than eBay's because it includes your returns.

## What goes wrong

- **Pricing from active listings.** Asking prices are wishes. The sold filter is the only one that counts.
- **Pricing from the top comp.** One sale at £45 does not make yours worth £45. Price from the middle and let the high quarter be a bonus.
- **Forgetting postage is in the total.** Business sellers pay the final value fee on postage too, and buyers compare totals. Item price alone is a fiction.
- **Comparing your business price with a private seller's without the Buyer Protection fee.** Their buyer sees a price with the fee inside it; yours sees exactly what you set.
- **Ignoring Completed.** Fifty sold in three months looks good until you see three hundred completed and unsold at the same price.
- **Auctioning a known-price item.** If every comp is a tight fixed price cluster, an auction just sells it for less to the same buyers.
- **Working margins on last year's rate card.** Fees changed in February 2026 and will change again.
- **No returns allowance.** Every category has a return rate. If yours is zero, you have not sold enough yet.
