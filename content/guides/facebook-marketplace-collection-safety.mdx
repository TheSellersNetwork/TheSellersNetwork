This is for UK sellers who sell on Facebook Marketplace for collection: furniture, bikes, bulky items, anything where postage makes no sense. After reading it you will have a routine for each collection, from when you give out your address to the moment the item leaves, including how to check cash and bank transfers and what to do about buyers who never turn up. It draws on Facebook's help pages, police.uk and police force advice, the Bank of England and MoneyHelper, linked where used.

## Why collection needs its own rules

Collection is the main reason Marketplace works for resellers. No postage, no packaging, no parcel lost in transit. But it moves the risk from the parcel to you: a stranger knows where you are, and payment happens face to face, where fake screens and fake notes work best. Facebook's [Purchase Protection](https://www.facebook.com/help/2374002556073992) does not cover items exchanged in person with cash or person-to-person payment methods, so on a collection sale there is no one to appeal to afterwards. You are the protection.

## Before the buyer comes

### Decide where the handover happens

Facebook's [tips for meeting in person](https://www.facebook.com/help/2329750133711372) start with: keep your home address private, and meet in a public, well-lit area. That is right for anything portable.

| Item | Where to hand over | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phones, consoles, jewellery, anything small and valuable | A public place: a busy supermarket car park, a café, the entrance of a shop with cameras | Portable and attractive to steal; no reason for anyone to see your home |
| Clothes, books, small homeware | Public place, or doorstep drop-off | Low value; a doorstep collection is fine for many sellers |
| Furniture, white goods, bikes, anything bulky | Your home, with precautions below | Cannot reasonably be carried to a car park |

For higher-value items, the police.uk advice for vehicle sales is worth borrowing: if cash is involved, consider completing the sale at your bank or the buyer's ([police.uk](https://www.police.uk/advice/advice-and-information/fa/fraud/personal-fraud/online-shopping/)). A bank branch has cameras, staff and a counter that can check notes.

### If they must come to your home

- **Give the full address late.** Share the street or area when you agree the sale, and the house number on the day, once the buyer has confirmed they are on their way.
- **Put the item by the door, in the porch, or in the front garden or garage.** The buyer does not need to come in. If they need to test something bulky, test it at the door.
- **Have someone else in**, or at least tell someone who is coming and when.
- **Keep valuables, keys and paperwork out of sight** of the door.
- **Do not list other things you are selling at the same address** in a way that advertises the contents of your house.

### Share your plan

Facebook's help pages suggest sharing your meeting plan with a trusted friend or family member; Messenger has a Send Plan option that shares the time, date and location with someone in your Friends list and removes it after the meeting. Facebook also suggests live location sharing during the meetup, a charged phone, and not changing the meeting place once it is agreed. That last one matters both ways: if the buyer suddenly wants to meet somewhere else, treat it as a warning sign.

### Agree the price and payment method in writing first

Before anyone travels, confirm in Messenger:

- The price, and that it is the final price
- How they will pay: cash, or bank transfer that must show in your account before the item goes
- The time and the place

This saves the doorstep haggle and gives you a record. Our guide to [buyer messages and haggles](/guides/handling-haggles-and-is-this-still-available) has wording that works.

## Getting paid

### Cash

Cash is simple and final, which is why counterfeiters like collection sales. Police forces have warned about exactly this: Staffordshire Police reported sellers on social media and local selling sites being handed counterfeit notes at the point of sale, including a phone sold for £1,000 at a public meeting ([Staffordshire Police, January 2026](https://www.staffordshire.police.uk/news/staffordshire/news/2026/january/selling-scam-warning-as-counterfeit-notes-circulated/)).

Police advice is to **look, feel and tilt**:

- **Look:** sharp edges, no smudging.
- **Feel:** raised print, for example on the words "Bank of England".
- **Tilt:** check the see-through window and the metallic image.

The Bank of England's [How to check your banknotes](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/banknotes/counterfeit-banknotes/how-to-check-your-banknotes) page lists the features on the current polymer notes, including the see-through window with a portrait, the hologram in the foil patch, the 3D crown above the window, raised print, a second window on £20 and £50 notes, and a coloured foil patch on the back. Under good ultraviolet light, the value shows in bright red and green. Check more than one feature on every note.

Other points:

- **Count and check before handing anything over,** not after. A genuine buyer will not mind waiting while you do. Someone rushing you through it is a warning sign.
- **Check every note, not just the top one of the pile.**
- **Paper Bank of England notes are no longer legal tender.** The last paper £20 and £50 notes stopped being legal tender on 30 September 2022 ([Bank of England, withdrawn banknotes](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/banknotes/withdrawn-banknotes)). You can decline them.
- **Scottish and Northern Irish notes** are issued by banks there and look different. If you are not confident checking them, it is reasonable to ask for a transfer instead.
- **For larger sums, ask for a transfer,** or meet at a bank.

If you are given a counterfeit, the Bank of England says it [cannot reimburse you](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/banknotes/counterfeit-banknotes) and the note is worthless. Do not try to spend it. Report it to the police, keep the note as evidence, and follow their instructions.

A small ultraviolet torch or note-checking pen costs little and is worth having if you take a lot of cash. It is an aid, not a substitute for looking, feeling and tilting.

### Bank transfer

Transfers avoid counterfeit notes, but bring the fake screen problem. MoneyHelper describes buyers showing a fake payment confirmation on their phone, sometimes after getting the seller to type bank details into a fake app, and then claiming it can take a couple of hours to show ([MoneyHelper](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/blog/scams-and-fraud/facebook-marketplace-scams-how-to-spot-fake-messages)).

The routine:

1. **Give your sort code, account number and name in Messenger, typed by you.** Never type them into an app or website on the buyer's phone.
2. **Wait until the money shows in your own banking app,** opened by you, on your phone. Not a screenshot, not a text, not an email, not their screen.
3. **Check the amount and the name.**
4. **Only then hand over the item.**

If the buyer says it takes hours to arrive, they can come back when it has. A genuine buyer will usually wait or pay cash. This is the rule [police.uk](https://www.police.uk/advice/advice-and-information/fa/fraud/personal-fraud/online-shopping/) gives: always check your account online or ask your bank to make sure cleared funds have been received.

**Refuse overpayments** and do not refund anything until your bank confirms the original payment. See [Marketplace scams](/guides/facebook-marketplace-scams) for why.

### Payment apps and cheques

Facebook's help pages say buyers and sellers can offer person-to-person payment methods and warn that cashier and certified cheques can be counterfeit. For a collection sale, we would not accept a cheque of any kind. If a buyer offers to pay through a payment app, be aware that its protections are usually built for posted goods; MoneyHelper describes scammers using exactly that route to claim a collected item never arrived. Check that app's own terms before relying on it, and keep evidence of the handover.

## At the handover

- **Let them look at the item properly.** A buyer who inspects it and pays is less likely to come back with a complaint. Facebook's advice to buyers is to inspect items closely before paying.
- **Stay with the item until you are paid.** Do not let it go into a car "while I get the cash".
- **Take a photo of the item as it leaves,** and send a short message in Messenger after: "Thanks for collecting the table today, paid in cash." It records the sale if anything is disputed later. police.uk suggests photographing items before they go, as proof of condition.
- **Wipe electronics** before they go. Facebook's help pages remind sellers to clear personal information from devices.
- **Trust your instinct.** If something feels wrong, you can end it. Say the item is no longer available and close the door.

## No-shows

Every collection seller gets them. They are mostly annoying rather than dangerous, but they cost time.

| Approach | How it works | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed time, confirmation message | Agree a time; ask them to message when they set off | Always |
| "First to collect" | Tell each buyer it goes to the first person with the money | Low-value items with lots of interest |
| Hold for a set time | "I'll hold it until 6pm today, then it goes to the next person" | One serious buyer, others waiting |
| Back-up buyer | Keep the second person warm and tell them honestly they are next | Popular items |
| Deposit by transfer | A small part-payment to hold the item | Bulky, high-value items only; see below |

A deposit reduces no-shows but creates its own problems. If the buyer then does not come, you have to decide whether to keep it, and if they do come and dislike the item, whether to return it. If you take one, agree in writing what happens in each case. For most sellers we know, a clear time limit and a back-up buyer work better than deposits.

After a no-show, do not chase. Mark the item available again, and move to the next person. If someone repeatedly books and fails to appear, you can block them in Messenger.

## If something goes wrong

- **You feel unsafe:** leave, or close the door, and call 999 if you are in danger.
- **You were given counterfeit notes:** report to the police, keep the notes.
- **You handed over an item and the transfer never arrived:** call your bank, then report to Report Fraud, which has replaced Action Fraud for England, Wales and Northern Ireland, at [reportfraud.police.uk](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/) or on 0300 123 2040. In Scotland, contact Police Scotland on 101.
- **Report the buyer to Facebook** from the conversation, using the steps on Facebook's [Report a Facebook Marketplace buyer](https://www.facebook.com/help/280774317577363) page.

This is general safety information, not legal or financial advice.

## What goes wrong

- **Giving the full address the moment someone says "is this available".**
- **Letting the buyer into the house** for something that could have been carried to the door.
- **Checking the notes after the buyer has gone.**
- **Accepting a transfer shown on the buyer's phone.**
- **Typing your bank details into their app.**
- **Changing the meeting place at the buyer's last-minute request.**
- **Holding an item all weekend for someone who said "defo".**
- **No message after the handover,** so nothing records the sale.
