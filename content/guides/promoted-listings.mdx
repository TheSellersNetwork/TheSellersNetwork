This is for business sellers on eBay UK who are paying for Promoted Listings or wondering whether they should. After reading you will know the difference between the general and priority strategies, what triggers a fee under each, how to set an ad rate from your margin rather than from eBay's suggestion, which report numbers mean something, and when the right move is a better listing rather than a bigger ad budget.

eBay changes the names, the placements and the attribution rules for this product regularly. Every figure below links to the eBay page it came from; check the page before relying on it.

## The two strategies

eBay now describes Promoted Listings as one product with two campaign strategies. Older names (Standard, Advanced, Express) still appear in forum posts and in some of eBay's own pages; the [Promoted Listings overview](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/promoted-listings-overview?id=5295) and the [Seller Centre page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/grow-your-sales/ebay-advertising/promoted-listings) use the current ones.

| | General | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Old name | Standard | Advanced |
| You pay | When the item sells after an ad click (cost per sale) | Every valid click, whether or not it sells (cost per click) |
| You set | An ad rate, as a percentage of the sale | A daily budget, and either let eBay target (smart) or choose keywords and bids yourself (manual) |
| Placement | General ad slots across eBay | Priority slots, including the top of search, with keyword targeting and video |
| Risk | Bounded: no sale, no fee | Unbounded within the budget: clicks cost money even at zero sales |
| Who it suits | Almost any seller with margin to spare | Sellers with a known conversion rate on a product and the time to manage keywords |

A listing can be in only one general campaign at a time, but can be in several priority campaigns, and in both strategies at once.

There are also Promoted Shops and offsite campaigns aimed at larger sellers. They charge per click on the same lines as priority; the [attribution page](https://pages.ebay.co.uk/promote-your-listings/attribution/) covers them. Most resellers never need them.

## How the general fee is actually charged

This is where the money goes, so it is worth being precise.

- The fee is the ad rate multiplied by the **total** sale amount: item price plus postage plus any taxes and fees the buyer pays. If you charge £4.99 postage, you pay ad rate on the £4.99 as well.
- The rate used is the one in effect when the item **sells**, not when it was clicked. Lowering the rate on a Friday lowers the fee on Saturday's sales.
- The sale is attributed to an ad if the item sells within 30 days of a click on the ad, and the item was promoted at both the click and the sale. eBay's attribution page describes this as a purchase of the item featured in an ad that was clicked by any buyer in the most recent 30 days, on a last click basis. In practice, once one buyer has clicked your ad, the next sale of that item within 30 days is likely to carry the fee whether or not that particular buyer arrived through the ad. On a fast moving line, expect most sales to be attributed.
- Cancelled orders and full refunds have the ad fee credited; partial refunds credit the fee in proportion. VAT is added to the fee if it applies to you.
- The fee shows in the Payments tab in Seller Hub as a Promoted Listings fee line against the order.

The consequence for planning: on an item you promote continuously, treat the ad rate as a fee on every sale, like the final value fee. Do not model it as "only sometimes".

## Choosing an ad rate

eBay shows a suggested rate for each listing and offers a dynamic setting that moves your rate to the suggestion each day. The suggestion is calculated from item attributes, seasonality, past performance and competition. It is not calculated from your margin, and it is produced by the party that receives the fee. Use it as information about what other sellers are paying, not as a price.

The rate comes from your margin, and it goes like this:

1. Work out net profit per unit on the listing at its current price, after cost of goods, postage, packaging, final value fee, the regulatory operating fee, and VAT if you are registered. Call it £P on a total sale of £S.
2. Decide how much of £P you are willing to give up to sell faster. A third is a common ceiling among sellers we know; half is desperation.
3. The maximum ad rate is that share of £P, divided by £S. On a £30 sale (item plus postage) with £6 net profit, a third of the profit is £2, and £2 divided by £30 is about 6.7%. Round down.
4. Start below the maximum, usually at or a little above the 2% floor on lines that already sell, and higher only on lines that do not.
5. Never set a rate the profit cannot pay. A 10% rate on a £30 sale is £3; if net profit was £2.50, every promoted sale loses money and the report will still call it a success.

The dynamic setting has a place: seasonal stock where the suggested rate moves and you do not want to manage it daily. Everywhere else, fixed rates you have calculated. If you use dynamic rates, set them on a campaign whose listings all have similar margins, and look at the rate the campaign is actually paying every week.

Two more decisions:

**Which listings.** Promote the ones with the most to gain from being seen: new listings with no sales history, listings in crowded categories where you are on page three, and lines with strong margin and stock depth. Do not promote a listing that already holds the top organic position for its search; you will pay for sales you were getting.

**One campaign or several.** Several, grouped by margin band, so that the rate matches the profit. One campaign at one rate across everything is the commonest way to lose money without noticing.

## Priority: when the cost per click model makes sense

Priority charges you for the click. The bid is a second price auction against other sellers, above a reserve price eBay sets per click from item quality, competition and seasonality. You will spend up to the daily budget whether or not anything sells, and invalid clicks are not charged but your own clicks on your own ads are.

The arithmetic is different. If a listing converts one click in twenty (5%) and each click costs 30p, a sale costs £6 in ad fees. That has to fit inside the profit on the item, or the campaign is paying eBay to lose you money. You cannot know the conversion rate until you have run enough clicks to measure it, so a priority campaign is an experiment with a budget, not a setting you turn on.

Use it when:

- You have a product with margin above £10 a unit and a conversion rate you have measured on general or organic traffic.
- You can name the searches that buy it. Manual targeting with exact and phrase match on ten to thirty keywords beats broad match and smart targeting for a reseller, because broad match spends the budget on adjacent searches.
- You will look at the search query report weekly and add the wasted searches as negative keywords.

Do not use it for low value items, for auctions (not eligible), or as a way to move dead stock; a click on a listing nobody wants still costs 30p.

## Reading the reports

The Advertising dashboard in Seller Hub reports impressions, clicks, quantity sold, sales, ad fees and conversion rate for general campaigns, plus return on ad spend and average cost per click for priority. Reports can be generated at listing, keyword and search query level, and metrics can take up to 72 hours to reconcile, so do not judge a change the next morning.

The numbers that matter, and the ones that mislead:

| Metric | What it tells you | What it does not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impressions | Whether the ad is being shown at all | Whether anyone wanted the item |
| Click through rate (clicks divided by impressions) | Whether the title, photo and price make people click | Whether they buy |
| Conversion rate (sales divided by clicks) | Whether the listing itself sells once seen | Whether the sale would have happened without the ad |
| Ad fees | Exactly what you paid | Nothing else |
| Sales via ads | Sales eBay attributed to an ad | Sales that would have happened anyway, and profit |
| Return on ad spend (sales divided by fees) | A ratio eBay likes | Your margin; a ROAS of 10 on a 5% margin item is a loss |

The report you should build yourself, monthly, is simpler: for each promoted listing, total sales this month against the same listing in the month before you promoted it (or against the unpromoted period), and ad fees against net profit. If sales are up by more than the fees cost, promotion is working. If sales are flat and fees are present, you are paying for sales you already had. That comparison is the only one that includes the sales you did not need to buy.

A quick test for a line that has been promoted for months: pause the campaign for two weeks and watch. If sales hold, they were yours all along. If they fall away, promotion is doing real work and you can turn it back on knowing the price is worth paying.

## When promotion is worth it

- A new listing with no sales history in a category where the first sale brings the rest.
- A line with deep stock and healthy margin where more volume at a slightly lower net is better than slow sales.
- Seasonal stock with a date on it, where sitting on it costs more than the fee.
- Crowded categories where the organic position is far down and the listing is already good.

## When it is throwing money away

- A listing that already ranks at or near the top for its search. You are buying your own sales.
- Any item whose net profit is less than the fee at the rate you have set. Do the sum per listing.
- A listing with a low conversion rate. Traffic to a listing that does not convert is a listing problem, and ads make it a more expensive listing problem.
- One campaign with one rate across a mixed catalogue.
- Dynamic rates left unchecked on a line whose suggested rate has drifted up.
- Promoting to rescue a bad price. Buyers who click and see a higher price than the listing next to it leave; you have paid for the comparison.

## The alternatives, in the order to try them

Promotion multiplies what the listing already does. Fix the listing first, because each of these is free and each moves the same numbers the ad is meant to move.

1. **Price.** Check sold listings for the same item, filtered to the last month, and price against the ones that sold, not the ones still listed. The commonest reason a listing needs promoting is that it is priced against hope.
2. **Title.** All [80 characters](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/listings/create-listings/best-practices), the words buyers type, brand, model, size, colour, key attribute, nothing wasted on "look" or "wow". Search the title yourself and see what it competes with.
3. **Item specifics.** Filters on the left of search results are built from item specifics; a listing with blanks is invisible to anyone who filters. Fill every one eBay offers.
4. **Photos.** A clean first photo on a plain background at full size, then every angle, every flaw, the label, the box. Click through rate is mostly the first photo.
5. **Postage and returns.** Free delivery and 30 day returns change both search placement and conversion, and they are the route to the Premium Service discount if you are Top Rated.
6. **Best Offer and offers to watchers.** Cheaper than an ad rate on a listing with watchers who are not buying.
7. **Stock depth and sell through.** Listings that sell regularly rank better organically; sometimes the answer to a slow listing is to end it and relist with the fixes above.

Then, if the listing is good and the margin is there, promote it, at a rate you calculated.

## What goes wrong

- **Setting the suggested rate because it was suggested.** It is a market signal, not a margin calculation.
- **Forgetting the fee is on postage too.** The total sale amount includes it.
- **Promoting everything at one rate.** The thin margin lines subsidise eBay.
- **Judging by sales via ads.** That figure includes sales you would have made anyway. Compare against the unpromoted period.
- **Priority with broad match and smart targeting on a low margin item.** Budget spent by lunchtime, nothing sold.
- **Leaving dynamic rates on through a busy season.** The suggestion rises when competition does.
- **Never pausing.** The two week pause test is the cheapest research there is.
- **Promoting a listing that needs a new photo.** Paying for people to leave.

## Questions

**Is there a minimum rate?** 2% for general. Below that eBay says visibility may be affected; in practice a listing at 2% still gets ad placements, just fewer of them.

**Am I charged if the buyer clicked an ad but bought through search later?** If the sale is within 30 days of a click and the item was promoted at the click and the sale, the sale is attributed and the fee applies. The organic route the buyer took later does not matter.

**Can I promote as a Below Standard seller?** No. The seller standards policy blocks Promoted Listings for Below Standard accounts. Fix the metrics first.

**Where do I compare notes on rates?** [Promoted Listings and traffic](/community/c/ebay-promoted-listings-and-traffic). Post the category, the rate, the margin and what happened; the numbers are more useful than the opinion.
