This is for UK resellers who sell on one platform and are wondering whether a second would sell more of the same stock, and for those already on three who keep selling things twice. It covers the decision, the mechanics and the numbers. Fees and rules change often, so every figure links to the platform's own page; check it before you rely on it. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.

The private seller fee lines above are for context, not a plan.

## Which platform suits which stock

Platforms are not interchangeable. Each has a buyer who arrives expecting a certain kind of thing at a certain kind of price, and stock that is wrong for that buyer sits.

| Stock | Where it sells | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branded second-hand clothing and shoes, under about £50 | Vinted first, Depop for younger fashion, eBay for anything with a size or model number buyers search for | Vinted's buyers are there for exactly this and there is no seller fee; eBay's search is better for specific items |
| Higher value clothing, designer, vintage | eBay, Depop | Buyers searching by brand and era, and a returns process that supports a bigger ticket |
| New, barcoded products with existing demand | Amazon, then eBay multi quantity | Amazon's catalogue does the selling; eBay reaches people who do not shop on Amazon |
| Collectables, media, parts, anything niche | eBay | The only marketplace with the depth of buyers and the search to find one specific thing |
| Large, heavy or fragile items | Facebook Marketplace for local collection, eBay with collection only | Postage makes them uneconomic to send |
| Handmade, craft supplies, personalised | Etsy | Buyers arrive expecting exactly that |
| Bulk lots and job lots | eBay, Facebook Marketplace | Buyers are other traders |

**What we would do.** Match the platform to the stock, not the other way round. A clothing reseller belongs on Vinted and eBay. A new goods reseller belongs on Amazon and eBay. Almost everyone belongs on eBay, and almost nobody needs five platforms. The point of a second platform is to reach buyers the first cannot; if both platforms have the same buyers, you are doubling the admin for nothing.

## The double sale, and how to avoid it

The problem with listing one item in two places is obvious the first time it happens: it sells on both within an hour and you have to cancel one. On eBay that cancellation is a [defect](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/seller-performance-policy?id=4347), and defects at low volume move your rate fast. On Vinted or Depop it is a disappointed buyer and a review. Everywhere, it is a buyer who does not come back.

The fix is not to avoid listing in two places. It is to make sure that a sale anywhere removes the listing everywhere, quickly enough that the window for a second sale is small. There are four ways to do that and they scale with your volume.

### Level one: one item, one place

The simplest rule: each item is listed on exactly one platform, chosen for the stock type above. No double sales are possible. What you lose is the reach, and for a lot of stock that is fine; the buyer for a specific car part is on eBay and nowhere else. Sellers we know who run two platforms this way, by category, have almost none of the problems below.

### Level two: a spreadsheet and a routine

One row per item, with a SKU, a description, cost, the platforms it is listed on, and a status. When something sells, you update the row and end the other listings, and you do it before you pack the parcel, not after. The discipline is checking the sheet at fixed times: first thing, midday, evening, and immediately after any period away. This works to a few hundred listings if you are strict. It stops working the first weekend you are away from the laptop.

Put the SKU in every listing's SKU or custom label field, and on the item's bag. It makes the sale notification tell you which row to update, and it makes the item findable.

### Level three: platform features that reduce the risk

Some things narrow the window without a tool. On eBay, the [out of stock option](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/multiquantity-listings-listings-variations?id=4150) for multi quantity listings means a line that sells out elsewhere can be set to zero and hidden rather than ended. Longer handling times on the second platform give you a day to catch a clash before the buyer expects dispatch, though eBay [recommends](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/posting-items/estimated-delivery-dates-sellers?id=4086) no more than one working day and buyers prefer it. Listing high risk items (anything likely to sell fast) on one platform only, and slow movers on several, puts the risk where it is smallest.

### Level four: inventory or cross-listing software

Tools of this type hold one stock record and push quantity to every platform; when an item sells anywhere, the tool sets quantity to zero everywhere else within minutes. Some also create the listings on each platform from one master listing. The sync is the function you are paying for. Before buying, check three things: which platforms it actually connects to (many cover eBay and Amazon well and the clothing apps poorly or not at all), how fast the sync runs, and what happens to your listings if the tool goes down or you leave. Set up is real work: every existing listing needs a SKU that matches the tool's record before it can manage it.

**What we would do.** Level one until you have a reason not to. Level two when you have a category that genuinely sells on two platforms. Level four when you are past a few hundred active listings on two or more platforms and a double sale a month is costing you more than the subscription. Level three all the time.

## Platform rules that clash

Each platform has a rule book, and when you are on two the rule books disagree. The safe approach is to run the strictest rule everywhere, so one process satisfies all of them.

**Returns windows.** The law gives a consumer buying from a business at a distance [14 days](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) after delivery to cancel for any reason, and 30 days to reject faulty goods. eBay requires business sellers to state a returns policy and honour both; Amazon's own [returns policy](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GKM69DUUYKQWKWX7) gives buyers 30 days for most items, and on seller fulfilled orders you pay the return postage; Vinted Pro sellers agree to the platform's business terms. Run 30 days everywhere and the differences stop mattering. The margin has to absorb some returns whatever you do.

**Who pays return postage.** Faulty or not as described: you, on every platform and in law. Change of mind: you can make the buyer pay on eBay if your policy says so, Amazon's rules differ by category and fulfilment method, and the clothing apps handle it through their own process. Decide one policy and write it into every listing.

**Listing formats.** eBay lets you put up to [250 variations](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/multiquantity-listings-listings-variations?id=4150) of size and colour in one listing with a quantity for each; Amazon has one catalogue entry per product that you attach an offer to; the clothing apps expect one item per listing with its own photographs. A master listing built for one format has to be pulled apart for another, and the item specifics eBay wants (brand, size, colour, material, in its own fields) are not the hashtags a clothing app wants. Photograph once, with the strictest platform's requirements in mind (plain background, every angle, every flaw), and write the description once in plain sentences; then adapt.

**Condition language.** Amazon's condition grades are defined and enforced; eBay's are defined; the clothing apps are looser. Grade to the strictest definition and describe the flaws in words and photographs on every platform. This is the single largest cause of not as described cases for multi-channel sellers: an item graded generously for one platform and copied to another where the grade means something stricter.

**Dispatch and handling.** eBay counts an item as dispatched only when the carrier [scans it](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/posting-items/estimated-delivery-dates-sellers?id=4086) and recommends a one day handling time; Amazon measures late dispatch against your stated time; the clothing apps set their own days to post. One packing routine that gets every parcel scanned the next working day satisfies all of them.

**Messages.** Amazon wants buyer messages answered within 24 hours including weekends; eBay shows buyers your typical response time; the apps are conversational. Answer everything within a day and you are fine everywhere.

**Business seller status and reporting.** Every platform reports you to HMRC once you pass [30 sales or 2,000 euros](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reporting-rules-for-digital-platforms) in a year on that platform. Splitting sales across platforms to stay under the line on each is not a strategy; the threshold is about what the platform reports, not about whether you owe tax, and the [bookkeeping guide](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers) covers what does.

## Pricing differences between platforms

The same item should not carry the same price everywhere, because you do not keep the same amount of it everywhere.

Take a jacket you want to net £25 from after the platform's cut, with the buyer paying the postage separately.

- On eBay as a business seller in Clothes, Shoes and Accessories, the fee is 11.9% of the total including postage plus 40p plus 0.35%, so the item price needs to be roughly £29 to net £25 after fees on the item portion alone, and a little more once the fee on the postage is included.
- On Vinted, there is no seller fee, so the item price is £25 and the buyer sees £25 plus the Buyer Protection fee plus postage on top.
- On Amazon, the referral fee in Clothing is tiered by price (the [pricing page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) shows the bands), so the price needed depends on where it lands in the tiers, plus the monthly plan cost spread across your sales.

Two things follow. First, the fee difference means the buyer on a no seller fee platform can see a lower price than the buyer on eBay while you net the same. Sellers who set one price everywhere are either overcharging on the cheaper platform or underearning on the dearer one. Second, the buyer's view differs: Vinted and eBay private listings show a price with a buyer fee added; eBay business listings show the price the buyer pays. Compare what the buyer sees, on each platform, against what similar items actually sell for there, and price to that market, not to your other listing.

**What we would do.** Work the price backwards from the net you need on each platform, using that platform's fee page, and then check it against the sold prices there. Keep a small table of the fee percentages you actually pay by platform and category, and update it when the fee pages change, which they do.

## When to add a platform, and when to go deeper on one

Adding a platform is not free. It is a second set of rules, a second fee table, a second inbox, a second returns process, and a double sale risk. It is worth it when it brings buyers you cannot otherwise reach, and only then.

**Add a platform when:**

- You have stock that the current platform is poor at (clothing under £50 on eBay, collectables on Vinted) and enough of it to make the admin worthwhile.
- Your current platform is saturated for your category and the sell-through is falling while your listings are as good as they will get.
- You have a listing process that is consistent enough to reproduce: templates, photographs to a standard, a SKU on everything.
- You can commit to checking the new platform's messages and orders as often as the old one, from day one.

**Go deeper on one when:**

- Your listings are not yet good: poor photographs, thin item specifics, no returns policy. Fixing those on one platform raises sales more than a second platform will.
- Your sell-through on the existing platform is healthy and the constraint is stock, not buyers. More platforms do not make more stock.
- You have not yet learned the existing platform's tools for bulk editing, promoted listings or business policies. Most sellers are using a fraction of what they already pay for.
- You are already selling things twice or missing messages. A second platform multiplies that.

The order that works for most: get one platform to the point where listing is routine, sell-through is known, and returns are a process. Then add the one platform that fits the stock the first is poor at. Then stop, for a while, and see whether it earned its admin. Sellers we know who run two platforms well outsell those who run five badly, every time.

## What goes wrong

- **The same item sells twice.** No process for delisting, or a process that only runs when you remember.
- **Cancelling on eBay to honour a Vinted sale.** The eBay cancellation is a defect; the Vinted buyer would have understood a message. If a clash happens, cancel where it costs least and message honestly.
- **One price everywhere.** Overpriced on the platform with no seller fee, underpaid on the one with a fee.
- **One condition grade everywhere.** Generous grading copied to a platform where the grade is enforced.
- **Copied listings that break the format.** eBay item specifics missing because the description was written for an app; hashtags in an eBay title.
- **Returns policy from the loosest platform applied to all.** The law's 14 days and 30 days apply to you everywhere, whatever a listing says.
- **A sync tool that covers two of your three platforms.** The third is where the double sales happen.
- **Ignoring the second platform's messages.** A buyer waiting three days on any platform is a lost buyer.
- **Adding platforms instead of stock.** More places to sell the same forty items does not make forty one.
- **Treating per platform reporting thresholds as a way to stay invisible.** Trading is trading; the bookkeeping guide explains what actually decides whether tax is due.
