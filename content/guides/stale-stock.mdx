This is for resellers on eBay and other UK platforms whose money is sitting on shelves. After reading it you will have a definition of stale that fits your stock, a way to measure sell-through so you can see the problem early, a markdown ladder to run without thinking, the alternatives to markdowns (bundling, another platform, liquidation), and enough of the accounting to record a write-down without getting it wrong. Figures link to the official page they came from; they change, so check the page. None of this is tax, legal or financial advice, and the accounting section is a description of the rules, not a recommendation for your return.

## What stale means

Stale is not old. A rare book can sit for a year and sell at full price; a phone case is stale in six weeks. Stale is stock that has been listed longer than its category takes to sell, at a price the comps support, with a listing that is not broken. All three conditions matter: if the price is above the comps or the listing has no photos, the stock is not stale, it is mislisted, and the fix is different.

Define it with a number per category, not one number for the shop. The way to get the number is sell-through.

### Sell-through rate

Sell-through is the number sold divided by the number listed over a period. Across a category it tells you how fast the market clears; on your own stock it tells you how fast you clear. eBay's [Research tab](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/product-research?id=4853) gives a sell-through rate for any search covering 90 days or less, alongside average price and the number of sellers. Run it on the item type before you buy and again when you are deciding whether a listing is stale.

Turn it into days. If 90 day sell-through for the item type is 60%, six in ten listings sold within the quarter; a listing of yours at the middle price that has gone 90 days without a sale is in the four that did not, and is stale. If sell-through is 20%, 90 days is nothing and your threshold is longer. If it is 90%, a listing past 30 days is already telling you something.

For your own stock, calculate it monthly from Seller Hub: units sold in the last 90 days divided by units listed at the start of the period. Do it per category. A shop-wide figure hides the category that is dragging.

### A working definition

For most general resale stock, sellers we know use days listed against category sell-through and end up with something like this. Adjust the days to your categories.

| Category sell-through (90 days) | Stale after | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above 70% | 45 days | The market clears fast; if yours has not, the price or the listing is wrong, or demand has moved. |
| 40% to 70% | 90 days | Typical for most resale categories. One full quarter is a fair test. |
| 15% to 40% | 150 days | Slow categories (collectables, parts, specialist). Give it a season. |
| Below 15% | 270 days, and only buy it at a price that survives the wait | Very slow. Stock like this should be bought cheaply or not at all. |

Whatever the number, write it on the category and check it monthly. The check is the whole system; the ladder below only works if it is triggered.

## The markdown ladder

A ladder is a set of price steps with dates, decided in advance and applied without a decision each time. It removes the two things that keep stale stock stale: hope, and the effort of deciding. Build it from the floor you worked out in [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps): the sale price at which, after fees, postage, packaging and returns, you still make the profit you need. Below the floor you are choosing between a loss now and a loss later, and that is a different decision covered under liquidation.

A ladder for a listing that hits its stale date.

| Step | When | What | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Stale date | Fix the listing first: title against the current top sold results, photos, item specifics, postage options. Check the comps again. | Half of "stale" stock is mispriced or badly listed. Only proceed if the listing is right and the price is at the middle comp. |
| 1 | Stale date | Send an offer to watchers at 5% to 7% off | Costs nothing, changes no public price, and lasts 96 hours. See [offers to watchers](/guides/offers-to-watchers). |
| 2 | Plus 7 days | Send at 10%, and turn Best Offer on if it was off, with auto-decline at the floor | Catches the buyers who wanted a proper discount. See [Best Offer strategy](/guides/best-offer-strategy). |
| 3 | Plus 14 days | Public price to the low quarter of the comps; if you have a Shop, a 14 day sale event with strikethrough rather than a plain price cut | The sale event needs 14 days of no changes first, which is why steps 1 and 2 must not touch the price. |
| 4 | Plus 30 days | Price at the floor. End and relist with a fresh listing rather than editing the old one, if the old one has no useful watchers left. | Fixed price listings renew monthly anyway, but a new listing with a new title gets a second look from search. |
| 5 | Plus 45 days | Off the ladder. Bundle it, move it, or liquidate. | Below the floor is not a markdown; it is a decision about the loss. |

Rules for the ladder. Never skip a step downward; buyers who see a price fall 30% in a week wait for 40%. Never step back up; it wastes the step. Do not markdown the category, markdown the listing; some items in a category are stale and some are not. And write the date of the next step in the listing's custom label field so that the weekly check is a filter, not a memory test.

For business sellers, re-read the floor at step 3: at the low quarter the per-order fee, label and packaging are a bigger share of the sale than they were at the middle.

## Bundling

Stale units rarely become unstale on their own. Grouped with something that does sell, they can. Three ways to bundle, in order of preference.

**Multi-buy and order discounts (Shop needed).** eBay's [Discounts Manager](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/promotions-manager?id=4094) lets Shop subscribers offer quantity discounts and spend thresholds. Set a "buy two, save" on a category where you have a stale colour or size next to a selling one, and let the buyer of the good one pick up the slow one at a price that covers its cost. This is the cleanest because the stale unit still sells at a stated price and the fee is on the discounted total.

**A single bundle listing.** List the stale item with a complementary selling item at a combined price a little under the two separately. The bundle gets its own title and appears in searches for both items. Photograph them together. Price the bundle so the stale unit contributes at least its cost after fees; the good item carries the profit.

**A job lot.** Group several stale units of a kind (a bag of phone cases, ten paperbacks of one author, a box of mixed cables) into one listing in the Wholesale and Job Lots category and price it to clear. Two warnings. On the current [business fee page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/fees-business-sellers?id=4809) Wholesale and Job Lots sits in the higher fee band, so the fee line on a job lot is worse than on the individual items. And a job lot sells to another reseller who wants a margin, so expect the low quarter of the comps divided by two, at best. It is a liquidation tool, not a markdown.

A bundle that is just the good item with the stale item free is a markdown of the good item wearing a disguise, and buyers spot it.

## Moving stock to another platform

The comps are not the same everywhere. A jacket stale at £28 on eBay may be a normal sale on Vinted, where there are no seller fees ([Vinted help](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/373-is-selling-on-vinted-free)) and the buyer pays a protection fee on top, so a lower list price nets the same. A bulky item stale because postage kills the total may sell in an evening on Facebook Marketplace for collection, where the buyer comes to you and there is no label to pay for; check Facebook's own help centre for its current terms on Marketplace sales in the UK before relying on any fee figure. Anything with a young buyer base may do better on Depop; anything specialist has its own forum or platform.

A method rather than a scatter.

1. Run the sold comp exercise on the other platform first. If the sold price there, after that platform's fees and postage, is no better than your eBay floor, moving it is admin for nothing.
2. Cross-list rather than move: keep the eBay listing live at its ladder price and list on the other platform too. The first sale wins and you end the other listing the same day. Oversell once and you learn to check messages before posting; oversell twice and you need a cross-listing tool or a rule that stale stock only lives in two places.
3. Use collection platforms for the bulky and the low value. The item that is stale on eBay because £6 of postage on a £9 item makes the total silly is a £7 collection sale that costs nothing to post.

Sales on other platforms count towards VAT turnover like any other, and every platform reports sellers to HMRC under the [digital platform reporting rules](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reporting-rules-for-digital-platforms), so record them.

## When to liquidate

Liquidation is selling below the floor to get the cash and the shelf back. It is the right call more often than resellers admit, because holding has a cost that appears on no invoice: money tied up is not buying stock that sells.

Liquidate when any of these is true.

- The item has finished the ladder and the floor price got no sale in 45 days.
- Category sell-through has fallen since you bought (a new model, a fad over, a season passed) and the comps now sit below your floor with no reason to recover.
- The item has a date: a season, an expiry, a licensing tie-in, a school year.
- The space is needed for stock that sells, or the cash is.

Ways to liquidate, cheapest first.

| Method | What you get | Cost and risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low start auction, seven days, ending Sunday evening | Whatever two bidders pay | Cannot be ended in the last 12 hours once bid on; ending earlier with bids costs a fee ([ending a listing](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/cancelling-listing?id=4146)). A reserve is a paid upgrade; check the current fee on the [reserve prices page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/reserve-prices?id=4143). Fees on the sale as normal. |
| Job lot to another reseller | Roughly half the low quarter, in one transaction | Higher fee band; buyer wants a margin. Fast and final. |
| Sell to a local trader, boot sale or clearance buyer | Pennies in the pound, cash | No fees, no postage, no returns. Records still needed. |
| Donate | Nothing back, but the space | Get a receipt for your records. |
| Dispose | Nothing | Only for stock with no market at any price. Keep a note of what and when. |

The auction is the honest one. It tells you, in seven days and in public, what the item is worth, and the answer is often more than you feared. Start it at the price you would take to be rid of it, not at 99p, unless the comps show 99p auctions reliably reach the low quarter in that category.

Everything on the liquidation list was bought at a price that assumed the middle comp and a normal sell-through. Read the list before your next sourcing trip; it is the best sourcing guide you own.

## The accounting of writing stock down

Brief, because it depends on how you keep your books, and because it is a job for whoever prepares your return. This is the shape of the rules with links; it is not advice.

**If you use the cash basis.** GOV.UK describes the cash basis as the standard way for sole traders and partnerships without corporate partners to record income and expenses ([cash basis](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-cash-basis)), and HMRC's helpsheet [HS222](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/how-to-calculate-your-taxable-profits-hs222-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs222-how-to-calculate-your-taxable-profits-2024) lists "amount paid for goods bought for resale" as the expense under it. Stock is an expense when you pay for it, there is no year-end stock valuation, and so there is no separate write-down: the cost went through when you bought it, and when the stock finally sells for less, the smaller sale is simply smaller income. The consequence is that a stale shelf does not show up in your accounts at all, which is why cash basis sellers with a lot of stock can be profitable on the tax return and out of cash in the bank. Keep your own stock list anyway.

**If you use traditional (accruals) accounting, or you are a limited company.** Closing stock is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value ([BIM33115](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim33115)), and net realisable value is the estimated selling price less the costs to complete and sell ([BIM33140](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim33140)), on a normal commercial basis rather than a forced sale. If a unit cost you £12 and the realistic sale after fees and postage is £7, it goes in the year-end stock figure at £7, and the £5 reduces that year's profit. That is the write-down. Two things HMRC's manual says that sellers get wrong: slow moving stock is not by itself a reason to write down below cost, because slow stock can still sell at a profit ([BIM33150](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim33150)); and an age-based formula (a growing percentage off cost as stock ages) is acceptable only if it reflects a realistic appraisal of what the stock will actually fetch, reviewed each year ([BIM33145](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim33145)). Price changes that happen between the year end and the accounts being finalised can be taken into account.

**What to keep.** A stock list at the year end with cost, your estimate of net realisable value and the reason for any reduction (comps, the ladder date, the platform it moved to), plus the disposal record for anything liquidated or donated. Records are kept for at least 5 years after the 31 January deadline of the tax year they relate to ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/how-long-to-keep-your-records)). Your ladder log, if you keep one, is most of the evidence.

**VAT.** If you are registered, a write-down does not change the VAT already reclaimed on the purchase, and VAT is due on the sale at whatever price the item eventually fetches under your scheme. Anything more specific than that is a question for your accountant.

## What goes wrong

- **One stale threshold for the whole shop.** Fast categories rot and slow categories get panic-sold.
- **Marking down without fixing the listing.** A bad title at 20% off is a bad title.
- **Cutting the price and sending offers in the same week.** The offer discount comes off the new price and the sale event clock resets.
- **Skipping rungs.** A 30% cut teaches watchers to wait for 40%.
- **Bundling stale with new at the new item's price.** Buyers see the stale unit as free and value the bundle accordingly.
- **Moving to another platform without running its comps.** Same price, different fees, same shelf.
- **Holding because the buy price was high.** The buy price is gone. Only the sale price and the shelf space are still yours to decide.
- **Writing down slow stock just because it is slow.** HMRC's manual says slow is not the test; realisable value is.
- **No stock list on the cash basis.** The tax return does not need one; your business does.
