This is for people who sell on eBay, Vinted, Amazon or anywhere else and need stock, week after week, at a price that leaves profit. It covers the five places most UK stock comes from, how to size up a source you have not used before, the arithmetic to do before buying, and the legal side that nobody explains until it goes wrong. None of it is legal or tax advice; it is how the work is done.

## The five sources

Each has a price, a speed and a catch. The mistake is to pick one and stay there. The sellers we know who last use two or three, and know exactly which one is for which kind of stock.

### Car boot sales

**What it is.** Households clearing out, priced to leave. Early morning, cash, no receipts unless you ask.

**What it costs.** Entry for buyers is usually a pound or two. Stock is as cheap as stock gets, but you pay in time and fuel and there is no guarantee of finding anything.

**What we would do.** Treat it as a training ground and a source for small, light, high margin things: media, collectables, branded clothing, small electricals you can test. Go at opening, walk the whole field once fast before buying anything, and carry a phone with signal so you can check sold prices on the spot. Ask for a receipt on anything over a few pounds; a handwritten note with the date, item, price and the seller's name is enough and most people will oblige.

**The catch.** No comeback. What you buy is yours. Car boots are also where counterfeit goods and unsafe electricals turn up most, and the law on that (below) does not care where you bought it.

### Charity shops

**What it is.** Donated goods, sorted and priced by volunteers, sometimes with a manager who knows values, often not.

**What it costs.** More than a car boot, less than retail. Prices vary a lot between chains and between branches of the same chain.

**What we would do.** Build a route of five to ten shops you can cover in a morning and visit on the same day each week, because stock goes out on a rhythm. Learn what each branch is good for; one will be strong on books, another on branded clothing. Be polite and unhurried; the people behind the counter decide what goes on the shelf and when. Keep every till receipt: it is dated, it names the shop, and it is the proof of purchase you will want later.

**The catch.** Charity shops increasingly check values online and price accordingly, so the easy margin on obvious brands has gone. The money is in things the volunteer did not recognise.

### Auctions, including online auction houses

**What it is.** General sale rooms, specialist sales, house clearance lots and, increasingly, auction houses that run entirely online with collection or delivery.

**What it costs.** The hammer price is not the price. You also pay a buyer's premium, a percentage of the hammer price set by the auction house, and VAT is normally charged on that premium even when the lot itself has no VAT. Some online platforms add a further fee for bidding through them. None of this is fixed by law; it is in each auction house's terms, so read them before you bid, and work out your maximum bid from the all in cost, not the hammer.

**What we would do.** Online auction houses are the best source most small resellers have not tried. Search for sales in your region, register once, and watch a few sales without bidding to learn the rhythm and the real prices. Bid on mixed lots and job lots where the value is in the volume and the sorting; that is where the work is, and where the margin is. Always inspect in person or ask for extra photographs, and check the collection window: uncollected lots attract storage charges and eventually resale.

**The catch.** Auction lots are usually sold as seen with no returns, and the premium plus VAT can add a quarter or more to your cost. A lot that looks cheap at the hammer price is often not cheap at the door.

### Retail clearance

**What it is.** Supermarkets, high street chains and their websites marking down end of line, seasonal and damaged box stock, sometimes to a fraction of the ticket price.

**What it costs.** Retail prices, reduced. The lowest cost per unit of any source is not here, but the stock is new, boxed, barcoded and comes with a proper receipt.

**What we would do.** This is the natural feeder for Amazon and for multi quantity eBay listings, because the products already exist on those platforms with sales history. Buy after you have checked the sold price and the number of other sellers, not before. Buy online where you can: a returns window covers you if you got the numbers wrong, and the order confirmation is an invoice with your name and address on it, which matters for brand approvals and for anyone who later questions where the stock came from.

**The catch.** Everyone with a barcode scanner app sees the same clearance. The margin can vanish within days as other resellers list the same product. Never buy deep on the strength of today's price.

### Wholesale and liquidation lots

**What it is.** Buying in bulk from a distributor, a brand's clearance department or a liquidator handling returns, overstock and insolvency stock, usually by the pallet or the boxed lot.

**What it costs.** Low per unit, high per order. Minimum spend, delivery on a pallet, and often VAT on top of the quoted price.

**What we would do.** Not first. Wholesale rewards people who already know what sells and how fast, because you are committing hundreds or thousands of pounds to one decision. Start with small mixed lots from a liquidator, sort and list every item, and record what each one actually made. Do that three times before you consider a pallet. Ask for a manifest before paying and treat "customer returns" as meaning "a share of this is broken", because it does.

**The catch.** Unmanifested lots, grades that mean nothing, and suppliers who sell the same pallets to a hundred other resellers who then all list the same thing in the same week.

## How to evaluate a source

Before you spend a full day on a new source, spend an hour on these six questions.

1. **Can you see sold prices for what it sells?** If the goods do not exist on eBay's sold listings or Amazon's catalogue, you cannot price them and you should not buy them yet.
2. **What is the real cost per item after everything?** Entry, fuel, premium, VAT, delivery, your time.
3. **Is there any comeback?** Retail and online purchases: yes. Auctions, car boots, most liquidators: no. Sources with no comeback need a bigger margin to cover mistakes.
4. **Does it give you paper?** A receipt or invoice with a date, the seller's name and the item. Sources that will not give paper are sources you should buy less from.
5. **Is it repeatable?** A one off house clearance is fun; a charity shop route you can drive every Tuesday is a business.
6. **What is the competition?** If ten other people are scanning the same shelf, the price has already moved.

A source that fails on paper and comeback is not off limits, but it is for small, cheap, easily checked items only.

## The numbers to run before you buy

Do this for the first fifty things you consider buying, in writing, and after that you will do it in your head. Nothing here is complicated; the point is to do it every time.

### Sell-through

Sell-through is how many of an item sold against how many were listed over a period. eBay's [product research tool](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/news/2024-october/product-research-tool) in Seller Hub shows it for the last 90 days along with the average sold price, the range and how many sellers there are. It is free once you have made one sale. As a working rule, an item where most listings sell within 90 days is a fast item; one where a small fraction sells is slow, and slow stock is where cash goes to die. Also look at how many are currently listed: fifty active listings and five sales a month means you are joining a queue.

For Amazon, the equivalent is the sales rank and price history; the [FBA guide](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) covers that in detail.

### Margin after fees and postage

Work from the price you can actually get, which is the middle of the recent sold range, not the top. Then take off everything.

Here is the shape of it for a business seller on eBay. Assume a jacket that sells for £30 with the buyer paying £3.50 postage, a price you have looked up on the Royal Mail page for the size and weight, and that eBay's Clothes, Shoes and Accessories fee is 11.9% plus 40p per order plus the 0.35% regulatory operating fee.

- Total sale including postage: £33.50
- eBay final value fee: 11.9% of £33.50 = £3.99
- Per order fee: £0.40
- Regulatory operating fee: 0.35% of £33.50 = £0.12
- Postage you pay: £3.50
- Packaging: say £0.40
- What is left before the cost of the item: £33.50 minus £8.41 = £25.09

If you paid £5 for the jacket, you keep about £20 before tax, before your time, and before any return. If you paid £15, you keep about £10, and one return in five wipes out most of that. The percentages in the example are eBay's own for that category at the time of writing; check the [fees page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/fees-business-sellers?id=4809) for the category you are in, because they differ. If you are not VAT registered, remember that VAT is charged on the platform's fees and you cannot reclaim it.

Two rules of thumb the sellers we know use: the sale price should be at least three times what you paid for anything under about £20, and you should never buy something you would not be content to sell at the bottom of the sold range.

### Time

Add your time. A £3 profit on an item that takes twenty minutes to photograph, list and post is a wage below the legal minimum. Sourcing cheap is easy; sourcing cheap things that are worth listing is the skill.

## The legal side

You are a trader as soon as you buy things to sell them. That is the test HMRC uses and it is the test Trading Standards use, and it brings obligations with it whether or not you have registered anything.

### Receipts and proof of purchase

Keep a receipt or invoice for every purchase, however small, and keep them for years. They do three jobs. They are your expense records for tax, and HMRC expects you to [keep records](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records) of everything you buy for the business. They are your evidence that stock was legitimately sourced if a platform or a brand challenges you. And they are the beginning of your defence if anything you sold turns out to be counterfeit or unsafe. A dated photograph of a handwritten car boot note is better than nothing; a shop receipt is better; an invoice with your name and address on it is best.

### Counterfeits and Trading Standards

Selling counterfeit goods in the course of a business is a criminal offence, and the Intellectual Property Office's [guidance for businesses](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses) puts the maximum at an unlimited fine or up to 10 years in prison. Trading Standards is the lead agency that enforces it. You do not have to have known; the defence is that you believed on reasonable grounds that the goods were genuine, and "reasonable grounds" is where your receipts, your supplier checks and your common sense come in. The Trading Standards guidance on [car boot sales](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/car-boot-sales-s) specifically warns about branded clothing, media and games bought from those sources.

Practical rules: do not buy branded goods from a source that cannot tell you where they came from; learn what genuine packaging, labels and stitching look like for the brands you handle; if the price is a tenth of retail for a current line, it is not a bargain, it is a fake; and if you find you have bought one, do not list it. Trading Standards can be reached through [Citizens Advice](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses) if you want to report the seller.

### Safety

Everything you sell as a trader must be safe. Second-hand electricals must have a correctly fused, approved plug and must not be damaged; the Trading Standards guidance on [second-hand electrical goods](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/product-safety/second-hand-electrical-goods) describes the checks, and recommends checking products against the recall lists. Toys, nightwear and anything for children carry their own rules. The rules are for Great Britain; Northern Ireland follows the EU regime, so check if you are there. If a product needs a safety mark and you cannot see one, or an electrical item shows any damage, do not buy it for resale.

### Consumer rights

As a business seller at a distance (which means every online sale), your buyer has [14 days after delivery](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) to cancel for any reason and get a refund, and you must tell them so before they buy. If goods are faulty or not as described, they have 30 days to reject them for a full refund, and "sold as seen" or "no returns" in a listing does not remove that. Build this into your margin: some of what you sell will come back.

## Building repeatable sources

Stock you found once is luck. Stock you can find every week is a business. The difference is deliberate.

**Keep a source log.** For every trip or order: where, date, spend, number of items, and later, what those items made. After a couple of months it shows you which sources pay and which just feel productive. Sellers we know are regularly surprised by the answer.

**Keep a buy list.** Things you have sold before, with the price you paid and the price you got. Check it before every trip. A product you have sold three times is worth more to you than a product you have never handled, because you know the listing, the postage and the speed.

**Turn up on a schedule.** Charity shops put out new stock on set days. Auction houses hold general sales on set weeks. Clearance happens at predictable points in the retail calendar. Learn the rhythm and be there when the stock is.

**Be a good customer.** The manager who puts a bag aside for you, the auction house that emails you before a sale, the liquidator who offers you the manifest first: none of that comes from being clever. It comes from paying promptly, collecting on time and being pleasant.

**Diversify on purpose.** Two or three sources of different types, so a bad month at one does not empty the shelves. And within each source, several categories, so a price collapse in one does not take the business with it.

**Reinvest before you spend.** The money from this week's sales buys next week's stock. Sellers who take profit out before they have a reliable stock pipeline end up with an empty shop and a full wallet, for a while.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying because it is cheap, not because it sells.** The most common way to end up with a garage full of stock. The sold price and the sell-through come before the purchase, every time.
- **Working the margin on the top sold price.** You will get the middle, and sometimes the bottom.
- **Forgetting the premium and VAT at auction.** A £100 hammer is often £125 or more at the desk.
- **No receipts.** Fine until a platform, a brand or HMRC asks, at which point every item without paper is a problem.
- **Branded goods from unbranded sources.** A car boot trader with forty pairs of the same trainers is not a stroke of luck.
- **Untested electricals.** A £2 profit is not worth a fire.
- **Going deep on one product from clearance.** Everyone else saw it too.
- **Wholesale before you know what sells.** A pallet is a hundred decisions made at once. Make them one at a time first.
- **Ignoring your time.** Cheap stock that takes an hour an item to shift is not cheap.
