Most guides to Amazon FBA are written to be read once. This one is written to be used. It is organised the way the work is organised: what the numbers are, what you have to decide, what to do in which order, and what to do when something breaks. Keep it open while you set up and come back to the troubleshooting table when Amazon sends you an email you do not understand.

It is written with an Amazon seller who has been selling on the platform for years, so the process is the one that actually happens, not the one in the brochure. It is for the UK, for people who want to buy stock and sell it through Amazon's warehouses at a profit, and it assumes you have never done it. Figures change, so the ones that matter link to the official page. None of this is tax, legal or financial advice; it is how the work is done.

## Twelve decisions that decide whether you make money

Everything in this business comes back to a small number of choices. Get these right and the rest is admin. For each one: what the choice is, what we would do, and what it costs to get wrong.

### 1. Is this actually for you

**The choice.** FBA is a stock business. You spend money now and get it back, with a margin, weeks later. It rewards patience, record keeping and reading numbers, and it punishes impulse buying and hoping.

**What we would do.** Be honest about three things before opening any account: time (a few hours a week, mostly evenings, for at least six months), money (a pot you will not need back for six months), and temperament (can you follow a checklist you find boring?). If any of those is a no, wait until it is a yes.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** A few hundred pounds in subscriptions and a box of stock you do not want.

### 2. Sole trader or limited company

**The choice.** You can sell as an individual registered with HMRC, or through a company registered at Companies House.

**What we would do.** Talk to an accountant for an hour before deciding; the right answer depends on your other income and your plans. Sole trader is cheaper and simpler and fine to start. A company is more admin (annual accounts, confirmation statement) but separates the business legally, and some suppliers and credit products only deal with companies. If you form a company, the SIC code that fits is 47910, retail sale via mail order houses or via the internet.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Small if you switch early, painful if you switch after a year of trading history, brand approvals and supplier accounts tied to the old identity.

Whichever you choose: the name and address you give Amazon, your bank and your suppliers must be identical, character for character. More appeals and approvals fail on a mismatch than on anything else.

### 3. How much to start with

**The choice.** How big a pot to put in.

**What we would do.** Work it out rather than pick a number. Fixed costs come first and run whether you sell or not: the selling plan, a price history tool, a sourcing calculator, and later a repricer and a profit tracker. Budget £60 to £120 a month for those depending on what you take. Everything else is stock, and only stock earns. A pot that is mostly fixed costs cannot grow; a pot that lets you hold several products across a couple of buying cycles can. Then start smaller than the pot: buy modest quantities, sell them, and put the money back in. Most people who quote a starting figure land somewhere in the low thousands for exactly this reason.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Too little and one bad product stalls you. Too much, spent too fast, and you learn expensive lessons at scale.

### 4. Which way to source

**The choice.** Online arbitrage (buying from retailers' websites when their price is under Amazon's), retail arbitrage (the same in physical shops), wholesale (bulk from distributors) or private label (your own branded product).

**What we would do.** Online arbitrage, without hesitation, and here is the whole argument in one line: if you get a buy wrong, you can usually send it back. Retail arbitrage is a fine way to learn what listings do, but it does not scale and a till receipt is thin evidence when Amazon asks where a product came from. Wholesale wants trading history, minimum orders and VAT registration you do not have, and puts you on listings full of people with the same supplier. Private label asks for thousands of pounds, packaging, photography and an advertising budget before you understand the platform, against listings with thousands of reviews. Both can work. Neither is where you learn.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** This is the decision that ends most new sellers, usually by tying up their whole pot in a single order of something they cannot return.

### 5. Prep at home or use a prep centre

**The choice.** Stock has to be labelled and boxed to Amazon's standard before it goes in. You do it, or you pay a prep centre to receive, label and forward it.

**What we would do.** Home, to start, if you have a spare corner and a couple of hours a week. It keeps money in stock and teaches you exactly what Amazon expects, which matters when a shipment gets refused. Move to a prep centre when your time is worth more than the per unit fee. Two catches with prep centres: the fee comes straight off thin margins, and some retailers will only deliver to the billing address on your account, so a prep centre address can cut off suppliers.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Small either way. This one is reversible.

### 6. Which tools to pay for

**The choice.** There are dozens. You need types, not brands.

**What we would do.** Two from day one: a price history tool (shows a product's price, sales rank and seller count over time, so you can see whether it really sells and whether the price is stable) and a sourcing calculator (takes the buy price and shows net profit after every Amazon fee; Amazon's own [FBA calculator](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) does this free, the paid ones are faster). Add a repricer once you have more than about ten products, and a profit tracker when you can no longer keep the numbers in a spreadsheet.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Subscriptions you do not use are the classic slow leak. Review them monthly.

### 7. Accountant now or later

**The choice.** Pay for one from the first month, or keep your own records and bring one in as you grow.

**What we would do.** Either is fine. Doing it yourself only works if you keep every invoice and a simple list of every purchase from the first day, so it can all be reconciled later. Bring an accountant in by the time turnover is heading for £80,000, because VAT registration is a job for someone who has done it before.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Doing it yourself without records: a miserable week of backdating and possibly a missed VAT reclaim.

### 8. Which selling plan

**The choice.** Individual (per item, no monthly fee) or Professional (monthly fee).

**What we would do.** Professional. The crossover is about 34 items a month, and the Individual plan lacks things you will want. Both pay referral fees and FBA fees on top.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Trivial. Switch in settings.

### 9. UK only, or Europe

**The choice.** Amazon can offer your UK stock to European customers and copy your listings to other marketplaces.

**What we would do.** UK only until you are established. Three settings before you list anything: turn off every country except the UK under FBA export settings; make sure Build International Listings is off; and apply to the dangerous goods programme (aerosols, perfume, nail polish, anything with a lithium battery, and a surprising list of ordinary items get flagged, and approval takes weeks).

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Selling into Europe at a loss without noticing, intellectual property complaints from countries you never chose, and VAT questions you are not ready for.

### 10. Which brands and categories to attempt

**The choice.** Many brands and some categories are gated: Amazon will not let a new account sell them without approval, and approval needs invoices.

**What we would do.** Check whether you can sell a product before you pay for it (Seller Central tells you at the point of adding it). Start with brands that ask for small quantities on the approval invoice. Expect refusals from a new account, often automated; treat them as the normal path. Get the paperwork exact, apply again, ask for a human review. After a few months of clean selling, approvals get easier and some arrive without applying.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Stock you cannot list, which is the most expensive way to store things.

### 11. Manual pricing or a repricer

**The choice.** Adjust prices by hand, or let software move them within limits you set.

**What we would do.** By hand while you have under about ten products; it takes minutes a day and you learn how listings move. A repricer after that. Whichever you use, every product gets a floor price so a price war cannot take you below profit.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** Manual too long: lost Buy Box share. Repricer without a floor: selling at a loss on autopilot.

### 12. Your own money or credit

**The choice.** Fund stock from your pot only, or use a business credit card whose payment date falls after Amazon pays you.

**What we would do.** Your own money until you understand your cash cycle: how long from buying stock to the money landing back in your bank. Only then does a credit card with a sensible payment date become a tool rather than a trap. Never high interest loans, including the ones offered inside Seller Central; the margins in this business do not cover the interest.

**Cost of getting it wrong.** The whole business. Debt against stock that has not sold is how people end up out of pocket and out of the game.

## Your first thirty days

A plan, not a promise. Move faster if you can; do not skip steps.

**Week one: registrations.** Decide sole trader or company and register. Open a separate business bank account. Create the Amazon seller account on the Professional plan using an email you will keep forever, turn on two step verification, and complete identity verification (a passport or driving licence, proof of address dated within the last 90 days, and possibly a short video call to show your documents; details on [Amazon's verification page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/verification-compliance)). Add your bank as the deposit method and a proper online debit card as the charge method, assigned to the UK marketplace. Change the three settings from decision 9. Know that the business address you give will be shown on your public storefront; if that is your home, decide now whether you want a virtual office or registered office address instead, because changing it later means re-verifying.

**Week two: learn to read.** Do not buy anything. Install your price history tool and calculator. Pick twenty products you can see on retailers' sites and run every one through the fee calculation by hand: buy price, delivery, referral fee, FBA fee, what is left. Look at each product's history: does it sell, is the price stable, how many sellers, is Amazon itself on the listing. Twenty of these and you will start to see it before the tool finishes loading.

**Week three: first buys.** Buy small quantities of two or three products whose numbers you have checked twice, whose brand you have confirmed you can sell, and whose listing you have matched exactly (pack size, variant, colour). Keep the invoices. Set up a separate Amazon buying account for purchases from Amazon itself, on a different email and phone number from your Seller Central login, so a problem with one can never lock the other.

**Week four: first shipment.** Add each product to your inventory (attach your offer to the existing listing, condition New, FBA, your price, honest answers to the battery and dangerous goods questions, Amazon's barcodes). Wait a few minutes, then create one shipment for the lot: quantities, SKU labels on each unit, how they are boxed, box labels on the outside, a "heavy" sticker on each side of any box over 15kg, and a partnered carrier. Book a collection rather than driving to a drop off. Then set up a second user on your account (a real, trusted person with their own email and phone, verified through Amazon) so a lost login is never a lost business.

By the end of the month your first stock is on its way in and you have a system. That is the goal. Profit comes after.

## The buy

The buy is the skill. By the time you have paid for a product, your profit is more or less decided; the selling side largely takes care of itself if the buy was right, and nothing later fixes a bad one. So this is where the effort goes.

### The only question

Will this product sell, at a price that leaves profit after every cost, before its price falls? Everything else is detail.

### The signals, and what they mean

- **Sales rank** tells you how fast the product sells relative to its category. It is not comparable across categories; learn what "a few a day" looks like in the ones you work.
- **Price history** with sudden drops means someone dumped stock. Assume it happens again while you hold yours.
- **Seller count** decides your share. Ten sellers on a listing that sells three a day is a queue.
- **Amazon on the listing** as a seller is very hard to beat on price or for the Buy Box.
- **Reviews and return rate** tell you whether the product disappoints people. Returns come off your margin.
- **Your own history.** A product you have sold before is the best product in the world: you know the listing, the competition and the speed. Keep a list and check it first every time.

### Net profit, properly

Net is what is left after: what you paid, delivery to you, the referral fee (a percentage of the selling price), the FBA fulfilment fee (by size and weight), storage for however long it sits, prep if you pay for it, and VAT if you are registered. Run it in the calculator, and until you trust the calculator, run it by hand as well. A return of around 20% on your money in your first month is a good result; it is a real profit from selling on Amazon, and the people who last are the ones who build on that rather than chase the screenshots.

### Before you pay, every time

1. The listing is this exact product: same pack count, size, variant.
2. You are allowed to sell the brand and category.
3. The numbers work with delivery included and at a price you could actually get, not the highest price on the chart.
4. The product is not restricted, hazardous without approval, or expiry dated in a way that makes it unsellable by the time it lands.
5. You can return it if you are wrong.
6. The invoice will show your business name and billing address exactly as Amazon has them, and not a prep centre's address.

### Paperwork is part of the buy

An invoice is your proof that stock is genuine and legitimately sourced. Amazon asks for it when approving brands and when someone complains; HMRC asks for it at VAT time. It must carry your business name and your billing address, from a legitimate supplier, with the quantity and the product identifiable. Shop receipts are weak. A wrong address makes a genuine product indefensible. Keep every one, scanned, from the first purchase.

## The weekly routine

**Every day, ten minutes.** Dashboard: sales, pending orders, buyer messages (reply within 24 hours), account health notifications. Withdraw any available balance to your bank.

**Every week, an hour.** Reprice by hand (or check the repricer's floors). Look for stranded stock and fix or remove it. Check for business customer orders needing an invoice within 48 hours of payment. Review what sold and what did not, and update your list of replenishable products. Source.

**Every month.** Log every expense into your profit tracker or spreadsheet so your net figure is true. Review subscriptions. Reconcile the bank account. Check turnover against the VAT threshold and the insurance threshold. Clear unfulfillable stock with a removal order. Look at your Inventory Performance Index and at anything Amazon has flagged as excess or aged.

## The money

### How cash moves

You pay for stock today. It arrives in a week, goes to Amazon in another, checks in, sells over the following weeks. When a customer pays, Amazon holds the money in deferred transactions for around a week to cover returns, then includes it in your next payout, and your bank takes a few days more. Sale to bank is commonly about two weeks; purchase to bank is often six or more. That gap is where all the pressure in this business lives, and it is why people who are profitable on paper still cannot buy anything.

Three rules follow. Buy things that sell in weeks, not months. Withdraw daily, because money inside Amazon cannot buy stock and can be held if your account is reviewed. Plan purchases around the payout calendar, not around what is on offer today.

### Gross is a mood, net is a number

Profit trackers show a large figure for profit on sales. It is gross. Net is after referral fees, FBA fees, delivery, prep, subscriptions, advertising and everything else. Log expenses monthly or the big number lies to you. A small loss in the first month or two is normal because subscriptions start before sales do; the aim is for net profit to pass overheads by month three, and from then on the surplus goes back into stock.

### Tax and reporting

- **Tell HMRC.** As a sole trader, register for Self Assessment once your sales pass £1,000 in a tax year, by the following 5 October. Companies file with Companies House and HMRC on their own timetable.
- **Amazon reports you.** Marketplaces send HMRC details of sellers with 30 or more sales or about £1,700 in a year. Being reported is not owing tax, but silence was never an option.
- **Making Tax Digital** for Income Tax applies to sole traders over £50,000 from April 2026: quarterly digital updates. If you are heading there, get software before you need it.
- **VAT.** UK established businesses register once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling 12 months, within 30 days of the end of the month you went over. Turnover is sales, not profit. Prepare from about £80,000: accounting software that pulls Amazon data, an accountant, every invoice to hand, because VAT on stock bought before registration can often be reclaimed if the invoices are right. Once registered, set your sourcing calculator to standard VAT so your margins are real.
- **A correction to something you will read elsewhere.** Using FBA does not, by itself, force a UK business to register for VAT early. The threshold applies. It is businesses established outside the UK that must register from their first UK sale.

### Insurance

Amazon's UK agreement requires product liability insurance once your gross sales exceed £4,000 a month for three consecutive months: at least £400,000 of cover, naming Amazon and its assignees as additional insured, with the certificate available on request. Get a quote before you cross the line.

### Credit and cashback

Covered in decision 12: own money first, then a business card with a payment date after Amazon's payout, never high interest loans. If you buy stock online, a cashback site adds a little to every order for nothing; record it as income.

## Staying on the platform

Amazon can stop you selling faster than any competitor can. Account health is the backbone of the business, and it is mostly paperwork and habits.

**Account health has three parts.** Customer service (order defect rate, which Amazon wants under 1%: negative feedback, claims, chargebacks), policy compliance (intellectual property complaints, authenticity complaints, restricted products, listing violations), and delivery performance (Amazon's problem when you use FBA). Policy compliance is what ends arbitrage sellers.

**Intellectual property and authenticity complaints.** You can buy a genuine product from a real shop and still get one, because brands police distribution, not just fakes. When one lands: stop selling the item, gather the invoice, respond clearly with what happened and what you have changed, and if the answer is a template, ask for a human review and keep asking. Your case is only as strong as your invoice.

**Messages and feedback.** Reply to every buyer message within 24 hours. Ask for seller feedback politely and within the rules, never with incentives. Feedback about delivery or an Amazon handled return can be removed through Feedback Manager on the grounds it relates to fulfilment Amazon controlled.

**One account.** Never open a second seller account to get round a problem. Amazon links accounts well and it is the fastest route to a permanent ban.

**Two logins, kept apart.** Your Seller Central login is for selling. Buy stock, including from Amazon, on a separate buying account with a different email and phone. Add a verified second user to Seller Central for the day your main login is locked.

**Dangerous goods.** Apply early. A flagged product cannot ship until it is classified, and classification takes weeks.

**Business customers.** Some buyers order through Amazon Business and are owed an invoice from you within 48 hours of payment settling. Once you are VAT registered and have added your number, Amazon can generate them; before that, you do it.

## What most guides leave out

Each of these has cost someone real money. None of them is hard once you know.

**Amazon charges VAT on its own fees.** Since 1 August 2024 every Amazon fee (referral, FBA, storage, subscription) carries 20% VAT. If you are VAT registered you reclaim it on your return. If you are not, it is simply a cost, which means the fee you see on the rate card is a fifth higher for you. Put it in your calculator from the first product or every margin you work out will be wrong in the same direction.

**Your address is public.** Amazon shows a business address on your storefront. For a company that is the registered office; for a sole trader it is whatever you gave. If you do not want your home address next to your name on the internet, use a virtual office or registered office address from the start.

**Every unit needs an Amazon barcode label.** Amazon ended "commingled" stock on 31 March 2026. Resellers must label every unit with an Amazon barcode (an FNSKU) before it goes in; units arriving on the manufacturer's barcode alone can be refused at the dock or held for relabelling at your expense. It is the "use Amazon barcodes" choice when you add a product, and it also means a buyer can never receive someone else's counterfeit instead of your genuine unit.

**Some brands need a code on every unit.** Brands enrolled in Amazon's Transparency programme put a unique serialised code on each item, and Amazon will not let you list or replenish those products without a valid code for every unit. Retailer stock does not come with them. If a listing shows Transparency, it is not a product for arbitrage.

**The fair pricing rule can switch your listing off.** If your price is well above the same item elsewhere (including on Amazon's other stores or on retailers' sites), Amazon can suppress the offer without warning. The related rule on packs: a multipack must never cost more per unit than the single. And you cannot make your own multipack of a brand's product; a pack needs the brand's own barcode or the brand's written permission.

**Dated and meltable stock has rules.** Anything with a best before or use by date must arrive with enough shelf life to be consumed plus a further 90 days (Amazon publishes longer minimums for some product types), or it is refused, and Amazon disposes of it at 50 days out. Chocolate and other meltable goods are not accepted for FBA in the summer months; check the current dates before buying seasonal confectionery in spring.

**Returns cost more than the refund.** When a customer returns an item, Amazon gives you back the referral fee minus an administration fee (the lesser of £5 or 20% of the referral fee), keeps the fulfilment fee, and a returned item that has been opened comes back to you as unsellable. Products with high return rates are not profitable even when the calculator says they are. Check the return rate before you buy, and factor a few percent of sales into your margin.

**When Amazon loses your stock, the clock is short.** Claims for stock lost or damaged in Amazon's warehouses must be filed within 60 days, and since 2025 they are paid at your cost price, not the selling price. Amazon reimburses some losses automatically, but check your inventory reports monthly and claim what it misses.

**Money is held after delivery, not after the sale.** Payment for an order becomes available about seven days after it is delivered. New accounts and accounts with problems can have larger reserves held. Plan buying around when money is available, not around when it was earned.

**Selling a fake is your problem even if you did not know.** Trading Standards can prosecute for selling counterfeit goods whether or not you knew, and the defence is showing you took reasonable steps: bought from legitimate suppliers, kept invoices, checked when something looked wrong. The paperwork you keep for Amazon is the same paperwork that protects you here. Toys, electricals and anything for children carry extra safety rules (UKCA marking, instructions in English, traceability); if a product needs a safety mark and you cannot see one, do not buy it.

**Some categories are gated at the category level.** Beyond brands, whole categories such as grocery, health and beauty, baby, jewellery, watches and some sports goods need approval before you can list anything in them, and a few things (medicines, most knives, alcohol, certain supplements) are restricted outright. Check the [categories that need approval](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/200333160) before you plan around them.

**Christmas starts in October.** Stock for December has to be in Amazon's warehouses by the second half of October to be reliably available, and from mid October to mid January Amazon adds peak fees to storage and fulfilment. If you want to sell in Q4, buy in September and ship early; if you do not, keep your storage lean through the expensive months.

**New sellers get free things.** Amazon's new seller incentives include automatic enrolment in FBA New Selection (free storage and returns for a period on products that are new to FBA) and other credits. Most are aimed at brands and advertising, but the storage and returns waivers apply to arbitrage stock too. Read what your account has been given before you assume you have to pay for everything.

**Condition means condition.** "New" means sealed, complete, in original packaging, with no retailer price stickers or security tags. Peel every label, remove every tag, and never send anything opened as new. One "not as described" complaint at low volume is enough to hurt your order defect rate.

## When things go wrong

| What you see | What it usually means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "You need approval to sell this brand" | The brand or category is gated | Check before buying next time. Apply with an invoice from a legitimate supplier for the required quantity, dated within the last 180 days, name and address matching exactly. Refused? Fix, reapply, request a human review. |
| Stranded inventory | Stock is in the warehouse but the listing cannot sell | Inventory, Manage Inventory, Stranded. Relist, fix the listing issue, or create a removal order. Keep it near zero. |
| Unfulfillable inventory | Returned or damaged units Amazon will not sell | Create a removal order: back to you or disposed of. Check your removal address first. |
| Intellectual property complaint | A brand objects to you selling its product | Stop selling it, gather the invoice, respond with facts and what changed, push for a human review. |
| Authenticity complaint | Amazon wants proof the item is genuine | Same as above; the invoice from a legitimate supplier with your details on it is the whole case. |
| Negative feedback about delivery | An Amazon fulfilment problem is showing against you | Feedback Manager, request removal, reason: relates to fulfilment Amazon controlled. |
| Order defect rate rising | Claims or negative feedback at low volume | At small volumes one issue spikes the rate. Resolve fast, reply fast, and stop selling anything with a return problem. |
| Shipment not received or units missing | Check in discrepancy | Wait for the shipment to fully close, then open a reconciliation request with your proof of quantities. |
| Payout lower than expected | Deferred transactions, refunds, fees | Payments, Deferred Transactions and Transaction View explain every line. |
| Storage limit or excess inventory warning | Your Inventory Performance Index is low for the quarter (Amazon does not publish a fixed pass mark) | Sell through or remove slow stock, fix stranded listings, buy less deep. |
| Listing suppressed | Missing information or a compliance hold | Check Manage Inventory for the reason; supply what is asked or remove the offer. |
| Insurance request from Amazon | You crossed the sales threshold | Buy the policy (minimum £400,000, Amazon named) and upload the certificate. |
| "Transparency code required" | The brand serialises every unit | You cannot sell this product from retailer stock. Return it to the retailer if you can; do not try to list it without codes. |
| Offer suppressed for pricing | Your price is well above the same item elsewhere, or a pack costs more per unit than the single | Lower the price, or remove the offer and wait for the market to move. Do not relist at the same price. |
| Shipment refused or held for labelling | Units arrived without an Amazon barcode, with retailer stickers, or in packaging that fails prep rules | Relabel to Amazon's standard (or pay Amazon's unplanned prep fee if offered), then resend. |
| Dated stock refused | Too little shelf life on arrival for Amazon's expiry rules | Sell it another way or return it. Check dates before buying next time. |
| Account deactivated | A policy or verification issue | Read the notice fully, respond only to what it asks, with evidence, and do not open another account. |

## Six months in

If you have stayed consistent, kept overheads tight and put every pound of profit back into stock, month six looks nothing like month one: more products, brands approved that once refused you, a list of things you have sold before and can buy again, cash flow you understand, and a weekly routine that runs without thought. First year for learning, second year for earning is a fair way to think about the pace.

The mistakes that end most beginners, in the order we see them:

1. Starting with private label or wholesale before understanding the platform.
2. Reading the gross figure as profit.
3. Going deep on one product and strangling cash flow.
4. Sloppy invoices: wrong name, wrong address, nothing kept.
5. Ignoring account health until the deactivation email.
6. A second account to get round a problem.
7. Taking money out before the business can spare it.
8. Leaving overseas settings on and selling into Europe at a loss.
9. Assuming HMRC will not notice, when Amazon has already told them.
10. Working margins without the 20% VAT on Amazon's fees, or without a returns allowance.
11. Buying seasonal or dated stock without checking the calendar.

## Glossary

- **ASIN**: Amazon's identifier for a product listing. As a reseller you attach your offer to an existing ASIN.
- **Buy Box (featured offer)**: the "Add to basket" button on a listing. Most sales go to whoever holds it.
- **Deferred transactions**: money from settled sales that Amazon holds briefly before paying out.
- **FNSKU**: the Amazon barcode label that ties a unit to your account. Required on every unit you send in.
- **FBA / FBM**: Fulfilment by Amazon (Amazon stores and ships) / Fulfilled by Merchant (you do).
- **Gated**: a brand or category that needs approval before you can sell it.
- **IPI**: Inventory Performance Index, Amazon's score for how well you manage stock. Low scores bring storage limits.
- **Online arbitrage / retail arbitrage**: buying from retailers online / in shops to resell on Amazon.
- **Prep**: labelling and packing stock to Amazon's requirements before it goes in.
- **Referral fee**: Amazon's percentage of each sale.
- **Replenishable**: a product you can buy again and again because you know it sells.
- **Repricer**: software that moves your price within limits you set.
- **SKU label**: the barcode you put on each unit so Amazon scans it into your inventory.
- **Transparency**: Amazon's serialised code scheme for brands; products in it cannot be sold without a code per unit.
- **Stranded**: stock in Amazon's warehouse that cannot be sold because of a listing problem.

## Questions

**Can I do this with a full time job?** Yes, and it is the sensible way. Source in the evening, prep at the weekend, keep the pressure off while you learn.

**How long until it pays?** A small loss for a month or two, costs covered by month three if you are consistent, and compounding after that. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

**Do I need a company?** No. Ask an accountant which structure suits you, then keep the details identical everywhere.

**What if I keep getting refused for brands?** Check the paperwork matches, apply again, ask for a human review. Refusal is the normal path for a new account, not the end of it.

**Where do I ask when I am stuck?** The [Amazon forums](/community/c/amazon) here, and [FBA and FBM](/community/c/amazon-fba-and-fbm) in particular. Say what you tried, what happened and the numbers.
