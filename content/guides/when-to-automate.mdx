This is for resellers who are past the first few hundred sales and can feel the admin growing faster than the profit. It will help you decide what to automate, in what order, and what to leave alone, so that the hours you save are real and the mistakes you avoid are the expensive ones. It describes types of tools and what they do, not brands; the right product for you depends on your platforms and your volume, and the forum is the place to compare.

## What automation is for

Automation does not make a reselling business more profitable by itself. It moves hours from repetitive work to work that only you can do: sourcing, grading, pricing decisions, and dealing with the buyer whose parcel has gone missing. If you automate the wrong thing you save an hour and lose a customer. If you automate the right thing you get an evening back every week and your error rate goes down, because software does not mistype a postcode at eleven at night.

So the question is never "should this business automate" but "which job, and is it ready". A job is ready when it is repetitive, when the rules for doing it are written down (or could be), and when a mistake in it is cheap and reversible.

## The signs it is time

None of these on its own is decisive. Three of them together is.

**Hours per week.** Count them for a fortnight, honestly, split into sourcing, photographing, listing, packing, posting, messages, bookkeeping and everything else. Sellers we know are usually surprised that listing and bookkeeping take more than sourcing. When admin (listing, labels, records, messages) is more than half your hours, or more than about ten hours a week on its own, you are paying yourself to type.

**Listing counts.** Under about a hundred active listings you can hold the whole shop in your head. A few hundred and you cannot; things get relisted with wrong postage, ended by mistake, or sold twice. Past a thousand, doing anything to all of them by hand (a postage change, a returns policy change) is a day's work, and eBay's own tools for doing it in bulk are the first thing to learn.

**Error rates.** Wrong item sent, wrong weight on the label so the parcel is surcharged, a cancellation because the stock was not there, a missed message. Each one costs money and, on eBay, a cancellation for being out of stock is a [defect](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/selling-policies/seller-performance-policy?id=4347) that counts against your standing. If you are making one of these a week, the fix is usually a system, not more care.

**The backlog.** Stock you have bought and not listed is money on a shelf. If the unlisted pile grows every week, the listing process is the bottleneck.

**The January feeling.** If the tax return meant a week of reconstructing the year from platform downloads and bank statements, the bookkeeping side is overdue, and Making Tax Digital will force the issue in any case once gross sales pass the [thresholds](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/find-out-if-and-when-you-need-to-use-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax).

## What to automate first

In order. Each step is cheap or free, and each one makes the next easier.

### 1. Listing templates and saved policies

Cost: nothing. This is the biggest saving for the least risk and most sellers skip it. eBay lets you save up to [50 listing templates](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/selling-templates?id=4097) that carry category, item specifics, description layout, postage and returns, and [business policies](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/business-policies/business-policies?id=4212) that hold your payment, postage and returns settings once and apply to every listing; change the policy and every listing using it updates. "Sell similar" on any sold item does the same job for one offs. Other platforms have their own equivalents, from saved descriptions to the ability to duplicate a listing. Build one template per category you sell in, with the description written so only the item's own details need filling in.

What this fixes: inconsistent postage, inconsistent returns, forgotten item specifics, and the twenty minutes per listing that should be five.

### 2. Postage labels from the order

Cost: usually nothing beyond the postage. Every major platform will generate a label from the order, with the buyer's address pulled straight from the sale, and most carriers offer a free account that imports orders from several platforms at once and prints labels in a batch. The address is never retyped, the tracking number goes back to the platform automatically, and the buyer sees it. Add a set of scales and print weights from the actual parcel.

What this fixes: address errors, missing tracking (which is your only defence in an item not received case), and the late delivery rate, because eBay counts an item as [dispatched](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/posting-items/estimated-delivery-dates-sellers?id=4086) only when the carrier scans it, and a batch of labels with a booked collection gets that scan reliably.

### 3. Bookkeeping feeds

Cost: a monthly subscription for bookkeeping software, or nothing if you use a spreadsheet with disciplined monthly imports. The point is to stop typing transactions. Software of this type connects to your business bank account and imports every transaction; the better ones also import platform payouts and split them into sales, fees and refunds. You then categorise, which takes minutes a week rather than hours a month. This is also the step that puts you in the right place for [Making Tax Digital](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/find-out-if-and-when-you-need-to-use-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax), which requires compatible software once you are within its thresholds, and it produces the records HMRC expects you to [keep](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/how-long-to-keep-your-records).

What this fixes: the January week, the missed expenses, and not knowing whether you made money last month until three months later.

### 4. Stock control

Cost: from nothing (a spreadsheet with a SKU per item) to a subscription (inventory software). The first step is simply a unique reference on every item, written on the bag or a label and typed into the listing's SKU field, so that when it sells you know exactly which shelf it is on. The second is a single record of quantity for anything you hold more than one of, updated by the sale. On eBay, turn on the [out of stock option](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/creating-managing-listings/multiquantity-listings-listings-variations?id=4150) for multi quantity listings so that a sold out line is hidden rather than ended and rebuilt.

What this fixes: the ten minutes spent looking for an item, and the cancellation when it turns out you sold it last week on another platform.

### 5. Repricing, within limits

Cost: a subscription, and only worth it on platforms where you compete on identical products, which mostly means Amazon and multi quantity new goods on eBay. A repricer moves your price within a floor and a ceiling you set, in response to competitors. It is pointless for one off second-hand items where you are the only seller of that exact thing; there, the price is a judgement you make once.

The rule that makes repricing safe: every product has a floor price below which the software cannot go, and the floor is your cost plus fees plus postage plus a margin, worked out before the product is listed. A repricer with no floor sells at a loss without telling you.

### 6. Cross-listing and multi-channel sync

Cost: a subscription. Tools of this type take a listing from one platform and post it to others, and, more importantly, remove it everywhere when it sells anywhere. That second function is the one you are paying for; see the [multi-channel guide](/guides/multi-channel-selling) on why. Do this last, because it multiplies whatever your listing process is. If your listings are inconsistent, cross-listing gives you inconsistent listings on four platforms.

What this fixes: double sales, and the hour spent manually ending listings after a busy day.

### After that

Message templates for the questions you get every day (postage times, combined postage, measurements). Scheduled reports. Photo background removal. Each is a small saving; none is worth doing before the six above.

## What never to automate

Some jobs look repetitive and are not. The pattern is the same for all of them: the cost of a wrong answer is high, and the right answer depends on something a rule cannot see.

**Replies to buyers with a problem.** A template that says "we are sorry to hear that" to someone whose parcel has arrived broken loses the sale, the feedback and often the case. A message that shows you have read theirs and tells them exactly what happens next keeps all three. Auto replies for "what are your postage times" are fine. Anything involving a return, a fault, a missing item or an angry buyer is answered by a person, quickly, and on Amazon within [24 hours](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) including weekends. Have a set of saved paragraphs to build from, by all means; the message itself is written for that buyer.

**Sourcing decisions.** Software can show you sold prices and sell-through. It cannot tell you that the jacket has a repair, that the box has been opened, or that a car boot trader with forty identical pairs of trainers is not a lucky find. Buying is the skill, and it stays yours.

**Condition grading.** "Used, good condition" written by a template is the beginning of a not as described case. Every second-hand item is graded by someone who has held it, and the flaws are listed and photographed. This is the single most effective thing you can do for your defect rate, and it cannot be delegated to a rule.

**Anything that changes money without a limit.** Repricers without floors, automatic offer acceptance without a minimum, automatic refunds without a cap. Every one of these should have a number in it that it cannot cross.

**Relationships with sources.** The charity shop manager, the auction house, the liquidator. Automation does not get you the bag put aside.

## The cost of tools versus time

The arithmetic is simple and most people do not do it.

Work out what an hour of your time is worth to the business. Not your day job rate: the profit you make in an hour of the work only you can do, which for most resellers is sourcing and grading. If a morning at the auction reliably produces £150 of profit over the following month, an hour is worth about £40.

Then, for any tool, count the hours it saves per month and multiply. A tool costing £30 a month that saves five hours of listing is buying you £200 of sourcing time for £30. A tool costing £30 a month that saves one hour is marginal. A tool that saves nothing because you never set it up properly is the most common outcome, and it is why subscriptions are reviewed every month.

Two corrections to the arithmetic. First, add the mistakes avoided: one cancellation defect or one lost not received case a month is worth more than most subscriptions. Second, subtract the setup time, honestly. Inventory software can take a weekend to load; cross-listing tools need every listing tidied first. If you will not do the setup, do not buy the tool.

A guide to what the sellers we know actually pay: under about 300 listings a month, nothing beyond bookkeeping software. Between 300 and 1,000, bookkeeping plus a label tool plus possibly stock control. Above that, all of it. Anyone spending more than a tenth of gross profit on tools should look hard at which ones are earning their place.

## A decision checklist

Run this before you pay for anything or set up anything that acts without you.

1. Have you counted the hours the job takes now? If not, count them for two weeks first.
2. Is the job the same every time, or does it need a judgement? Same every time: automate. Judgement: keep it.
3. What does a mistake cost, and can you undo it? Cheap and reversible: automate. Expensive or permanent (a lost buyer, a defect, a loss making sale): only with a hard limit built in.
4. Have you tried the free version first? Platform templates, business policies, the platform's own label printing, a spreadsheet.
5. Is the process written down well enough that you could give it to someone else? If you cannot describe the rule, the software cannot follow it.
6. What is the setup cost in hours, and when will you do it?
7. What is the floor, cap or limit, and where is it set?
8. How will you know it is working? Pick the number (hours, errors, backlog) and check it a month later.
9. What happens when it breaks? Every tool that touches stock or prices goes wrong eventually; know how to turn it off and what state that leaves your listings in.
10. Will you review the subscription in three months? Put the date in the diary now.

## What goes wrong

- **Automating before the process exists.** Cross-listing chaotic listings to four platforms produces chaos on four platforms.
- **A repricer with no floor.** Selling below cost, at scale, while you sleep.
- **Auto replies to problems.** Buyers can tell, and the case follows.
- **Templated conditions.** The same phrase on every item, and the not as described cases that follow.
- **Sync tools that only push and never pull.** If the tool lists everywhere but does not delist when something sells, it manufactures double sales.
- **Subscriptions that outlive their use.** Reviewed monthly, or they become a permanent leak.
- **Trusting the tracking number.** The label is not the dispatch; the carrier scan is.
- **Automating the bookkeeping and never looking at it.** The feed imports; you still have to categorise and reconcile, or the numbers are a fiction.
- **Buying the tool the video recommended.** The right tool depends on your platforms and your volume. Ask sellers doing what you do.
