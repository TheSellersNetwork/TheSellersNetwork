This is for UK sellers who want to buy stock in physical shops and sell it on Amazon through FBA, and for online arbitrage sellers wondering whether a car and a barcode scanner would add to what they already do. It assumes you have read the [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) and have an Amazon account, a price history tool and a sourcing calculator on your phone. Retailers are named because they are where stock is bought; nothing here is an endorsement of any of them, and their policies change. None of this is tax or legal advice.

## How it works

You walk into a shop, scan a product's barcode with a sourcing app, and the app shows you the matching Amazon listing, its price, its sales rank and an estimate of profit after fees. If the numbers work and the listing's history looks right, you buy what is on the shelf, take it home, label it and send it to Amazon. That is the whole model.

The margin comes from the same place as [online arbitrage](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage): shops price stock to shift it, and Amazon prices stock to what customers will pay. The difference is that in a shop you see stock nobody online can see, in quantities that are limited to what is in front of you, with a till receipt instead of an invoice. Each of those differences is either an advantage or a problem depending on the day.

Where it beats online arbitrage: local clearance is invisible to sellers in other towns, so listings are less crowded; you handle the product and can check condition, dates and packaging before you pay; and you can start with twenty pounds and an afternoon. Where it loses: it does not scale past what you can carry and drive to, the paperwork is weaker, and it costs a day for what an online session finds in an hour. Most sellers who last use it to learn and to supplement, not as the whole business. The last section explains how the one leads into the other.

## What to look for

Shops signal clearance in ways that become obvious once you know them.

**Clearance and reduced stock.** End of line, discontinued and overstocked products, usually on a dedicated bay, an end of aisle, or a trolley near the tills. Reductions come in steps: the first is small, later ones deep. The deep step is where the margin is, and it happens when the shop needs the shelf space back, which is why the calendar below matters.

**Yellow stickers and reduced labels.** Supermarkets mark down with coloured stickers on the pack. Grocery reductions are mostly short dated and no use for FBA (dated stock must arrive at Amazon with at least 105 days of life). Non food reductions in the same shops, on health and beauty, homeware and seasonal, can be good. Peel every sticker before the product goes to Amazon; a reduced label on a unit sold as new is a complaint waiting to happen.

**Seasonal sell offs.** Everything with a date attached, from Easter eggs to garden furniture to Christmas gift sets, has to be gone by a date, and the shop would rather sell it at a loss than store it. The Amazon price for the same item often does not move at all, or moves up as other retailers sell out.

**Multibuys and promotional pricing.** Three for two, half price, buy one get one free. A promotion in one chain can put a product under its Amazon price for a fortnight. These are replenishable while the promotion runs and vanish when it ends, so buy quickly and do not assume next month.

**Packaging changes.** When a brand refreshes its packaging, shops clear the old design. The Amazon listing usually accepts both; check the listing's images and recent reviews for complaints about "old packaging" before you buy.

**The back of the shelf and the top shelf.** Stock that has been there longest, sometimes at a price that was never updated after a range change. Scan things that look forgotten.

The check on every one of these is the chart: is the Amazon price stable, does it sell, how many sellers, is Amazon itself in stock. The [online arbitrage guide](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage#reading-a-price-history-chart) covers how to read it. In a shop you have less time, so scan first, shortlist, then read charts properly on the shortlist before you go to the till.

## The receipt problem

This is the part that separates people who do retail arbitrage safely from people who get a nasty surprise a year in.

Amazon asks for proof of where stock came from in two situations. The first is when you apply to sell a gated brand or category: the application form asks for invoices from a manufacturer or authorised distributor, showing your name and address as Amazon has them, the supplier's name and address, at least ten units in total, dated within the last 180 days, and it says retail receipts are not accepted for that purpose. The second is when a customer, a brand or Amazon's systems question a product's authenticity, and Amazon asks you to show where the stock came from. A till receipt is what you will have, and its value depends entirely on what is printed on it.

A useful receipt names the shop, is dated, and lists each product by name or a recognisable description with the quantity and price. A weak receipt lists "beauty item" or a department code. A useless receipt is a card machine slip with a total. For authenticity, sellers we know have had shop receipts with clear product lines accepted; for ungating, they have not. Plan on that.

The practical answer is to buy from retailers that will give you a VAT receipt. A VAT receipt, or a simplified VAT invoice, is one that shows the retailer's VAT number and the VAT charged; GOV.UK sets out what a [VAT invoice](https://www.gov.uk/vat-record-keeping/vat-invoices) must contain. Retailers that sell to trade customers issue them as a matter of course, and many large chains will print one at the customer service desk if you ask, or make one available through their app or online account after the purchase. Some will put your business name and address on it. Ask, every time, and keep every one scanned, because the receipt you did not keep is the one Amazon asks for. Where a retailer sells online as well as in store, buying the same product online gives you an order confirmation addressed to you, which is a better document; that is one of the reasons retail arbitrage tends to push people online.

Two rules of thumb: do not build a large position in any gated or heavily policed brand from shop receipts, because you cannot get approval with them and cannot defend a complaint well; and do not buy from any source that gives no receipt at all.

## A tour of UK store types

What each type tends to yield, and what to watch for. Individual shops vary and the manager decides more than the chain does, so learn your local branches rather than trusting the name.

| Store type | Examples | What tends to work | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Supermarkets | Tesco, Sainsbury's, Asda, Morrisons | Health and beauty, household, seasonal non food, toys at Christmas clearance, own brand gift sets | Food reductions are short dated; grocery, beauty and baby are gated categories on Amazon; large stores have the clearance, small ones do not |
| Discount and variety chains | B&M, Home Bargains, The Range, Poundland, Wilko's successors | Branded household, toiletries, pet, garden, seasonal, discontinued lines from big brands | Prices move weekly, other sellers scan the same aisle, stock is often the last of a line and non replenishable |
| Health and beauty | Boots, Superdrug, Savers, Bodycare | Gift sets after Christmas, Valentine's and Mother's Day, discontinued fragrance and cosmetics, electricals such as hair tools | Beauty is gated; many brands gated on top; fragrance and aerosols are hazmat; check Transparency |
| DIY and tools | B&Q, Wickes, Screwfix, Toolstation, Homebase | End of season garden, discontinued power tool accessories, lighting, hardware multipacks | Heavy and bulky items pay high FBA fees; check size tier before buying |
| Toys | Smyths, The Entertainer, supermarket toy aisles | Discontinued sets, seasonal clearance in January, licensed products after a film or series has passed | Toy safety marks, Transparency on major brands, brands that police resellers |
| Electricals | Currys, Argos, supermarket electrical aisles | Discontinued models, open box and clearance, accessories | Returns rates are high, warranties and serial numbers create complaints, lithium batteries are hazmat |
| Homeware | Dunelm, Wilko style ranges, department stores, Aldi and Lidl special buys | Kitchen and dining sets, bedding, small appliances at end of season | Bulky, fragile, and often own brand with no Amazon listing |
| Wholesale clubs | Costco, Booker, Bestway | Multipacks, branded consumables, seasonal and clearance at the end of each season, and a proper invoice at the till | Membership required and eligibility rules apply ([Costco's membership page](https://www.costco.co.uk/membership) explains its types); pack sizes must match the listing exactly; big spends on a single line |

A note on the wholesale clubs. They sit between a shop and a supplier. The receipt is an invoice with your membership details and VAT shown, which solves the receipt problem, and the stock is sold in trade quantities. They do not use the stickers and clearance bays other shops use, so you scan more and rely on the chart rather than the ticket. Check the club's own website first, because what is reduced there is usually reduced in store.

## The yearly calendar

Clearance follows the retail calendar, and the retail calendar barely changes. Plan visits around it rather than turning up hopeful.

| When | What clears | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Late December to January | Christmas gift sets, confectionery gift boxes, toys, decorations, party | Health and beauty, supermarkets, toy shops, discount chains |
| Late January | Winter clothing and home textiles, heaters, electric blankets | Homeware, DIY, supermarkets |
| Mid February | Valentine's gifting, fragrance sets | Health and beauty, supermarkets |
| Late March | Mother's Day gifting | Health and beauty |
| After Easter | Easter confectionery (mostly dated and meltable, so mostly not for FBA), Easter toys and crafts | Supermarkets, discount chains |
| June | Father's Day gifting, tools and gadgets | DIY, health and beauty, department stores |
| July to August | Summer toys, paddling pools, garden furniture, barbecues, sun care | DIY, supermarkets, discount chains, wholesale clubs |
| Early September | Back to school lines, remaining garden | Supermarkets, stationery, DIY |
| Late September to October | Old toy ranges cleared for Christmas ranges, homeware resets | Toy shops, supermarkets, homeware |
| November | Black Friday and pre Christmas promotions across electricals, toys, beauty | Everywhere; margins thin because the Amazon price falls too |
| Early December | Last chance to get stock to Amazon before the Christmas cut off | Nothing clears; this is selling season, not buying season |

Two things the calendar does not say. First, Amazon's fees rise from mid October to mid January and dated or meltable stock has arrival rules, so what you buy in a summer clearance must still be sellable when it lands; the [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#what-most-guides-leave-out) covers the dates. Second, seasonal stock bought in January to sell next December is a year of storage fees and a year of price risk. It works for some products; run the numbers with twelve months of storage in the calculator, not one.

## Stores and situations to avoid

Not because they are bad shops, but because what they give you cannot be defended or sold.

- **Charity shops, car boots, classified ads and marketplace listings.** No receipt, or a receipt with no product detail and no business behind it. Fine for eBay, no use for Amazon authenticity. The [sourcing guide](/guides/sourcing-for-resale) covers those sources for other platforms.
- **Shops whose receipts do not name the product.** Some off price chains print department descriptions instead of product names. Genuine stock, useless paperwork. If you must, keep the product's own packaging photographed alongside the receipt, and keep quantities small.
- **Other sellers.** Buying stock from another Amazon seller, or from someone clearing their FBA returns, gives you their invoice, not yours. It cannot be used for approvals and it invites the question of why a retailer's stock has passed through two resellers.
- **Pet consumables and supplements.** Flea treatments, wormers, supplements and anything with a health claim are policed heavily, and complaints from these categories end accounts. Leave them alone until you have the approvals and the supplier invoices to match.
- **Anything with a safety mark you cannot see.** Toys, electricals and children's products need the right marking and paperwork; Amazon publishes its [UK and EU product marking guidance](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G3NRFVS4YUYRQ85J) for sellers. If a product should carry a mark and the box does not show one, it is not stock.
- **Opened, damaged or display units.** Amazon's "new" means sealed and complete. Damaged box is a different condition and a different, smaller market.
- **Brands that show Transparency codes on the listing.** Retail units do not come with codes. You cannot list them.
- **Anything you cannot return.** Some shops refuse returns on clearance. If the chart turns against you between the till and the label, you want the shop to take it back.

## Purchase limits and being a good customer

Shops set limits for a reason: stock is for their customers, and clearing a shelf annoys the people who work there. Some chains print a per customer limit on promotions; some will simply refuse a trolley of forty of the same item. Buy sensible quantities, split large buys across visits or branches if you must, and never argue at the till. The manager who knows you as polite and predictable is the manager who tells you when the next markdown is coming; the one who remembers you as the person who cleared the toy aisle is the one who refuses your next receipt request.

The same goes for online orders from the same chains: retailers cancel orders that look like resale and close accounts that order unusual quantities. Keep to household sized orders on any one account, and do not open accounts in other people's names.

## Numbers that make it worth the day

Retail arbitrage costs time and fuel before it costs anything else, so the margin per unit has to be higher than for an online buy. Sellers we know use a floor of at least £5 net profit per unit and 30% return on investment on retail finds, and will not pick up anything under £3 net however good the percentage, because the labelling, the drive and the risk are the same for a £3 item as for a £15 one. Set your own floor and write it on the phone case.

Plan routes. A retail park with six chains within a walk beats six high street shops across a town. Note which branches are large enough to have a clearance bay. Go on weekday mornings if you can, after the overnight markdowns and before the weekend crowd of other scanners. Keep a log per trip: shop, time, spend, units, and later what those units made. After ten trips the log will tell you which shops are worth the fuel and which just feel productive.

## How retail arbitrage leads into online arbitrage

Retail arbitrage teaches you three things faster than any other method: what a good listing looks like, which brands and categories sell, and which retailers under price which products. Every one of those transfers directly to buying online.

Take it a step at a time:

1. **Check the shop's website while you stand in the aisle.** If the same clearance price is online, order it online with delivery to your address. Same product, same margin, an invoice with your name on it, a returns window, and no carrying.
2. **Reverse source what you found.** For each product that made money from a shop, search for it across retailers online. Find who else stocks it and at what price. The product a shop clears once is often one a website under prices every month.
3. **Keep the sold list.** Everything that made money, with the retailer and the price you paid. This becomes the replenishable list the [online arbitrage guide](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage#replenishables) is built around.
4. **Use the receipts you get to learn what documents you will need.** When a receipt fails you, it tells you which brands need invoice level paperwork, and that list becomes the products you buy online or from a supplier rather than in store.
5. **Move the routine indoors.** Once the online buys are outnumbering the shop buys, keep the retail trips for the calendar dates that pay (January, post Easter, late summer, October) and spend the rest of the week at the desk.

Sellers who make the transition usually say the shop trips got them started and the online sessions kept them going.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying on the app's profit figure alone.** The app does not know Amazon is about to come back in stock, that the listing is for a pack of two, or that the price in the app is the top of the chart. Read the chart on the shortlist before the till.
- **Dated stock.** Anything with a best before needs at least 105 days on arrival at Amazon. Clearance food and some cosmetics fail this before they leave the car park.
- **Stickers left on.** A reduced label on a "new" unit is a "not as described" complaint.
- **Receipts in a shoebox.** Scan them on the day, name the file by shop and date, and put them with the shipment record.
- **Gated brands from shop receipts.** You cannot get approved with them. Check the listing in Seller Central before you buy, not after.
- **Bulky stock.** A £12 margin on a garden chair disappears into the oversize fulfilment fee. Check the size tier on Amazon's [fee page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) before you load the car.
- **Clearing a shelf.** The other sellers in your town saw it too, and the listing will have five new offers by the time yours lands.
- **Fuel and time not counted.** Forty miles and four hours for £30 of profit is a bad job, not a business. The trip log makes this visible.
