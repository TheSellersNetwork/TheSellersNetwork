This is for UK Amazon sellers who are past the beginner stage, are tired of listings crowded with arbitrage sellers, and are being told by every video and course that the real money is in wholesale or in their own brand. Some of that is true. Most of it is sold to people who are not ready. This guide sets out what each model requires, what it costs, how it fails and how to try it at a size you can afford to be wrong at. Figures link to official pages where they matter and change often; none of this is tax, legal or financial advice.

## The two models, plainly

**Wholesale** is buying branded products in bulk from the brand or its authorised distributor, at a trade price, and selling them on the existing Amazon listing alongside every other seller the distributor supplies. You do not own the brand or the listing. Your edge, if you have one, is price, stock depth and the relationship with the supplier.

**Private label** is having a product made to your specification, in your packaging, under your own brand, and creating and owning the listing. Nobody else can sell your product, and nobody knows it exists until you pay to tell them. Your edge is a product people prefer and a listing that ranks; both cost money before they earn any.

Arbitrage, which the [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) and the [online arbitrage guide](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage) cover, sits between: small quantities of branded stock bought at retail, with returns windows and invoices, on listings that already sell. The reason it comes first is that it teaches the platform on money you can get back. Neither of the models below gives your money back.

## What wholesale really requires

### A trade account

Distributors and brands sell to businesses. To open an account you will typically be asked for your business name and registration, a trading address, your VAT number, a website or a description of where you sell, and sometimes references or evidence of trading history. Many will decline a business that only sells on Amazon, and some brands prohibit their distributors from supplying Amazon sellers at all. Expect to apply to twenty suppliers to open three accounts.

Online wholesale marketplaces, which aggregate many distributors and let you buy smaller quantities with a proper invoice, have made the first step easier. They are a reasonable way to test the model and to get invoices that count. They do not give you a price advantage over anyone else on the same platform.

### Minimum orders

A trade account comes with a minimum, either per order or per line. A few hundred pounds per order is common at the small end; larger distributors want thousands. The minimum is what turns "I would like to try this product" into "I have bought sixty units of it."

### VAT registration

You do not have to be VAT registered to buy wholesale, but in practice it decides whether the model works. Trade prices are quoted without VAT, and VAT is added at 20%. If you are not registered, that 20% is a cost you cannot reclaim, on top of the 20% VAT on Amazon's fees you also cannot reclaim, and against sellers on the same listing who reclaim both. Registration is compulsory once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in a rolling twelve months, and it is possible to [register voluntarily](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat) below that. Registering voluntarily brings a sixth of every sale to HMRC as output VAT, so it is not free either; an accountant can show you the crossover for your numbers. Sellers we know who run wholesale seriously are all registered.

### Invoices that count

The one thing wholesale gives you that arbitrage cannot is the invoice Amazon actually wants. Brand and category applications ask for invoices from the manufacturer or an authorised distributor, with your business details and theirs, at least ten units in total, dated within the last 180 days. A distributor's invoice is exactly that, and it is why wholesale sellers get approved for brands that refuse arbitrage sellers, and why they can defend an authenticity complaint in one email. Insist on invoices that show your name and address exactly as Amazon has them.

### The listing problem

You are joining a listing, not creating one. The same distributor sells to dozens of Amazon sellers, and every one of them has your cost price. The Buy Box rotates between sellers at the same price, and the seller who wants more of it lowers the price. Margins on open wholesale listings compress to a few percent within months of a product becoming known. The wholesale sellers who make money either have a price other sellers do not (volume, a direct brand relationship, an exclusive), or sell products too dull or too awkward for the crowd to bother with. "Find a distributor, buy what they have" is not a strategy; it is a queue.

## What private label really requires

### A product and a manufacturer

A product with demand you can measure on the chart, that you can have made at a cost that leaves margin after every fee and after advertising, and that is not already sold by a hundred brands at a price you cannot match. Then a manufacturer, usually overseas, which means samples, communication across time zones, quality control you cannot see, and a minimum order that is typically hundreds or thousands of units. Then shipping, customs and import VAT, which the [Amazon to Amazon guide](/guides/amazon-to-amazon-arbitrage#route-two-eu-to-uk) touches on and which for goods from outside the EU means duty at the [tariff rate](https://www.gov.uk/trade-tariff) on top.

### A trademark and Brand Registry

Without a trademark anyone can list on your listing, copy your product and undercut you. Amazon's [Brand Registry](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/brand-registry), which gives you control of your listings, brand protection tools and access to advertising formats, is free to join but needs a registered trademark or a pending application in a country with an Amazon store, and your brand name permanently on the product or its packaging. A UK trade mark application made online costs £205 for one class and £60 for each extra class ([trade mark forms and fees](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/trade-mark-forms-and-fees/trade-mark-forms-and-fees), revised April 2026), usually takes three to four months to grant, and lasts ten years ([how to register](https://www.gov.uk/how-to-register-a-trade-mark)). Budget more if you use an attorney, and search before you file, because a name that conflicts with an existing mark is a wasted fee and a rebrand.

### Packaging, compliance and photography

Your name goes on the box, so the box is yours: design, printing, barcodes (you will need your own product barcodes from the official issuing body, and Amazon checks them), and the legal markings. Toys, electricals, cosmetics, anything for children and many other categories need the right safety marking, technical documentation and a UK responsible person; Amazon's [UK and EU product marking guidance](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G3NRFVS4YUYRQ85J) sets out what it expects from sellers, and Northern Ireland follows the EU regime. Then product photography, which on Amazon decides whether anyone clicks. Professional images of a single product run to hundreds of pounds; you cannot skip them.

### An advertising budget

A new listing has no reviews, no sales history and no rank, and Amazon's search does not show it to anyone. Sponsored ads are how a new private label product gets its first sales, paid per click at auction against every other brand bidding on the same searches. There is no official figure for what it costs because it is an auction; what sellers we know report is that the first months of a launch are usually loss making on the advertising line, and that a product which cannot reach profitability with ads switched on is a product that will not reach it at all. Budget for months of advertising, not weeks, on top of the stock.

### Reviews and ranking

Reviews come slowly and only from real customers, under strict rules, and every method of speeding them up that is not Amazon's own programme risks the account. Ranking follows sales, sales follow visibility, visibility follows ads and reviews. It is a slow loop, and it is why private label is a year long project, not a product launch.

### Insurance

Amazon's UK agreement requires product liability insurance once gross sales pass £4,000 a month for three consecutive months. As a brand owner you are the manufacturer of record in the UK, so the insurance is not a formality; a claim comes to you.

## The capital and cash flow reality

Arbitrage money goes out and comes back in weeks. Wholesale and private label money goes out and comes back in months, and some of it does not come back at all.

| | Arbitrage | Wholesale | Private label |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Money out before the first sale | Tens to hundreds of pounds per product | Hundreds to thousands per order | Thousands: stock, freight, duty, VAT, trademark, packaging, photography, launch advertising |
| Time from payment to first sale | Two to four weeks | Four to eight weeks | Three to six months from first sample |
| Time to recover the outlay | Weeks | Months | Six to twelve months if the product works |
| Can you get the money back if wrong | Usually, within the returns window | Rarely; distributors do not take stock back | No |
| What you own at the end | Nothing, and nothing to lose | A supplier relationship | A brand, a listing and, if it worked, an asset that can be sold |

The cash flow trap is the same in both models. Sales start, the product works, and you need to reorder before the first order has paid for itself, because the lead time on the second order is as long as the first. So the second order comes from new money, and the third. A product that is growing eats cash for the first year; Amazon pays out after delivery, and stock sitting at Amazon pays [monthly storage and, past 241 days, an aged inventory surcharge](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing). If you cannot fund three orders before the first one has returned its money, you cannot fund the product.

Fund it from money the business can lose. Never from a loan secured on stock that has not sold, and never from the cash that runs your existing arbitrage business, because that business is what pays the bills while the new one finds out whether it works.

## Five ways new private label sellers lose money

Every one of these has happened to sellers we know, and most of them happen to the same person in the same year.

**1. Choosing a product because it is easy to make, not because anyone wants it.** The research is done backwards: a manufacturer's catalogue first, the chart second. The result is a product that costs £4 to land and sells for £12 in a category where twenty other brands sell the same thing for £11, with reviews you will not have for a year. The fix is dull: the chart first, demand measured, competition counted, a reason someone would pick yours, and only then a manufacturer.

**2. Photographs and a listing that do not sell.** The product is fine, the listing is a phone photo on a kitchen table and three bullet points. Ads bring the clicks and nobody buys, so the advertising spend is a straight loss. Photography, a title that says what the product is and who it is for, and bullets that answer the questions in competitors' reviews are the difference between a listing that converts and one that burns money.

**3. Running out of stock.** The product works, sells faster than planned, and the reorder was placed too late. The listing goes out of stock for six weeks, rank collapses, ads pause, and when the stock arrives you are launching again. Reorder points based on lead time, not on gut feeling, and a second order placed before the first has proven itself if the early signs are good.

**4. Storage nobody budgeted for.** The opposite failure. The first order was sized to the manufacturer's minimum, not to demand, and two thousand units are sitting at Amazon paying monthly storage, higher in October to December, and the aged inventory surcharge after 241 days. Size the first order to what you expect to sell in three to four months even if the per unit price is worse, and hold the balance at home or with a prep centre if the minimum forces you higher.

**5. No cash for the second and third orders.** Covered above, and the most common cause of death. The product is profitable per unit and the business still fails, because the money for the reorder was spent on the launch. Model three order cycles before you place the first.

A sixth that is not on most lists: **starting private label before understanding the platform.** Every rule in the FBA manual about listings, condition, compliance, account health and Amazon's fees applies to your own brand as well, and mistakes that cost an arbitrage seller a few units cost a brand owner an order.

## When each becomes sensible

**Wholesale becomes sensible when** you are VAT registered or close to it, you have twelve months of clean selling history to show suppliers, you know from your own sales data which products and brands sell at what speed, you have cash that can sit in stock for two to three months, and you have found a supplier with a price other sellers on the listing do not have. If the only supplier you can find is one that sells to everyone at the same price, you have not found a wholesale opportunity; you have found a listing with a queue.

**Private label becomes sensible when** you have a product idea that came from selling and reading reviews, not from a catalogue; money the business can lose without the business ending; a year of patience; a trademark filed; and a plan for the listing, the photographs and the advertising that you have costed rather than hoped for. It also helps to have a category you already know well from arbitrage, because you will understand the customers, the pricing and the competition before you spend anything.

**Neither is sensible when** the arbitrage business is not yet reliably profitable, when you would be funding it with credit, or when the reason for moving is that a video said this is where the real money is. The real money, for most sellers we know, is in a boring arbitrage business run well for a long time, with wholesale added for the products it suits and private label attempted once, small, when the business can afford the lesson.

## How to test either without betting the business

The principle is the same as in arbitrage: buy small, measure, then decide.

### Testing wholesale

1. **Start with what you already sell.** Pick three products from your sold list that you have replenished more than twice. Find their distributor. You already know the speed and the competition; the only unknown is the trade price.
2. **Use an online wholesale marketplace or a small distributor for the first order,** at the smallest quantity that gives a proper invoice. Accept a worse price for a smaller commitment.
3. **Send the stock in and use the invoice.** Apply for one brand approval with it. If the invoice is accepted, you have learned the most valuable thing wholesale offers.
4. **Track the listing for sixty days.** Buy Box share, price movement, seller count. If the price compressed and your share was thin, the product is a queue, not a supplier relationship.
5. **Only then approach a direct account,** with a trading history, a VAT number and the three products you already know sell.

Cap the test at a sum you could write off. If it works, the second order is the first real one.

### Testing private label

1. **Pick the product from your own selling.** Something you have sold in arbitrage, whose reviews complain about a fixable thing, in a category you understand.
2. **File the trademark first.** It takes months and the fee is small next to everything else. A pending application is enough for Brand Registry.
3. **Sample before you order.** Two or three manufacturers, samples from each, and compare them in your hand against the product you already sell.
4. **Order the smallest run the manufacturer will do,** and if the minimum is too big for a test, look for a manufacturer with a smaller one or a product where the minimum is lower. Accept a higher unit cost for a first order you can afford to be wrong about.
5. **Budget the launch as a single line:** stock, freight, duty, VAT, packaging, photography, and three months of advertising. If the total is money you cannot lose, do not start.
6. **Launch on the UK marketplace only,** with Europe switched off, and set a date by which the product must be covering its advertising. If it is not, stop advertising, sell through, and treat the lesson as the product.

The sellers we know who have made private label work all describe the same pattern: one or two failed or flat products, funded small enough to survive, before the one that worked. The ones who did not survive funded the first attempt as if it were the one that would work.

## What goes wrong

- **Wholesale before VAT registration.** Twenty percent on the buy and twenty percent on the fees, against competitors who reclaim both.
- **Believing a distributor is exclusive.** They are supplying the other sellers on the listing too. Ask who else they sell to, and read the seller count on the chart.
- **Minimum orders sized by the supplier, not by demand.** Buy what the listing sells in a season, or do not buy.
- **Private label from a catalogue.** Demand is on the chart, not in the manufacturer's price list.
- **A brand name nobody searched.** Filing fees on a name that conflicts with an existing mark, or a name that cannot be registered at all. Search first.
- **Launching without the safety paperwork.** A listing suppressed until you supply documents you do not have, with stock sitting at Amazon paying storage.
- **Advertising to a bad listing.** Clicks with no conversion is money going straight to Amazon.
- **Reviews bought or begged.** The fastest way to lose the account that took years to build.
- **The second order funded by the first order's sales that have not arrived yet.** Model three cycles before you start.
- **Moving the whole business at once.** Keep the arbitrage running while you test. It is the thing paying for the experiment.
