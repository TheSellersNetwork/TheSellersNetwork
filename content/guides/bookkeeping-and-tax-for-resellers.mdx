This is for anyone in the UK who sells on eBay, Vinted, Amazon, Depop, Facebook or anywhere else and has gone, or is about to go, from clearing the loft to buying stock. It sets out the rules as HMRC publishes them, the dates, and a way of keeping records that makes the tax return a formality. It is not tax advice; every figure links to the official page, and if your situation is unusual, an accountant is worth an hour of their fee.

## Trading or selling your own things

This is the question everything else hangs on, and HMRC's own [guide for online sellers](https://taxhelpforhustles.campaign.gov.uk/buying-or-making-things-to-sell-and-online-selling-tax-rules) puts it plainly. Selling unwanted personal belongings from time to time is not trading and you do not usually need to tell HMRC, whatever the platforms report. Buying things to sell them, or making things to sell, is trading, and trading income is taxable once it passes the allowance.

The tests HMRC applies are known as the badges of trade, set out in its [Business Income Manual](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim20205): whether you set out to make a profit, how often you sell, whether you bought the item in order to sell it, whether you changed or repaired it to sell it, how you sold it, how you funded it, and how long you held it. No single test decides. In practice, if you have bought a job lot at auction, a bag of clothes at a car boot or a marked down product from a supermarket and listed it, you are trading. If you are selling last year's coat, you are not.

Two edges worth knowing. Personal possessions are outside income tax, but Capital Gains Tax can apply if a single item or a set (a collection of coins, a pair of paintings) sells for [£6,000 or more](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax-personal-possessions); your own car is exempt unless you used it for the business. And mixed situations are common: people who trade and also sell their own things. Keep the two apart in your records from the first day and it is simple. Muddle them and you will be trying to remember, a year later, which listings were yours.

## The trading allowance

The [trading allowance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/tax-free-allowances-on-property-and-trading-income) is £1,000 of gross trading income a tax year (6 April to 5 April), free of tax and free of the need to register. Gross means sales before any costs, not profit. It is one allowance across all your side income, not one per platform, and HMRC's guidance is explicit that everything from different side hustles counts towards the same £1,000.

Once gross sales pass £1,000, you must register and file. On the return you then choose: deduct the £1,000 allowance from your income, or deduct your actual expenses. Not both. For a reseller with stock costs, postage and packaging, actual expenses are almost always higher than £1,000, so the allowance stops mattering as soon as you are properly trading.

**What we would do.** Register as soon as it is clear you are trading rather than waiting to see whether you cross £1,000, because the record keeping you need is the same either way and the deadline is easy to miss.

## Registering, and the deadlines

You register for Self Assessment as a sole trader on [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-self-assessment). The deadline is 5 October after the end of the tax year in which you first went over the allowance: sales passing £1,000 during the year to 5 April 2026 means registering by 5 October 2026. HMRC issues a Unique Taxpayer Reference, which you need for the return and which some platforms will ask for.

The return for a tax year is due by [31 October](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines) on paper or 31 January online, and the tax is due by 31 January too. File online; the paper deadline is three months earlier and gains you nothing.

Missing 31 January costs [£100](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/penalties) even if you owe no tax, then £10 a day from three months late up to £900, then a further 5% of the tax or £300 at six months and again at twelve, plus interest and separate penalties for paying late. It is the most expensive admin failure in this business and the easiest to avoid.

**Payments on account.** Once your bill for a year is [£1,000 or more](https://www.gov.uk/understand-self-assessment-bill/payments-on-account), HMRC asks you to pay next year's tax in advance: half on 31 January alongside the balance for the year just gone, half on 31 July. The first January this happens, you pay a year and a half of tax at once. Sellers we know are caught by this every year. Put a percentage of profit aside monthly from the start and it is not a problem.

## What the marketplaces tell HMRC

Since 1 January 2024, UK digital platforms have had to collect and report seller information under the [reporting rules for digital platforms](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reporting-rules-for-digital-platforms). eBay, Vinted, Depop, Amazon, Etsy and the rest all fall under it. They must report sellers who make 30 or more sales of goods in a calendar year, or whose sales come to 2,000 euros (about £1,700) or more. Below both figures, you are not reported. Platforms send their reports to HMRC by 31 January for the previous calendar year and must [give you a copy](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/selling-goods-or-services-on-a-digital-platform), showing your total for the year less the platform's fees, broken down by quarter.

Three things follow. Platforms will ask for your name, address, date of birth and National Insurance number or UTR; that is the law, not the platform being nosy. Being reported does not mean you owe tax; HMRC says so on the same page, and someone clearing a wardrobe with 40 sales owes nothing. And if you are trading, HMRC now has a figure to compare with your return. The calendar year the platform reports and the tax year you file do not line up, so do not expect the two numbers to match; keep your own records and they will reconcile.

## Allowable expenses

You pay tax on profit, not sales. Profit is sales less allowable expenses, which HMRC describes as costs incurred wholly and exclusively for the business. The [GOV.UK list](https://www.gov.uk/expenses-if-youre-self-employed) covers the categories. For a reseller the ones that matter are:

- **Stock.** Everything you buy to sell, including the buyer's premium at auction and the delivery charge on a wholesale order.
- **Postage and packaging.** Labels, boxes, mailing bags, tape, tissue.
- **Platform fees.** Final value fees, listing fees, shop subscriptions, promoted listing charges, payment processing.
- **Travel.** Mileage to car boots, auctions and the post office. You can use the [flat rate](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/vehicles) per business mile instead of actual vehicle costs; keep a mileage log either way.
- **Working from home.** A [flat rate](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/working-from-home) by hours worked at home each month, or a reasonable proportion of actual household costs.
- **Equipment.** Scales, printer, lighting, phone if used for the business, in proportion to the business use.
- **Software and subscriptions.** Bookkeeping, listing, research and repricing tools.
- **Professional fees, insurance, bank charges.** An accountant, a business bank account's fees, any stock or liability cover.

Not allowable: your own time, clothes you wear to the car boot, fines, and anything that is also personal without an honest split. If you claim the trading allowance you claim none of these; see above.

Since April 2024 the [cash basis](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-cash-basis) is the default for sole traders: you record income when the money arrives and expenses when you pay them. For most resellers that is simpler and fairer than traditional accounting, and it means stock is an expense when you buy it, not when it sells. You can opt out on the return if you prefer.

## Keeping records

HMRC does not tell you how to keep records, only that you must, and [what to keep](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/what-records-to-keep): all sales and income, all expenses, and the evidence behind them (receipts, invoices, bank statements). Records must be kept for [at least 5 years](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/how-long-to-keep-your-records) after the 31 January deadline for the year they relate to. If records are lost you can file with estimated or provisional figures and say so, but expect questions.

The system that works, in our experience, has four parts.

1. **A separate bank account.** Every business receipt in, every business cost out, nothing personal. It is not a legal requirement for a sole trader; it is the single thing that makes everything else easy.
2. **A sales record.** One line per sale: date, platform, item, sale price, fees, postage charged, net paid out. Platforms give you this in their reports; download it monthly rather than trusting it will be there in five years.
3. **A purchase record.** One line per purchase: date, source, what, how much, and a photograph or scan of the receipt filed by month. Car boot buys with no receipt get a note with the date, place, item and price the day you buy.
4. **A stock list.** What you hold, what it cost and when you bought it. Not required for tax on the cash basis, but it is the only way to know whether you are making money, and you will want it if you ever go VAT registered.

A spreadsheet does all of this well into the tens of thousands of pounds a year. Bookkeeping software that pulls your bank feed and platform payouts in automatically saves time as volume grows and becomes necessary at the Making Tax Digital thresholds below. Whichever you use, the discipline is the same: nothing waits until January.

## National Insurance and income tax, briefly

Profit from trading is added to any other income (a job, for instance) and taxed at the [income tax rates](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates): nothing on the first £12,570 of total income, 20% to £50,270, 40% above that, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Scotland has its own bands. If you have a job that already uses your Personal Allowance, every pound of reselling profit is taxed at your marginal rate from the first pound.

On top of that, [Class 4 National Insurance](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-national-insurance-rates) at 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270 and 2% above, collected through the return. Class 2 is no longer paid once profits pass the small profits threshold; it is treated as paid, which protects your state pension record. Below that threshold you can pay it voluntarily.

A rough rule for setting money aside: 30% of profit if you have a job paying you above the Personal Allowance, and it will usually cover tax and National Insurance with a little over.

## VAT

VAT registration becomes compulsory when your taxable turnover (sales, not profit) in any rolling 12 months passes [£90,000](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat), or when you expect it to in the next 30 days alone. You must register within 30 days of the end of the month in which you went over. Watch the rolling figure monthly from about £70,000; sellers who only check at the year end find they went over in the summer.

For a reseller of second-hand goods there is a scheme that changes the arithmetic: the [VAT margin scheme](https://www.gov.uk/vat-margin-schemes), under which you pay VAT at one sixth of the difference between what you paid and what you sold for, rather than on the whole sale price. It needs a stock book recording every item's purchase and sale, and it needs purchase invoices, which is one more reason to get paper from every source. New goods bought from retailers do not qualify; the VAT on those is on the full sale price, with the VAT on your purchases reclaimable. This is the point at which an accountant earns their fee.

Platforms charge VAT on their own fees. If you are not registered, that is simply part of the cost.

## Making Tax Digital for Income Tax

This is the biggest change to Self Assessment in a generation and it is now live. From [6 April 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/find-out-if-and-when-you-need-to-use-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax), sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000 must keep digital records and send HMRC [quarterly updates](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/use-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax/send-quarterly-updates) through compatible software, then a final digital return. From 6 April 2027 the threshold falls to £30,000, and from 6 April 2028 to £20,000. Qualifying income is gross income from self-employment and property, not profit, and it is measured from the tax return two years earlier; a reseller turning over £2,000 a month is inside the 2028 net.

The quarterly updates are summaries of income and expenses by category, due by 7 August, 7 November, 7 February and 7 May, and they must be sent even for a quarter with nothing in it. The year end return and the 31 January payment date stay as they are.

**What we would do.** If your gross sales are heading towards £20,000 a year, move to bookkeeping software now, while the volume is small enough to set up properly. Anyone who reaches the threshold with two years of spreadsheets and a shoebox will have a bad quarter.

## Sole trader or limited company

Most resellers should start as a sole trader, and many should stay one. It is free to register, the admin is one return a year (plus quarterly updates once Making Tax Digital applies), losses can be set against other income, and the money is yours as you earn it.

A limited company is a separate legal person. It costs [£100](https://changestoukcompanylaw.campaign.gov.uk/changes-to-companies-house-fees/) to register online, pays [Corporation Tax](https://www.gov.uk/corporation-tax-rates) at 19% on profits up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000 with marginal relief between, files annual accounts with Companies House [9 months after](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company) its year end, a confirmation statement every year, and a Company Tax Return 12 months after the year end. You then take money out as salary or as dividends, which are taxed again in your hands above a [£500 allowance](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-dividends). Its advantages are limited liability, a cleaner separation from your personal finances, and, at higher profits, some flexibility in how and when you pay yourself. Its costs are the accountancy fees, the public filing, and the loss of simplicity.

**What we would do.** Sole trader until profits are consistently well into the higher rate band or you need the limited liability for another reason, and then a conversation with an accountant rather than a decision made from a forum post. Whichever you choose, the name and address you use with HMRC, your bank and every platform should match exactly.

## A simple monthly routine

An hour, on the same day each month.

1. Download the month's sales and payout reports from every platform and save them in a folder for the tax year.
2. Enter or import sales, fees and payouts into your sales record. Check the payout total against the bank account.
3. Enter every purchase from the month with its receipt. Chase any invoice you have not received.
4. Photograph or scan the month's paper receipts and file them by month. Then keep the paper anyway.
5. Add mileage to the log.
6. Move your tax percentage of the month's profit into a separate savings pot.
7. Check rolling 12 month turnover against the VAT threshold and the Making Tax Digital thresholds.
8. Note the diary: 5 October to register, 31 January to file and pay, 31 July for the second payment on account, and the quarterly update dates if they apply.

Do this twelve times and the tax return is an afternoon.

## What goes wrong

- **Not registering because "it is only a side thing".** The threshold is £1,000 of sales, not profit, and the platforms have already told HMRC.
- **Treating stock bought for resale as personal items.** The badges of trade are not hard to apply; HMRC applies them.
- **No separation between personal and business money.** A year of untangling a joint account is the most common reason sellers pay an accountant to do something they could have done themselves.
- **Missing 31 January.** £100 on day one, and it climbs.
- **Not expecting payments on account.** The first bill of £1,000 or more is followed by a demand for half of next year's, the same day.
- **Claiming the trading allowance and expenses.** It is one or the other.
- **Throwing receipts away.** Five years after the filing deadline, and they are also your proof of where stock came from.
- **Finding out about VAT in the December after going over in the June.** Check the rolling 12 months monthly.
- **Ignoring Making Tax Digital until the letter arrives.** The thresholds are gross sales and they are falling each April.
- **Forming a company because someone said it saves tax.** Sometimes it does; usually not at the level most resellers are at, and the admin is real.
