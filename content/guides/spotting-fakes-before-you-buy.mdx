This is for UK resellers who buy branded goods from car boot sales, charity shops, auctions, marketplaces or suppliers and want to avoid buying fakes. It gives the general checks that catch most counterfeits before money changes hands, sets out what the law and the platforms say, and explains what to do if you have bought counterfeits already. It is general information, not legal advice. For brand-specific authentication, nothing replaces handling genuine examples of the brand you sell.

## The numbers you will need

| Thing | Figure or rule | Where it comes from |

## Why it matters to resellers

Three points about the law that sellers often get wrong.

**It is not only about knowing.** The offence under [section 92](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/92) does not require you to have made the goods. Selling them, offering them, or holding them in the course of a business with a view to selling them can all be offences. The defence is that you believed on reasonable grounds that the goods were genuine. "Reasonable grounds" is judged on what you did: where you bought, what you paid, what you checked and what records you kept.

**Where you bought it does not help.** A counterfeit bought in a charity shop or at a car boot sale is still a counterfeit when you list it. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's [car boot sale guidance](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/car-boot-sales-s) specifically warns about branded goods, media and games at boot sales.

**Calling it a replica does not make it legal.** Describing an item as "replica", "inspired by", "dupe" or "unbranded version of" does not remove the problem if it carries a brand's mark or copies it, and the platforms ban the wording anyway ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/prohibited-restricted-items/counterfeit-item-policy?id=4276), [Vinted](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/307-item-authenticity-policy)).

Separately from the criminal law, brands enforce their rights through the platforms. On eBay that is the rights owner programme; see [avoiding VeRO and suspensions](/guides/avoiding-vero-and-suspensions). A listing removed for counterfeit can cost an account.

## The price check comes first

Before you look at stitching, look at the price.

- **Know the retail price.** Check the brand's own site or a major retailer for the current line.
- **Know the resale price.** Check recent sold prices for the genuine item on the platform you will sell on.
- **Compare with what you are being asked.** A household clearing a wardrobe may price a genuine item very low, and that is the whole point of sourcing. A seller with several of the same current item, new with tags, at a small fraction of retail, is not clearing a wardrobe.

| Situation | Risk | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One used item, low price, household seller | Lower | Check it properly, then buy if it passes |
| One new item with tags, low price, household seller | Medium | Ask where it came from; check it closely |
| Several identical new items, low price, any seller | High | Walk away unless you can authenticate each one and trace the source |
| Bulk branded stock from a supplier without invoices | High | Walk away; see [returns pallets and liquidation stock](/guides/returns-pallets-and-liquidation-stock) |
| Any item where the seller refuses close inspection | High | Walk away |

The price check filters out the obvious cases. The physical checks catch the rest.

## General physical checks

These are general signs. Each brand has its own details, and counterfeiters copy the well-known ones. Use these checks to decide whether an item deserves closer inspection, not as proof that it is genuine.

### Labels and tags

- **Print quality.** Genuine labels are usually sharp and even. Blurred, uneven or thick lettering is a warning.
- **Spelling and grammar.** Errors on care labels and swing tags are a common sign of a fake.
- **Consistency.** The brand name, font and logo should match the brand's own current or period style. Compare with photos from the brand's own site, not from other listings.
- **Care and composition labels.** Genuine items normally have them, with country of manufacture, fibre content and care symbols. Missing labels on a new item are a warning.
- **Codes.** Many brands use style, batch or serial codes. Check the format against the brand's own information where it publishes it, and check the code matches the item (a code for a different colour or style is a warning).

### Stitching and construction

- **Even stitches.** Genuine items from established brands usually have even, straight stitching with consistent spacing. Wavy lines, loose threads and double stitching where there should be single are warnings.
- **Pattern matching.** Where a brand is known for patterns that line up across seams, check they do.
- **Lining and inside finish.** Counterfeiters often spend on the outside and save on the inside. Look inside pockets, bags and shoes.
- **Materials.** Feel and smell. Leather that smells of chemicals, plastic that should be metal, fabric that feels thin for the price bracket.

### Hardware

- **Weight.** Zips, buckles, clasps and studs on genuine premium items are usually substantial.
- **Engraving.** Brand engraving on hardware should be crisp and consistent.
- **Finish.** Flaking plating or discolouration on a new item is a warning.
- **Zip makers.** Many brands use particular zip makers. Check the brand's own information if you handle that brand often.

### Packaging

- **Boxes and bags.** Check print quality, colours, fonts and labels against genuine examples.
- **Matching labels.** Box labels should match the item inside (size, colour, style code).
- **Inserts and documents.** Cards, booklets and dust bags should look right, but remember that packaging is also counterfeited and genuine packaging is sold separately to dress up fakes. A good box does not prove a genuine item.

### Media, games and electronics

- **Discs and cases.** Check the print on the disc and the insert, and look for regional and age rating marks.
- **Electronics.** Check serial numbers against the item and the box, and look for proper safety and conformity marks. Counterfeit electricals are also a safety risk; see the Chartered Trading Standards Institute's [second-hand electrical goods guidance](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/product-safety/second-hand-electrical-goods).

### Beauty and fragrance

Counterfeit cosmetics and fragrance can be harmful as well as illegal, and some platforms restrict their resale. Check the platform's rules for the category before you buy at all.

## Before you pay: questions to ask

- **Where did it come from?** A household seller usually knows: a present, bought in a shop, a family member's. A vague answer on a high-value item is a warning.
- **Is there a receipt, box or tag?** Not proof, but useful.
- **Can I look closely?** A genuine seller rarely minds.

If you buy online from another seller, ask for photos of the labels, codes and hardware before paying, and pay in a way that gives you protection. Report Fraud (the service that replaced Action Fraud in England, Wales and Northern Ireland) advises against paying for goods by bank transfer to sellers you do not know; see its [online shopping advice](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/onlineshopping).

## Keep a record that shows reasonable grounds

Your defence, if it ever came to it, rests on showing you acted reasonably. The same record also answers a platform or buyer who questions an item.

- **Receipts or invoices** for every branded item, with the date and the seller.
- **Your checks.** A few photos of the labels, codes and stitching taken when you checked the item.
- **The source.** Where and from whom, and anything the seller told you about its history.
- **Authentication, if you used it.** Some platforms offer authentication for certain categories; for example, Vinted describes a paid [item verification service](https://www.vinted.co.uk/help/1147-item-verification-for-designer-items) for selected designer items. Keep the outcome.

## If you have bought fakes

This is the part that matters most, because it happens to careful resellers too.

1. **Stop.** Do not list the item. If it is listed, end the listing. Do not sell it as a replica, a "dupe", for parts, or to a friend.
2. **Keep it and the evidence.** Keep the item, the packaging, the receipt, the listing if you bought online, and any messages. Photograph everything.
3. **Ask for your money back.**
   - Bought on a platform: open a return or case under the platform's own process, stating that the item is counterfeit. Platforms normally have a specific route for counterfeit items, and some tell buyers to dispose of the item instead of returning it; follow what the platform tells you, because returning a counterfeit to the seller can put it back into circulation.
   - Paid by card: contact your card provider about your options.
   - Bought for cash at a car boot or shop: go back to the seller if you can.
4. **Report the seller.** Trading Standards takes reports through the [Citizens Advice consumer service](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/get-more-help/report-to-trading-standards/). The [IP crime guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses) also lists Crimestoppers for anonymous reports. If you were deceived into paying, report it to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) or to Police Scotland.
5. **Dispose of it properly.** Do not resell it. eBay's policy says counterfeit items must be disposed of, not resold ([eBay](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/policies/prohibited-restricted-items/counterfeit-item-policy?id=4276)). If Trading Standards or the police are involved, they may want it; ask before destroying anything.
6. **Record the loss.** The cost of the item is still a business cost; record it with a note. See [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers).
7. **Check what else came from that source.** If one item from a supplier or seller was fake, check the rest before listing anything.

### If you have already sold one

If you find out after a sale that an item you sold was counterfeit, contact the buyer, explain, and refund in full. Tell the platform if it asks. If you are contacted by Trading Standards, the police or a brand's lawyers, get legal advice before you reply in detail. A seller who acted in good faith, kept records, and refunded promptly when they found out is in a far better position than one who did not.

## What goes wrong

- **Trusting the source.** A charity shop, auction house or other reseller can sell you a fake without knowing.
- **Trusting the box.** Genuine packaging is sold separately.
- **Buying bulk branded stock on a low price.** It is the most common route to a garage full of fakes.
- **Authenticating from other listings.** Other sellers' photos may be of fakes. Compare with the brand's own images and with genuine items you have handled.
- **Selling it as a replica.** Still illegal, still banned.
- **Returning the fake to the seller when the platform says dispose of it.** It ends up back on sale.
- **No records.** Without receipts and check photos, you cannot show reasonable grounds.
- **Branching into a brand you do not know.** Stick to brands you have handled until you know what genuine looks like.
