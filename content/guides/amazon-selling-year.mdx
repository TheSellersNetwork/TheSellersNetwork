This is for arbitrage sellers who have a few months on Amazon behind them and want to stop being surprised by the calendar. After reading it you will know what each part of the year does to sales, fees and stock, when stock has to be in Amazon's warehouses for each event, and what to buy in the quiet months. It ends with a calendar you can copy into your own planner.

The dates that matter come from Amazon's announcements, which appear in Seller Central under News each year, and from the [fee pages](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing). They change; check the current year before you commit money. For the basics of shipping, prep and pricing see [the working manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk).

## The shape of the year

Sales on Amazon are not flat. For most arbitrage sellers the year has one peak (November to mid December), one spike (Prime Day), one dip (mid January to February) and long stretches of ordinary trade in between. The money is made by having the right stock in the warehouse before the peak and not having too much of it after.

Three things move with the season and all of them are in your control:

- **What you buy.** Gifts and toys in autumn, wellness and organisation in January, seasonal categories around each event.
- **When you ship.** Amazon's warehouses slow down in November and the inbound deadlines for peak come earlier than most new sellers expect.
- **What it costs to hold.** Storage doubles in October and a per unit peak fee runs from mid October to mid January.

## Prime Day

### What it is and when

Prime Day is Amazon's own sale for Prime members, usually two to four days in the summer. In 2026 it ran from 23 to 26 June, the first time it had been held in June rather than July, according to [Amazon's announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.co.uk/news/retail/when-is-amazon-prime-day-2026-shop-deals-from-23-to-26-june). Amazon confirms the dates a few weeks ahead, so plan for late June or July and firm it up when the announcement lands. There has also been a second, smaller Prime event in early October in recent years.

### What it does to arbitrage sales

Traffic rises sharply and so do sales of ordinary, non-deal products, because customers are on the site with the intention of buying. Sellers we know see a lift across their whole catalogue for the event days, not only on discounted items. The lift is short. Stock that was going to sell in three weeks sells in three days, and then trade returns to normal. Two consequences:

- If your best products are out of stock on Prime Day you miss the lift entirely. Have stock in place two weeks before.
- Do not read the event days as the new normal and reorder deep. The following fortnight is often quieter than usual because customers have spent.

### Deal eligibility

Amazon's deal programmes (Lightning Deals, Prime exclusive discounts and the Prime Day deals themselves) have eligibility criteria: a Professional selling plan, Prime eligible offers, minimum discounts against a reference price, and account and product rating thresholds. The current criteria are on Amazon's [Prime Day eligibility page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/GLREB2WH9JA7R63D) and the [Amazon deals](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/202111490) help page, and they change each year. In practice most arbitrage offers do not qualify or do not benefit: you are one of several sellers on a listing you do not control, the discount required is often deeper than your margin, and the deal fee is charged whether or not you sell. Our recommendation is to ignore deals as a reseller and take the general lift instead. The exception is a product where you hold the only offer, have real margin and a lot of stock; then run the numbers on the deal fee and the discount and decide.

### Prime Day as a buying day

Prime Day is also a sourcing event. Amazon's own prices drop, competing retailers run sales against it, and a product bought on Prime Day at a discount can be sold at the ordinary price later. This is the Amazon to Amazon and online arbitrage approach applied to a sale, and the same rules apply: check the price history to see what the price was before the discount and whether it recovers, check the buy limits, and buy on your separate buying account, never on the Seller Central login. See [Amazon to Amazon arbitrage](/guides/amazon-to-amazon-arbitrage) if you have not read it.

Do not spend your Q4 money on Prime Day. The stock you buy in June sells in July at ordinary margins; the stock you buy in September sells in November at peak margins. Keep the two pots separate.

### When to send stock

For the event itself: in the warehouse two weeks before, which means shipping three weeks before. Amazon publishes an arrival date for Prime Day deal inventory each year; for ordinary stock there is no formal deadline, but receiving slows as the event approaches.

## Q4

### The deadlines

Two sets of dates decide Q4, and both come from Amazon's announcements.

**Inbound deadlines.** Each year Amazon publishes a date by which stock must arrive at UK fulfilment centres to be reliably available for Black Friday and Cyber Monday. In recent years that date has been the end of October, as in [Amazon's announcement extending it to 31 October](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/seller-forums/discussions/t/74522160-ba2b-41b2-abd0-cfb9583e3875), with an earlier date in September for the October Prime event. The same announcements warn that in November Amazon prioritises customer orders and accepts shipments more slowly, especially pallets. The dates are announced in Seller Central News in late summer; put them in your calendar the day they appear.

Stock that arrives after the deadline is still received and still sells. It just may not be checked in for the biggest days, which is the point of sending it.

**Peak fees.** Amazon charges a festive peak fulfilment fee on top of the normal fulfilment fee. For 2026 it applies from 15 October 2026 to 14 January 2027, at an average of £0.12 per parcel item and £0.07 per large or extra large envelope item for UK orders, with a 1.5% fuel and logistics surcharge on top; see Amazon's [2026 fee pages](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) and the UK announcement in Seller Central News. Monthly storage also rises: the [rate card](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) shows standard size stock at £0.76 per cubic foot a month from January to September and £1.51 from October to December.

Neither fee is large per unit. Together they mean a product on a thin margin that is fine in March is not fine in November, and a slow product that is cheap to hold in summer is expensive to hold in December. Put both into the calculator from October.

### What sells

Gifts, in the broadest sense. Toys and games, board games, beauty gift sets and fragrance, small electronics and accessories, kitchen gadgets, books, anything that photographs well in a box. Also the things people buy for hosting: kitchen and dining, decorations, party supplies. And the boring seasonal category that new sellers forget: winter (heaters, hot water bottles, thermals, de-icer, cold and flu). Look at last year's price and rank history for every candidate; a product that spikes every December is visible in the chart, and so is the fall in the first week of January.

A word on toys. Toys are the biggest Q4 category and also the most heavily regulated and most heavily gated. Check that you can sell the brand before you buy, and read [product safety and listing accuracy](/guides/amazon-product-safety-and-listing-accuracy) on UKCA marking and age warnings before touching them.

### How deep to buy

Deeper than the rest of the year, but not as deep as your enthusiasm. A rule that works for sellers we know: buy Q4 stock in two rounds. Round one in September, based on last year's charts, shipped to arrive by the end of October. Round two in early November, replenishing only what round one has proved sells, shipped to arrive by the end of November. The second round is where the profit is, because it is bought with evidence.

Do not spend the whole pot in round one. Cash you still have in November buys the products that are selling; cash spent in September on products that are not selling is gone until January.

### Black Friday behaviour

Black Friday is the last Friday in November (27 November in 2026) and Cyber Monday follows. It matters to an arbitrage seller in two directions.

**As a seller.** Sales rise through the week and peak over the weekend, and continue strongly through the first half of December. Prices on competitive listings tend to fall as sellers with too much stock chase the Buy Box. Set your floor prices before the week starts and do not let a repricer take you under them in the noise.

**As a buyer.** Black Friday is the best sourcing week of the year. Retailers discount across whole categories, often for the month, with the deepest cuts in the last week and a second wave on Cyber Monday. Stock bought at Black Friday prices sells at ordinary December prices, and some of it sells at ordinary March prices. Prepare a list of retailers and products in advance, sign up to the retailers' mailing lists in early November so you see early access, and set the price alerts on your price history tool for products you already sell. Then on the day, buy from the list, not from the homepage. Buy limits per customer are common; respect them.

Stock bought on Black Friday will not reach Amazon before the inbound deadline. That is fine. It arrives in the second week of December and sells through Christmas and into January.

### Pricing towards Christmas

Prices rise into mid December on products that are running out and fall on products that are not. The listing tells you which you are on. Look at three things: how fast the listing sells, how many sellers there are and how much stock they seem to have (a small offer count with stable prices is strong; a growing offer count with falling prices is weak).

- **Strong listing, stock you want to sell before Christmas:** hold your price or raise it gently as the competition sells out. Do not chase the lowest offer; the Buy Box rotates and the demand is there.
- **Weak listing:** get out early. Undercut in the first week of December and recover the cash rather than fight for a share of a falling price in the third.
- **Stock you are happy to hold into January:** ignore the noise, price for margin, sell what sells.

### The last shipment before Christmas

Stock has to be received to sell, and receiving is slowest in December. The last shipment that reliably sells before Christmas leaves you around the second week of December, and even that is a gamble in a busy year. The Christmas delivery cut-off for customers is a few days before the 25th. After that, anything you send in is January stock, so only send it if it will sell in January at January prices. The temptation to buy one more round in mid December is how people end up with a spare room of gift sets in February.

### After Christmas

Between Christmas and New Year, sales continue: gift cards are spent, returns are exchanged, and the sales that start on Boxing Day pull buyers back. Prices on gift categories drop sharply in the first week of January as everyone with leftover stock lowers at once. If you still hold seasonal stock on 27 December, either price to clear it in that week or accept you are holding it until next autumn (with storage the whole way). Do not sit in the middle.

### Storage in December

October to December storage is roughly double the rest of the year, and from 15 October the peak fee applies to every unit sold. Two disciplines: send only what will sell before mid January, and remove or clear anything slow before the 15 October snapshot rather than paying peak rates to hold it through the busiest quarter. Aged inventory surcharges are assessed on the 15th of each month; check the age report in early October.

## January

### Is it really slow

Slower than December, and busier than most new sellers fear. The first week is often strong (gift card spending, sales). The dip comes in the second half of January and runs into February, when people have less money and less reason to shop. For a seller in their first year, January can still be a growth month because you are adding products; for an established seller it is a month where sales fall against December and you should plan cash accordingly.

The useful way to think about it: December's profit funds Q1's stock. If you take December's money out of the business in January, Q1 is thin. If you put it back in, Q1 is when you build the range that sells all year.

### Returns season

January is when December's gifts come back. Return rates rise across gift categories, and the returns arrive as refunds against sales you thought were done, often three or four weeks after the order. Two things follow. Keep a cash reserve through January rather than spending December's payout to the last pound. And run the returns review in [where the money leaks](/guides/amazon-where-the-money-leaks) properly in February, because the refund administration fee, the unrefunded fulfilment fee and unsellable returns all peak now.

### What to source in Q1

The categories change and the retailers are clearing. In rough order through the quarter:

- **Health, fitness and wellness** from the first week of January: supplements (mind the category rules), fitness accessories, water bottles, meal prep, sleep and stop smoking. The demand is strong for six weeks, then fades.
- **Organisation and home** in January: storage, cleaning, declutter products, planners.
- **Retailer clearance.** Christmas stock gets cleared in January at prices that can make it worth holding until next Q4 if you have the cash and it is not dated or meltable. Only do this with products whose charts show the same spike every year.
- **Valentine's Day (14 February).** Gift sets, fragrance, confectionery, cards and small jewellery. A short window; stock needs to be in by the end of January.
- **Mothering Sunday** in the UK falls three weeks before Easter, so it moves each year; check the date. Similar categories to Valentine's plus beauty and homeware. Stock in by mid February in a year with an early Easter.
- **Easter** confectionery, eggs and gifts. Easter moves; work backwards from the date.
- **Year round products.** The best thing to do in Q1 is build the list of replenishable products that sell every month, because that list carries the business through summer.

Price drops on Amazon itself are common in January, which makes Amazon to Amazon buys worth checking, and retailers discount deeply in the first half of the month.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying Q4 stock in October.** The deadline is the end of October for arrival, not for buying. September is the buying month.
- **Spending the whole pot in round one.** Cash in November buys proven sellers; cash spent in September on guesses is stuck.
- **Sending a big shipment in mid December.** It arrives in January and sells at January prices.
- **Holding seasonal stock into January at December prices.** Everyone lowers at once in the first week. Decide before Christmas.
- **Treating Prime Day sales as the new run rate.** The fortnight after is often slower than usual.
- **Forgetting peak fees and the October storage rate.** A thin margin in March is a loss in November.
- **Ignoring returns in January.** They arrive weeks after the sale, against cash you may have already spent.
- **Running deals as a reseller on a shared listing.** The fee is charged either way and the discount is usually deeper than the margin.

**Should a first year seller do Q4 at all?** Yes, on a modest scale. The lift is real and the learning is faster. Buy fewer products than you want to, ship early, and keep half the pot for November.
