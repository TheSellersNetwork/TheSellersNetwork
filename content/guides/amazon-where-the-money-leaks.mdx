This is for sellers who are already trading on Amazon and whose profit tracker shows a healthy number that never quite turns up in the bank. Most of the gap is not one big mistake. It is a dozen small leaks, each too small to notice on its own, each running every month. After reading this you will know where each one is, how to measure it and how to close it, and you will have a checklist to run once a month so they do not open again.

If you are still setting up, start with [the working manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) and come back to this once you have a few months of sales to audit. Figures link to Amazon's own policy pages; check the current page before relying on any of them. Nothing here is tax, legal or financial advice.

## Why leaks matter more than sales

A new product might make you £2 a unit. A leak of £150 a month is the profit on 75 units, and it costs nothing to close except an hour of attention. Sellers we know who have gone through this exercise for the first time have usually found several hundred pounds a year in unclaimed reimbursements alone, and that is before subscriptions and storage.

The leaks fall into three groups. Money Amazon owes you and has not paid. Money you are paying that you do not need to. And money tied up in stock and systems that should be working harder. We take them in that order.

## Money Amazon owes you

### What Amazon is supposed to reimburse

Amazon's [FBA lost and damaged inventory reimbursement policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/200213130) says that if a unit is lost or damaged at a facility or by a carrier that Amazon operates, Amazon will replace it or reimburse you. That covers four situations, each with its own claim process:

| Where it went wrong | What it looks like in your account | Where to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shipment to Amazon | Fewer units received than you sent, shipment closed short | Shipping queue, the reconcile tab on each closed shipment |
| Fulfilment centre operations | Units marked lost, damaged or "disposed" in the warehouse | Inventory adjustments report, inventory ledger |
| Customer returns | A refund issued but the item never came back, or came back damaged by Amazon's carrier | FBA customer returns report against the refunds in your payments |
| Removals | Units you asked to be sent back that never arrived or arrived damaged | Removal order detail report |

Amazon reimburses a good proportion of these automatically. It does not find all of them, and the ones it misses are yours to claim.

### The 60 day window

Since 9 January 2025 the claim window for everything except shipment-to-Amazon claims is 60 days. Before that it was 18 months, which is why older advice tells you to audit once a year. That advice is now wrong. A lost unit you spot in month three is a lost unit you cannot claim for. Shipment claims have their own timing: you must wait for the shipment to be fully closed before you can reconcile, and there is a separate window after that, so check the [policy page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/200213130) for the current figure.

The practical effect: audit monthly, not annually.

### What you get back

Since 10 March 2025 Amazon reimburses pre-order losses (shipment, warehouse and removal claims) at what it calls the manufacturing cost of the item: the cost to acquire, purchase or produce it, excluding shipping, handling, customs duties and any other costs. Amazon estimates this figure itself from comparable products unless you tell it yours. The maximum reimbursement for a single unit of any item is £2,000.

This is where a quiet leak hides. Amazon's estimate is often below what you actually paid, and if you do nothing you get the estimate. There is a Manage Your Manufacturing Cost page inside the Inventory Defect and Reimbursement portal in Seller Central where you can enter your real cost per product, with an invoice as proof if asked. Do it for every product you send in. If you disagree with a reimbursement value after it is paid, you can dispute it through the Get support page within 90 days of the reimbursement.

Losses after an order (a customer return that never came back, for example) are reimbursed against the refund or sale price on that order minus fees and VAT. Amazon also lists the conditions it will not reimburse for on returns: damaged box or packaging, missing factory seal, missing packaging or a different box. Those are treated as customer damage, and customer damage is your cost.

### Returns that never returned

When a customer requests a return, Amazon usually refunds them on the spot. The customer then has a period in which to send the item back. If they never do, you are owed a reimbursement, and Amazon pays most of these automatically once the return window closes. Some slip through. The check is simple: take your refunds for a month, then look for each one in the FBA customer returns report. A refund with no matching returned unit and no reimbursement, older than the return window, is a claim.

### Fees charged wrongly

Every FBA fee is calculated from the dimensions and weight Amazon has on file for the product. If Amazon's measurement is wrong, every unit you sell is overcharged. Look at the fee preview report and compare the size tier and weight against a ruler and a set of scales. If Amazon has a small item down as a standard parcel, request a re-measure through Seller Support. Unlike lost units there is no 60 day window on the mistake itself, but past overcharges are only refunded for a limited period, so catch it early.

### How to audit reimbursements monthly

1. Open the inventory adjustments report for the last 60 days. Every line marked lost, damaged or destroyed should have a matching reimbursement line in your payments. List the ones that do not.
2. Open every shipment closed in the period. Compare units sent with units received. Reconcile any shortage with your proof of quantity (packing list, carrier weight, photos of the packed boxes).
3. Match last month's refunds to the customer returns report. Flag refunds with no return and no reimbursement beyond the return window.
4. Open your removal orders. Confirm every unit arrived.
5. Check the reimbursement report. For each payment, compare the amount with your cost price. Dispute the ones that are short, with an invoice.
6. File the claims. One at a time, with all the information asked for. Amazon's policy warns that submitting premature or high volumes of requests can get your support slowed down or your account looked at, so make each claim clean rather than firing off dozens.

An hour a month. Sellers with a few hundred units a month typically find something every time.

## Customer returns

### What a return actually costs

The refund itself is not the cost. When a customer returns an FBA item you get back the referral fee minus the [refund administration fee](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G21531), which is the lesser of £5 or 20% of the referral fee. The FBA fulfilment fee is not refunded. The unit then comes back to the warehouse, where Amazon grades it. If it is sellable it goes back into your stock. If it is not, it sits in unfulfillable inventory until you remove or dispose of it, and either of those has a per unit fee on the [rate card](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing).

So a returned £20 item can easily cost you the fulfilment fee, the administration fee, a removal fee and the unit, against a sale that made £3. One return wipes out several sales.

### Read the reasons

Seller Central shows a return reason and often a customer comment for every FBA return. Read them monthly, and sort them into three piles:

- **The product's fault** (does not work, broken, missing parts, poor quality). If one product keeps appearing, it has a return rate problem that no calculator will show you. Stop buying it.
- **The listing's fault** (not as described, wrong size, wrong item). Either you matched your stock to the wrong listing, or the listing itself is wrong. Check your units against the detail page. If the listing is wrong, report it rather than keep selling on it (see [the product safety and listing accuracy guide](/guides/amazon-product-safety-and-listing-accuracy)).
- **Not the product's fault** (changed mind, found cheaper, ordered by mistake, arrived late, damaged in transit). Some of this is the cost of selling on a platform with generous returns. Damage in transit is Amazon's carrier, and a unit marked damaged on the way back is a reimbursement, not your loss.

### Evaluate high return products before you rebuy

Before reordering anything, look at its return rate over the last 90 days. A product that returns at 10% needs a margin roughly double what your calculator shows to be worth the same as a product returning at 2%. Clothing, footwear, anything sized, anything fragile and anything with a battery tends to sit at the high end. Some sellers refuse to stock anything with a return rate above a threshold they set; that is a sensible rule for a small account because a single unfulfillable return on a thin margin product wipes out a week of that product's profit.

### Unsellable returns

Do not let unfulfillable inventory sit. It is charged storage like anything else, and it is dead. Once a month, create a removal order to send it home, then triage it:

- Sealed and undamaged, mislabelled by the warehouse: relist and send back in.
- Opened but complete and working: sell it as used on another platform. Amazon's condition rules are strict about what "new" means; other platforms are less so, as long as you describe honestly.
- Faulty within the manufacturer's warranty: claim from the manufacturer, not Amazon.
- Bought from a retailer within its returns period: some retailers accept returns of opened goods; check the terms.
- Missing parts, and Amazon's grading says customer damage: this is a loss. Take it, record it and move on.

Keep a simple list of every returned unit and what happened to it. Without one, returned stock has a way of ending up in a cupboard at its full cost price on your balance sheet.

## Prep and shipping waste

Prep is an area where small amounts compound. The leaks we see most:

**Unplanned prep fees.** If a unit arrives without the label, poly bag or bubble wrap Amazon expected, Amazon may do it and charge you a per unit fee, or refuse the shipment. The rule for each product is shown when you create the shipment. Read it rather than assume.

**Carrier charges on split shipments.** Amazon may split a shipment plan across several warehouses. Each box you send has a cost. Where the plan gives you an option, choose the one with fewer destinations if the extra placement fee is lower than the carrier cost of the extra boxes. Work it out per shipment, not by habit.

**Boxes that are too big.** Partnered carrier rates are by weight and dimensions. A half empty box costs the same to ship as a full one. Buy boxes in two or three sizes rather than one.

**Shipping small quantities often.** Twenty boxes of five units cost more to ship than five boxes of twenty. Batch your sends weekly rather than daily unless a product is out of stock.

**Retailer delivery.** The cost of getting stock to you is part of your buy price. Free delivery thresholds and click and collect change a product's margin; put delivery into the calculator every time, not the average.

**Units lost in prep.** Count units in and out. If you or your prep centre send 48 when the invoice said 50, Amazon will never reimburse the two, because they never arrived. Reconcile the retailer invoice against the shipment before it leaves.

## Outsourcing done badly

Outsourcing is not a leak. Outsourcing before you have a process is. Four ways it goes wrong:

**A prep centre on thin margins.** A per unit prep fee that is fine on a £4 profit product is ruinous on a £1 product. Run the fee through the calculator per product, and prep the thin ones yourself or drop them. Check what the fee includes: receiving, labelling, bagging, boxing and forwarding may all be separate lines.

**A sourcing assistant without your criteria.** If you cannot write down exactly what makes a product a buy (minimum profit, minimum return on investment, maximum sellers, sales rank range, no Amazon on the listing, brand allowed, return rate cap), you cannot brief someone to find products, and you will pay for a list of things you would not have bought. Write the criteria first. Then pay someone to apply them.

**An admin assistant without a system.** Handing over your reimbursement audit, returns log or bookkeeping when none of them exists yet means paying someone to invent a system you cannot check. Build the spreadsheet, run it yourself for three months, then hand over the spreadsheet.

**Returns handling with no reconciliation.** A third party that receives your removal orders needs to report every unit and what happened to it. If they do not, units vanish and claims lapse. Ask for a monthly list, and compare it with your removal orders.

The test for any outsourcing: can you check the work in under ten minutes a week? If not, you are not managing it, you are hoping.

## Stock that sits

Storage is charged per cubic foot per month. The [rate card](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) shows standard size stock at £0.76 per cubic foot a month from January to September and £1.51 from October to December, and Amazon adds an aged inventory surcharge to units that have sat too long, assessed on a snapshot on the 15th of each month, with the rate rising through age bands. The exact bands and rates are on the fee pages in Seller Central and change; check them before you decide to hold something.

The bigger cost is not the fee. It is the money. £500 of stock that takes six months to sell has earned you one margin in six months. The same £500 turned monthly earns six. Slow stock is the most expensive kind because it stops you buying fast stock.

**How to find it.** The inventory age report and the FBA inventory dashboard both show units by age band and Amazon's own estimate of excess. Sort by days in stock, oldest first.

**What to do with it.** In order of preference: reprice to move it before the next age band, even at a smaller profit; create a removal order and sell it elsewhere; dispose of it. The one thing not to do is leave it because you paid £8 and it is "worth" £8. It is worth what it will sell for this month.

**What to change.** If the same kind of product keeps ageing, your buying criteria are wrong. Buy fewer units per product, more products.

## Overheads creep

Every subscription started because it was going to save time or make money. Some still do. Once a month, list every recurring charge on the business bank account and card, and next to each one write the last time you used it and what it did for you. Price history, calculator, repricer and a bookkeeping tool are typical. A second repricer, a keyword tool you used once, a course platform, an extra user seat: cancel.

Two more overheads that hide:

- **VAT on Amazon's fees.** Since 1 August 2024 every Amazon fee carries 20% VAT. If you are not VAT registered it is a cost, and if your calculator is not set to include it every margin is overstated by the same amount.
- **Interest.** A business credit card that is not cleared in full each month charges interest that no arbitrage margin covers. Treat any interest line on a statement as a leak to close that month.

## Invoice gaps

An invoice you cannot find costs money twice. Amazon can ask for supplier invoices for brand approval, for an authenticity complaint or to release stock, and a missing invoice means stock you cannot sell. HMRC needs it for your expenses, and if you become VAT registered, VAT on stock bought before registration can often be reclaimed only against a proper invoice.

The fix is a habit, not a tool: every purchase gets its invoice saved on the day, named by supplier and date, in one folder. Retailer order confirmations are not invoices; download the VAT invoice from the account page. Once a month, match the bank statement to the folder. Anything on the statement with no invoice, chase before the retailer's records age out.

## The monthly leak audit

Run this in the first week of each month for the previous month. About an hour once you are used to it.

| Check | Where | What you are looking for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lost and damaged units | Inventory adjustments, inventory ledger | Adjustments in the last 60 days with no reimbursement |
| Short shipments | Shipping queue, closed shipments | Received fewer than sent; reconcile with proof |
| Refunds without returns | Payments refunds against FBA customer returns report | Refund, no return, no reimbursement, past the return window |
| Removals | Removal order detail | Units that never arrived |
| Reimbursement value | Reimbursements report | Payments below your cost price; dispute within 90 days |
| Manufacturing cost | Manage Your Manufacturing Cost | Every active product has your real cost entered |
| Fee accuracy | Fee preview report | Size tier or weight that does not match the item |
| Return reasons | Manage FBA returns | Products with repeat product fault or not as described |
| Unfulfillable stock | Inventory, unfulfillable | Anything older than a month: remove |
| Aged stock | Inventory age report | Units approaching the next surcharge band |
| Subscriptions | Bank and card statements | Anything not used last month |
| Interest and charges | Card statement | Any interest line |
| Invoices | Bank statement against invoice folder | Purchases with no invoice saved |
| Storage in Q4 | Inventory dashboard | Slow stock still in the warehouse at the October rate |

Log the pounds recovered each month. It is the most motivating number in the business, because it is profit you already earned.

## What goes wrong

- **Auditing annually because an old guide said so.** The window is 60 days. Anything older is gone.
- **Accepting Amazon's cost estimate.** Enter your real cost, with invoices ready, or every reimbursement is short.
- **Firing off claims in bulk.** Amazon's policy treats insufficiently researched or high volume requests as interference. One clean claim beats ten rushed ones.
- **Rebuying a product without checking its return rate.** The calculator does not know it comes back one time in eight.
- **Leaving unfulfillable and aged stock because of what it cost.** It costs more every month it stays.
- **Outsourcing a process that does not exist yet.** Build it, run it, then hand it over with a way to check it.
- **Treating order confirmations as invoices.** Download the VAT invoice every time.

**Is a reimbursement service worth paying for?** Under a few hundred units a month, the audit above takes an hour and finds the same things. Above that, a service that takes a percentage of what it recovers can pay for itself, provided it respects the 60 day rule and does not file the bulk requests Amazon warns about on your account.
