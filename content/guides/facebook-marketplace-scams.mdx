This is for UK sellers on Facebook Marketplace, whether you clear out the loft or turn over stock every week. After reading it you will be able to recognise the scams that are aimed at sellers rather than buyers, have a set of rules that stop almost all of them, and know how to report one to Facebook, your bank and the police. The advice is drawn from Facebook's own help pages, MoneyHelper (the government-backed money guidance service), police.uk and the National Cyber Security Centre, each linked where it is used.

## The rule that stops most of them

**Do not let an item go until the money is in your account, and you have checked that by logging in to your bank yourself.** Not an email, not a screenshot, not a text, not the buyer's phone. Your banking app, opened by you.

police.uk puts it as: make sure you receive payment confirmation before sending goods, and always check your account online or ask your bank to make sure cleared funds have been received ([police.uk, online shopping](https://www.police.uk/advice/advice-and-information/fa/fraud/personal-fraud/online-shopping/)). MoneyHelper says the same: do not let a buyer take the item until the money is in your bank account, or ask for cash instead ([MoneyHelper, Facebook Marketplace scams](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/blog/scams-and-fraud/facebook-marketplace-scams-how-to-spot-fake-messages)).

Almost every scam below is a way to get you to break that rule.

## The scams

### Fake payment emails and screenshots

**How it works.** The buyer agrees your price and then sends a screenshot of a bank transfer, or you get an email that looks like it is from a payment service saying the money is waiting. Often there is a catch: you need to "upgrade" your account, "verify" as a seller, or pay a small fee before you can receive it.

**Why it works.** The screenshot looks real because it is easy to fake. The email uses the right logo.

**What Facebook says.** Its page [About scams on Facebook Marketplace](https://www.facebook.com/help/2374002556073992) warns against trusting screenshots of payments as confirmation, and against emails that appear to come from payment apps asking you to take some action, upgrade or pay a fee before you can accept payment. Always check your account to confirm payments.

**What to do.** No payment service charges you to receive money from a stranger. Delete it, check your own account, and do not reply to the email.

### "It can take a couple of hours to show"

**How it works.** A variation on the above, done in person. The buyer turns up, taps at their phone, shows you a confirmation screen, and says bank transfers can take a while to appear. MoneyHelper describes scammers asking sellers to put their bank details into a fake app and then showing a fake confirmation, sometimes using a fake banking website to back up the claim.

**What to do.** Say, politely, that the item stays until it shows in your account. A genuine buyer waits or pays cash. Someone who suddenly has to leave was not going to pay.

### The courier collection

**How it works.** The buyer is "away working" or "buying for a relative", cannot come, and will pay by a payment service and send a courier. The courier arrives, takes the item, and later the buyer tells the payment service they never received it. They get their money back, and you have no proof of delivery because you never posted it.

**What official sources say.** MoneyHelper describes exactly this, and its [types of scam](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/money-troubles/scams/types-of-scam) page says plainly: do not agree to let the buyer arrange their own courier to collect the item. Facebook's scam page lists buyers offering extra money to cover third-party movers as a sign of a scam.

**What to do.** Collection by the buyer in person, or you post it with tracking. Never a courier the buyer booked.

### Overpayment

**How it works.** The buyer "accidentally" pays too much, or offers more than your price and says the extra is for a courier or mover. Then they ask you to send the difference back. The original payment never existed, bounces, or is reversed, and you have sent real money.

**What official sources say.** Facebook lists both the accidental overpayment and the deliberate over-offer as common payment scams. MoneyHelper says if you receive an overpayment, speak to your bank and ask if the payment has been confirmed; if not, you have not received any money yet.

**What to do.** Never refund an overpayment until your bank confirms the original payment is real and settled. Safer still, refuse any payment above your asking price.

### Refund before payment

**How it works.** The buyer says they have paid, then asks for a refund because they changed their mind or paid twice, before any money has actually arrived.

**What to do.** Facebook's advice is to avoid giving refunds before checking your account to confirm payment. If nothing arrived, there is nothing to refund.

### Verification codes

**How it works.** A buyer, often a new account, asks you to "prove you are real" by reading back a code sent to your phone or email. The code is actually a login or account recovery code for your Facebook, email or another account. Hand it over and they take the account, then use it to scam your friends or list fake items under your name.

**What official sources say.** Facebook's scam page lists requests for verification codes sent to your phone or email, such as two-factor codes, as phishing. MoneyHelper says never give out passwords or authentication codes.

**What to do.** Never read a code to anyone, for any reason. No genuine buyer needs one. If you have already given a code, change your passwords straight away, starting with email, and check the security settings on the account.

### Links to "receive your payment"

**How it works.** A message or email with a link to a page that looks like a delivery company, a payment service or Facebook itself, asking you to enter card or bank details so the buyer's payment can be released or a delivery booked.

**What official sources say.** Facebook calls these off-platform link scams and link manipulation, and says never to share card or banking details. police.uk says to check emails by contacting the company directly using details you already know, or by logging in to your account yourself, not through the links or chat in the message.

**What to do.** You never need to give card details to receive money. Do not click.

### Moving off Facebook

**How it works.** The buyer asks to carry on by text, WhatsApp or email, early in the conversation. Or you are asked to deal with someone other than the account holder.

**Why it matters.** Facebook says conversations moved off Facebook and Messenger make scams harder to detect. It is also where the fake payment emails and links arrive.

**What to do.** Keep all messages in Messenger until the item is paid for and collected.

### After the sale

MoneyHelper also warns about scams that happen after the sale: the buyer reporting the payment as fraud, attempting a chargeback, or claiming the item never arrived. Keep evidence of the handover or delivery. For posted items, that means tracking. For collections, see [selling on collection safely](/guides/facebook-marketplace-collection-safety).

## Red flags at a glance

| Sign | Why it is a warning |
| --- | --- |
| Buyer agrees your price without looking or asking anything | They are not buying the item |
| Buyer cannot collect and will send a courier | Classic courier scam |
| Offers more than you asked | Overpayment scam |
| Wants to move to WhatsApp, text or email early | Facebook's own warning sign |
| Asks for a code sent to your phone | Account takeover |
| Sends a link, or an email "from" a payment service | Phishing |
| Urgency: "need it today", "pay now or I go elsewhere" | Pushing you past your checks |
| Account with no friends, no photo, created recently | Facebook's and police.uk's warning signs |
| Any request involving gift cards | Facebook lists gift card payment requests as a scam |

Facebook's advice for when you see any of these: stop communicating with the person and report them.

## How to report it

### To Facebook

From Facebook's [Report a Facebook Marketplace scam](https://www.facebook.com/help/1295340050874305) page, on a computer:

1. Go to Marketplace, then Selling, then Seller dashboard.
2. Open the listing the person contacted you about, then the message thread.
3. Choose More options, then Report, then Scam, and follow the instructions.

You can report a listing as a scam from the listing itself. If the person has blocked you, Facebook's help centre has a route for reporting something you cannot see.

### Suspicious emails and texts

The National Cyber Security Centre asks you to forward scam emails to report@phishing.gov.uk ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/report-scam-email)) and scam texts to 7726, a free service most phone providers support ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/report-scam-text-message)).

### If you have lost money or goods

1. **Call your bank straight away** if money has left your account, or if you shared bank details. Use the number on your card, not one from a message.
2. **Report it to the police.** Report Fraud, run by the City of London Police, has replaced Action Fraud as the national reporting service for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Report online at [reportfraud.police.uk](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/reporting-a-fraud/) or call 0300 123 2040 ([police.uk](https://www.police.uk/advice/advice-and-information/fa/fraud/personal-fraud/online-shopping/)). In Scotland, contact Police Scotland on 101. If a crime is happening now, call 999.
3. **Secure your accounts** if you gave out a code or password. MoneyHelper suggests changing passwords on email, Facebook and anything else the scammer could reach, and watching your bank account.
4. **Report the account to Facebook** so it can be removed.

MoneyHelper runs a financial crimes and scams line on 0800 015 4402 if you need help working out what to do next.

### Bank transfer scams where you paid

If you were tricked into sending money, for example refunding an overpayment, MoneyHelper says banks must now refund most people scammed into making a bank transfer, but may deduct up to £100, and may refuse if you are found to have been extremely careless. Tell your bank quickly. This is general information, not financial or legal advice; your bank will tell you how the rules apply to you.

## Rules to sell by

1. Money in your account, checked in your own banking app, before the item goes. Or cash, checked.
2. No couriers booked by the buyer.
3. No refunds of overpayments. Refuse anything above your price.
4. No codes, no passwords, no links.
5. Keep every message in Messenger.
6. Evidence of every handover: tracking, or a message confirming collection.

## What goes wrong

- **Trusting a screenshot because the buyer seemed nice.** Screenshots prove nothing.
- **Letting the item go "because the transfer is pending".** Pending is not paid.
- **Agreeing to a courier to save the buyer a trip.**
- **Sending back an overpayment the same day.**
- **Reading out a code "just this once".** It only takes once.
- **Not reporting.** The account moves on to the next seller.
