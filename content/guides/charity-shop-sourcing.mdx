This is for resellers who buy from charity shops, or who are thinking about it and wondering whether it is fair to the charity. It covers the ethics plainly, how to plan a route, what to check on the rail and the shelf, how to build a good relationship with the people who run the shop, and the records to keep. The comparison with other sources is in [sourcing stock for resale](/guides/sourcing-for-resale); this guide is the detail for one of them.

## Is it fair to the charity

It is a fair question and it deserves a straight answer.

A charity shop exists to turn donations into money for the charity. When you buy an item at the price the shop set, the charity gets exactly what it asked for. The shop has chosen the price, and it has already chosen not to spend more time researching it. Buying from a charity shop to resell is legal and, done properly, does the charity no harm. Many shops sell far more to resellers than they realise, and the stock turns over faster because of it.

Where it becomes unfair is in behaviour, not in the buying. The lines sellers we know hold:

- **Pay the price on the ticket.** Haggling in a charity shop takes money from the charity. If you think the price is too high, do not buy.
- **Do not strip the shop.** Clearing an entire rail or section as soon as it goes out leaves nothing for other customers, who are the shop's main income. Buy what you will sell, not everything that might.
- **Do not swap tickets, hide items or ask for things to be held unfairly.** These are dishonest, and in the case of ticket swapping, theft.
- **Be honest if asked.** If a volunteer asks whether you resell, say yes. Most shops are relaxed about it, and some are glad of a regular customer.
- **Donate back.** Items that do not sell, and your own unwanted things, can go back into the shop you bought from. It keeps the relationship healthy and the stock circulating.

If a shop has a stated policy on resellers, on buying in bulk or on reserving items, follow it.

## Plan a route

Stock is random from day to day but predictable over weeks. The way to get a reliable supply from a random source is volume and routine.

### Build a list of shops

- **Map every charity shop you can reach in a morning.** A town centre may have several close together; a group of nearby towns may give you ten or more.
- **Visit each at least twice before judging it.** One visit shows you that day's stock, not the shop.
- **Note what each shop is good at.** One branch will be strong on books, another on branded clothing, another on homeware. Stock depends on the donors in the area and on the manager's decisions.
- **Note how each shop prices.** Some price low to move stock; some check values online and price close to resale. The second type still has bargains, but only in things the checker did not recognise.

### Fix a rhythm

- **Visit the best shops on the same day each week.** Many shops put out new stock on a pattern, and a regular visitor sees it first. Ask, politely, when new stock tends to go out; many staff will tell you.
- **Keep a route that fits the time you have.** Five shops in an hour and a half that you actually visit every week beat fifteen you reach once a month.
- **Watch the calendar.** Donations rise when people clear out: after Christmas, in spring and at the end of the school year. See the [UK reselling calendar](/guides/selling-calendar-uk) for what to look for when.

### Online and specialist charity shops

Many charities also sell donated items through their own online shops and through the main marketplaces. Those items are priced with sold prices in mind, so they are rarely a source of stock. Specialist charity shops (books, furniture, electricals, vintage or designer) price closer to market but carry stock you will not see elsewhere; treat them as a different kind of shop with thinner margins.

## What to look for

Every seller's list is different, and the best list is one built from your own sales. As a starting point, charity shops often carry:

- **Clothing and shoes** in good condition from brands that sell second-hand. Check the recent sold prices for the brand and item type, not the brand alone.
- **Books** in categories that hold value: specialist non-fiction, textbooks in current editions, and collectable editions. Most paperback fiction sells for little.
- **Homeware and kitchenware** from known makers, sets and pieces that complete sets.
- **Media and games** that are complete and in good condition.
- **Craft supplies, fabric and haberdashery,** which often come in as whole collections.
- **Collectables** in whatever category you know well.

The rule for anything unfamiliar: **check the sold price on your phone before you buy**. Asking prices online tell you what people hope for; sold prices tell you what buyers paid. See [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps). If you would not be content to sell at the low end of the sold range after fees and postage, leave it on the shelf.

## What to check before you buy

Charity shops generally accept returns on their own terms, but most resellers treat a charity shop purchase as final. Check at the shelf.

### Clothing

| Check | What you are looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Armpits, collars, cuffs | Marks, yellowing, bobbling and wear |
| Seams and hems | Splits, loose threads, alterations |
| Zips, buttons, fastenings | All present and working |
| Against the light | Moth holes and thin patches |
| Smell | Damp, smoke and perfume, which are hard to remove |
| Labels | Size and care labels present; brand label intact |
| Measurements | Measure if the size label is missing or the fit is unusual; see [measuring clothes for listings](/guides/measuring-clothes-for-listings) |

### Branded and designer items

Charity shops receive counterfeits, usually from donors who did not know or did not care. Shops do their best to remove them but some reach the shelf. The law on selling counterfeits applies to you as a trader wherever you bought the item. Check labels, stitching, hardware and packaging against a genuine example before you buy anything where the brand is the value. The checks are in [spotting fakes before you buy](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy).

### Books

- Check the edition and printing if that is where the value is.
- Look for water damage, missing pages, heavy underlining and ex-library stamps, and price accordingly.
- For textbooks, check the edition is current; old editions often sell for very little.

### Homeware, media and games

- Look for chips, cracks and crazing on ceramics and glass; run a finger round rims.
- Open cases and check discs and cartridges.
- Count pieces in games and puzzles, or price them as incomplete.

### Electricals and children's items

Anything you sell as a trader must be safe. The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's guidance on [second-hand electrical goods](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/product-safety/second-hand-electrical-goods) describes the checks for electricals, and it is worth checking items against the [GOV.UK product safety alerts and recalls](https://www.gov.uk/product-safety-alerts-reports-recalls) list before you list them. Toys and children's clothing carry their own safety rules. If you cannot be confident an item is safe, do not buy it for resale.

## Building relationships

The people who run charity shops decide what goes out, when, and at what price. A good relationship does not get you special prices, and should not; it gets you information and a friendly welcome, which over months is worth a great deal.

- **Be pleasant and unhurried.** Say hello, say thank you, learn names over time.
- **Do not crowd the stockroom door or follow volunteers as they put stock out.** It is uncomfortable for them and other customers.
- **Pay promptly and do not take up the till for long.** If you are buying a lot, wait until the shop is quiet.
- **Donate.** Your unsold stock and your own unwanted items are useful to them.
- **Tell them about things they could price higher, if they ask.** Some sellers we know do this and find it builds trust rather than costing them. It is your choice; it is never an obligation.
- **Respect a no.** If a shop does not want to put items aside, tell you when stock goes out, or sell to resellers in quantity, accept it.

A shop that knows you as a regular, polite customer is more likely to mention that a large donation came in on Tuesday. That is the whole of the relationship, and it is enough.

## Keeping records

Charity shop purchases are some of the easiest to record, because every till gives you a receipt.

- **Keep every receipt.** It is dated, names the shop and shows the price. That is your proof of purchase for tax and for any platform or buyer who asks where an item came from.
- **Photograph receipts the same day.** Till receipts fade. A dated photograph in a folder on your phone lasts.
- **Log the trip.** Date, shops visited, total spend, number of items, and miles. The [sourcing log spreadsheet](/tools/downloads) records each buy against the shop and shows which sources make money once items sell. If you claim mileage using simplified expenses, the flat rate for cars and goods vehicles is 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026, then 25p ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/vehicles)).
- **Give every item a stock number and cost when you get home.** Write the stock number on the receipt, so a receipt can be matched to its items. See [storage and stock numbers](/guides/storage-and-stock-numbers).
- **Review each shop monthly.** Spend, items, what those items made, and time. Some shops will turn out to be worth twice the visits; some will drop off the route.

Once you are buying to resell, you are trading, and trading income over £1,000 in a tax year means registering for Self Assessment ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader)). The detail, including what you can claim, is in [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers). None of this is tax advice.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying on brand alone.** A brand that sells well in one item type can sell badly in another. Check the sold price for the item, not the label.
- **Skipping the checks.** A mark under the arm or a moth hole turns a profit into a return.
- **Haggling.** It takes money from the charity and costs you goodwill.
- **Stripping the shop.** Short-term stock, long-term cold welcome.
- **Too many shops, visited irregularly.** Stock you saw a fortnight ago has gone.
- **No records.** Faded receipts and no trip log mean no way to tell which shops pay.
- **Buying counterfeits because the shop sold them.** The shop's mistake becomes your liability once you list it.
- **Forgetting your time.** Two hours for three items is fine if they sell well; it is not a business if they do not.
