This is for Amazon resellers who use a price history tool when sourcing and want to read the chart properly rather than trusting the summary numbers beside it. After reading you will be able to look at a chart and say, with reasons, what price the product really sells at, roughly how often it sells, how much of that you are likely to get, and whether anything on the chart is a warning. [The online arbitrage manual](/guides/amazon-online-arbitrage#reading-a-price-history-chart) introduces each line; this guide goes further into reading them together and spotting the traps.

Amazon publishes very little about how rank is calculated, and nothing about how any third-party tool estimates sales. Where Amazon does say something, we link it. Everything else here is reading technique, not fact about the algorithm.

## What the chart is made of

Price history tools record data from the Amazon listing at intervals and draw it over time. The details differ between tools, but most show some or all of these:

| Line or panel | What it records | What it is good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amazon price | Amazon's own retail offer, when in stock | Whether and when Amazon competes with you |
| Lowest new price, third party | The cheapest new offer from a marketplace seller, sometimes split into FBA and merchant fulfilled | The floor other sellers are prepared to go to |
| Buy Box price | The price of the featured offer | What customers actually pay, and what you will be paid |
| Used and other conditions | Lowest used or collectible offer | Rarely relevant for new stock |
| Sales rank | The Best Sellers Rank in the main category | How recently and how often it sells |
| Offer count | Number of new offers | Competition, and its direction |
| Buy Box share or holder | Who held the featured offer, over a period | Whether you would get a look in |

Many tools also print averages (30, 90, 180 day) and an estimated monthly sales figure. Read the chart first and the numbers second. An average can hide a spike, and an estimate is only as good as the model behind it.

## Choose the right time window

Set the chart to at least 90 days for every product, and to a full year for anything that might be seasonal or that you plan to hold for more than a month.

- **30 days** shows current conditions and nothing about whether they are normal.
- **90 days** shows whether the current price is typical and whether the offer count is rising or falling.
- **365 days** shows seasonality, last Q4, and whether this is a product that has one good month a year.

A product that looks excellent on 30 days and ordinary on 365 is usually in its good month. That is fine if you can sell through before it ends, and a trap if you cannot.

## The price lines, read together

### Start with the Buy Box line

The Buy Box price is what a customer pays when they press Add to Basket. It is the number your margin depends on. The lowest third-party price is often a merchant fulfilled offer with slow delivery, or a seller with one unit priced to clear, and Amazon may not feature it at all.

### Find where the price lives

Ignore the peaks. Look at where the Buy Box line spends most of its time over 90 days and use that as your expected selling price. If it has two levels (say, one with Amazon in stock and one without), plan on the lower one.

### Why you cannot plan on a spike

Spikes on a chart usually happen when Amazon or the main sellers run out, and the few remaining offers are priced high. They are real sales, but you cannot rely on them. Amazon's [fair pricing policy](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G5TUVJKZHUVMN77V?locale=en-GB) says a price significantly higher than recent prices on or off Amazon can lose the Buy Box or be removed. A product bought on the assumption that it will sell at the spike price usually cannot be listed there for long.

### Step down or dip

A **step down** (price drops and stays at the new level) means the market moved: a retailer cut its price, a wholesaler flooded the listing, or a new version appeared. Work from the new level.

A **dip and recovery** within a few weeks means someone cleared stock. It will probably happen again, possibly while your units are sitting there. Build a dip into your margin rather than hoping to avoid one.

## Sales rank: what it tells you

Amazon says the Best Sellers calculation is [based on Amazon sales and updated hourly](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GGGMZK378RQPATDJ) to reflect recent and historical sales. It also says it creates category and subcategory lists because the overall list does not show how an item sells against similar items. That is roughly all Amazon publishes. From it, and from how the line behaves, you can take four things:

1. **Lower is better.** A rank of 1 is the best seller in that category.
2. **Rank is relative to the category.** It measures the product against the others in the same list, so the same number means different things in different categories.
3. **Recent sales matter.** A sale moves the rank down (better). With no sales, rank drifts up (worse).
4. **The line is a sales record.** Each sharp drop in a slow product's rank line is usually a sale.

### Counting drops

For a slow or medium seller, count the distinct drops in rank over 30 days. That is a rough count of the days it sold, not of units: two units sold in the same hour look like one drop. For a fast seller, the line is a dense band and drops cannot be counted; there, the level of the band is what matters.

### Comparing like with like

Learn the rank ranges in the two or three categories you actually buy in. Keep a note of products you have sold, their typical rank, and how many you sold a month. After a few months you will have better numbers for your categories than any rule of thumb.

## Sales rank: what it does not tell you

- **How many units.** Rank is a position, not a count. Estimated sales figures on tools are modelled, and are least reliable for products that sell in bursts.
- **Who sold them.** A drop could be Amazon, a merchant fulfilled seller or you. The rank belongs to the listing, not to any offer.
- **Price at the time.** A rank drop at a low price tells you people buy it at the low price.
- **Why.** A deal, a TV mention, a competitor going out of stock, a school term starting. Check the price line at the same moment.
- **Future sales.** A rank that was excellent for six weeks last December says nothing about March.

Amazon now shows its own sales band on many listings ("bought in past month"). Its [Sales Trend FAQ](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/help/hub/reference/external/G2CRYWPU52D7TGAK) says it is Amazon's own transaction data, refreshed at least daily, excluding returns and cancellations. Use it as a check on the rank line and any tool estimate. It is the listing's total, shared between every seller, and it is the bottom of a band.

## Offer count

The offer count line tells you how crowded the listing is and whether it is getting more or less so.

| Pattern | Likely meaning | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rising offers, falling price | A deal was found and many sellers bought it | You are probably late. Pass, or buy very few |
| Falling offers, steady price | Sellers are selling out and not restocking | Often a quiet opportunity if you can buy at the right cost |
| Steady offers, steady price | A settled listing | Your share is roughly your fraction of the qualifying offers |
| Offers jump on the same day every year | Seasonal stock or a known retailer sale | Expect the price to follow |
| Very few offers for a long time with no Amazon | Hard to source, gated, or a brand that restricts sellers | Check why before you buy |

Offer counts can include merchant fulfilled offers with long delivery times and offers priced far above the rest. What matters is how many offers are competing at or near the Buy Box price.

## The Buy Box and your share

### Rotation

Amazon's [featured offer eligibility page](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G200418100) sets out who can be featured. Among eligible offers that are close on price and performance, the featured offer moves between them over time. [Seller feedback and the Buy Box](/guides/amazon-seller-feedback-and-buy-box#it-rotates) explains how this works in practice.

For reading a chart, the practical point is simple. If a listing sells about 60 a month and there are four FBA offers at the featured price with good metrics, you might expect roughly 15 a month if nothing changes. If one of the four is Amazon priced below what you can match, your share is smaller.

### Reading Buy Box history

Where the tool shows who held the Buy Box and for what share of the period:

- **Amazon holding it most of the time** means Amazon is in stock and usually at or below the price you need. Only buy if your cost lets you match Amazon's price and still profit.
- **Amazon in and out of stock** shows as gaps in the Amazon price line with third-party sellers winning in between. Profitable if your stock is there during the gaps, which you cannot guarantee.
- **Shared fairly evenly between several FBA sellers** is the normal, healthy case.
- **One seller holding it nearly all the time** at the same price as others may mean that seller has better metrics, or may mean something about the listing you cannot see. Be cautious.

## Red flags

Any one of these is a reason to look harder. Two together are usually a reason to walk away.

1. **The Buy Box line has been falling for 90 days.** You will sell into a lower price than today's.
2. **Offer count doubled in the last month.** You are joining a queue.
3. **Amazon has held the Buy Box continuously for months** at a price you cannot match.
4. **The price you need only appears at the peaks.** You are pricing on the exception.
5. **Rank is good but the price line has gaps.** Sales may be Amazon's alone, or the product may go out of stock everywhere for reasons such as a recall.
6. **Rank history is short or missing.** A new or merged listing. No history means no evidence.
7. **Only one seller, ever, and it is the brand.** A reseller joining that listing is the classic route to an intellectual property complaint.
8. **The rank improved sharply once, last Q4, and never since.** Seasonal. Only worth buying for this Q4, sized for it.
9. **The listing title, image or pack size has changed recently.** The history you are reading may be for a different product.
10. **The product is gated, hazmat, dated or Transparency protected.** None of these appear on the chart. Check them in Seller Central on the exact listing before buying. [Dangerous goods review](/guides/amazon-dangerous-goods-review) explains why a hazmat flag can strand stock for weeks.

## A routine for each product

Before buying, with the chart set to a year:

1. Where does the Buy Box price live over 90 days? That is your planned selling price.
2. Is there a Q4 pattern, and are you buying for it?
3. How many rank drops in the last 30 days, and does the Amazon sales band agree?
4. How many offers are competing at the Buy Box price, and in which direction is that number moving?
5. Is Amazon on the listing, and at what price against your cost?
6. Any of the ten red flags?
7. Only now, run the calculator at the planned selling price, with Q4 fees if it will sell after 15 October. [Q4 on Amazon](/guides/amazon-q4-peak-season) has the 2026 figures.

Write your answers down for every product you buy. Compare them three months later with what actually happened. That comparison is the fastest way to get better at this.

## What goes wrong

- **Reading the lowest price instead of the Buy Box price.** The lowest offer is often one nobody buys.
- **Using a 30 day window.** It shows you this month and nothing else.
- **Trusting the estimated sales number.** It is a model, and it is the listing's total, not yours.
- **Pricing on a spike.** The fair pricing policy stops you listing there for long.
- **Treating rank as comparable across categories.** It is not.
- **Buying because the chart looks busy.** A busy rank line with ten new sellers is a busy queue.
- **Forgetting what the chart cannot show.** Gating, hazmat, dates and Transparency are all checked elsewhere.
