This is for resellers who buy at car boot sales, or want to, and would like to come home with stock that sells rather than a bag of things that seemed like a good idea at seven in the morning. It covers when to arrive, what to take, how to haggle, how to check items at the pitch, and the paperwork that makes a boot sale purchase a proper business purchase. The wider comparison of sources is in [sourcing stock for resale](/guides/sourcing-for-resale); this guide is the detail for one of them.

## The numbers you will need

Car boot sales are informal and mostly unregulated as to price, so there are few official figures. The ones that matter are about what happens after you buy.

| Thing | Figure or rule | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When you count as a trader at a boot sale | Buying to resell, selling regularly, or making significant income are signs you are trading | [Business Companion: car boot sales](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/car-boot-sales-s) (Chartered Trading Standards Institute) |
| Tax once you are trading | Register for Self Assessment if trading income is over £1,000 in a tax year | [GOV.UK: become a sole trader](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader) |
| Mileage if you claim simplified expenses | 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026, then 25p | [GOV.UK: simplified expenses, vehicles](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/vehicles) |
| Selling counterfeits | Criminal offence; up to 10 years in prison or an unlimited fine | [GOV.UK: IP crime and enforcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses/ip-crime-and-enforcement-for-businesses) |

Entry charges, opening times and early-entry rules are set by each organiser. Check the organiser's own page or social media before you go; they change with the season and the weather. None of this is tax or legal advice.

## Early bird or late

Most boot sales have two useful windows for a buyer: the first hour, and the last hour. The middle of the morning is for browsing.

| | Early | Late |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you get | First pick of everything, including the items a seller has underpriced | Sellers who do not want to reload the car, and the best prices on bulky or bulk items |
| What you pay | Higher prices, sometimes an extra early-entry fee, and competition from every other reseller | Picked-over stock; the best items are gone |
| Best for | Branded clothing, collectables, media, anything where the value is in spotting it | Job lots, furniture, garden items, boxes of mixed stock, anything you can sort at home |
| Risk | Buying fast and checking badly | Wasting a morning on leftovers |

**Our recommendation:** go early for the first visits to a new sale, until you know what it has. Once you know the sale, match the timing to what you sell. If your stock is branded clothing or collectables, early is the only time worth going. If you sell volume at low prices, the last hour is where the margin is.

### The first-hour routine

- **Arrive before the gates open.** Many sales let buyers in as sellers set up; others hold everyone back until a fixed time. Know which before you go.
- **Walk the whole field fast first.** A quick lap tells you where the stock is. Buy on the first lap only if an item would be gone in five minutes: an obvious find at a price you would pay twice over.
- **Watch the unloading.** Sellers put boxes on the ground before they put things on tables. Asking "anything in there you are selling?" is normal, and many sellers are happy to let you look. Do not rummage in a box or a car without being invited.
- **Second lap, slower.** Check, haggle, buy.

### The last-hour routine

- **Look for sellers who are packing.** A seller loading the car has decided the stock is not worth taking home.
- **Offer for the lot.** "What would you take for the whole box?" is often answered with a price well below the items separately, because the alternative is carrying it.
- **Have a plan for the leftovers.** A box of twenty items of which you want five means fifteen for the charity shop, the bin or your own boot sale. Price the five and ignore the fifteen.

## Cash

Most car boot sellers take cash only. Some take card or bank transfer by phone; do not rely on it.

- **Bring coins and small notes.** A seller who cannot change a £20 note for a £2 item often gives up the sale or rounds the price in their favour. Aim for a spread of coins, fivers and tens.
- **Keep the float in two places.** A small amount in an easy pocket for quick purchases; the rest somewhere safer. It also makes haggling easier: "that's all I've got on me" only works if it is true.
- **Set a budget before you go.** Car boots make it easy to spend a little at a time and a lot in total. Decide the maximum for the morning and stop when it is gone.
- **Record the spend as you go.** A note on your phone after each purchase ("pitch near the gate, jacket and two books, £6") takes seconds. Trying to remember at home does not work.
- **Cash from the business.** If you have a separate business account, draw the float from it, so the records match.

If a seller offers a bank transfer to someone you have never met, be aware that bank transfers are hard to reverse. For anything more than a few pounds, cash in hand for goods in hand is the safest form.

## Haggling etiquette

Haggling is expected at car boot sales, but it is a conversation, not a contest. Sellers are mostly households, not traders, and many will remember you next week.

**Do**

- **Ask the price first.** "How much is this?" Many sellers name a price lower than you would have offered.
- **Offer a reasonable amount.** A modest reduction on a single item, or a round figure for several. Offers far below the asking price usually end the conversation.
- **Bundle.** "Three for a fiver?" is easier for a seller to accept than a cut on each item.
- **Be friendly and quick.** Smile, make an offer, accept the answer.
- **Walk away politely.** "No problem, thanks" leaves room to come back at the end of the morning, when the price may be different.

**Do not**

- **Criticise the item to get the price down.** It rarely works and it annoys people.
- **Tell the seller what it is worth online.** It is not illegal to pay a low price for something valuable, and many resellers would not do otherwise, but announcing the resale value costs you the item and the goodwill.
- **Haggle over very small amounts.** A 50p item is already cheap.
- **Pay less than agreed.** Once a price is agreed, pay it.

There is an ethical line worth holding for yourself. If a seller is clearly unaware that something is valuable and asks you directly what it is worth, give an honest answer or walk away. Few resellers ever face the question; it is worth knowing your answer before you do.

## What to carry

| Item | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Phone, charged, with a portable power bank | Sold price checks, a torch for early mornings, notes, photos of receipts |
| Cash float, as above | Most sellers take nothing else |
| Strong bags or a folding trolley | Carrying stock around a field is the tiring part |
| A tape measure | Clothing measurements, furniture sizes, checking parcel size for postage |
| A small torch | Checking labels, stitching and inside boxes before the sun is up |
| Batteries in common sizes | Testing battery-powered items on the spot, with the seller's permission |
| A notebook or notes app | Recording purchases and prices |
| Hand gel and wipes | Boxes and tables are dirty |
| Weather gear | A wet morning is still a working morning |

A phone with signal is the most important item on the list. Before you buy anything you do not know well, check the recent sold prices on the platform you will sell it on. Use sold prices, not asking prices; see [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps).

## Checking items before you pay

Car boot purchases have no comeback in practice. Check everything at the pitch.

### Clothing and shoes

- Check armpits, collars and cuffs for marks and wear.
- Check zips, buttons and seams.
- Look for holes against the light.
- Smell it. Damp and smoke are hard to shift.
- Check the care and size labels are present; many buyers want them in the photos.
- Measure it if the size label is missing or unclear; see [measuring clothes for listings](/guides/measuring-clothes-for-listings).

### Media and games

- Open the case. Check the disc or cartridge matches the case, and look at the playing surface for scratches.
- Check for manuals and inserts if they affect value in that category.
- Count the pieces in board games and puzzles, or price them as incomplete.

### Electricals

The Chartered Trading Standards Institute's [car boot guidance](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/car-boot-sales-s) specifically warns about second-hand electricals. If you sell electricals as a trader, they must be safe, and the guidance on [second-hand electrical goods](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/product-safety/second-hand-electrical-goods) describes what that means in practice.

- Look for damage to cables, plugs and casings. Walk away from anything damaged.
- Ask the seller if it works, and ask to test battery items.
- Check the product against the [GOV.UK product safety alerts and recalls](https://www.gov.uk/product-safety-alerts-reports-recalls) list when you get home, before you list it.
- If you cannot test it and cannot be sure it is safe, the most you can do is sell it for parts or not repair, described honestly. Many sellers simply do not buy electricals at boot sales.

### Branded goods

Car boot sales are a known outlet for counterfeit goods, and the law on selling them applies wherever you bought them. A seller with several of the same current branded item, new, at a fraction of retail, is a warning sign, not a bargain. See [spotting fakes before you buy](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy) for the checks.

### Children's items

Old toys and children's clothing carry their own safety rules, and the Trading Standards guidance mentions hood cords on children's clothing in particular. Check before buying anything you plan to sell for children.

## Keeping records

A car boot purchase is a business purchase once you are trading. You need a record of it for tax, and a trail that shows where the stock came from if a platform or a buyer asks.

- **Ask for a receipt on anything over a few pounds.** Receipts are not legally required at boot sales, but a handwritten note with the date, the item and the price is enough, and most sellers will write one. Photograph it the same day.
- **Keep a trip log.** Date, sale, entry fee, fuel or miles, total spend, number of items. The [sourcing log spreadsheet](/tools/downloads) records each buy against its source and compares sources side by side; entry fees and mileage belong in your bookkeeping as expenses.
- **Give every item a stock number when you get home.** Write the cost against it. When it sells, the profit is one subtraction. See [storage and stock numbers](/guides/storage-and-stock-numbers).
- **Record mileage.** If you use the simplified expenses method, business miles are claimed at the [GOV.UK flat rate](https://www.gov.uk/simpler-income-tax-simplified-expenses/vehicles). If you use actual costs, keep fuel receipts instead. GOV.UK says that once you use the flat rate for a vehicle, you must keep using it for as long as you use that vehicle for the business.
- **Keep records for at least five years** after the 31 January deadline of the tax year they relate to ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/how-long-to-keep-your-records)).

After a few months, the trip log tells you which sales are worth the early start. Some sellers we know drop half the sales they used to visit once they see the numbers.

## What goes wrong

- **Buying because it is cheap.** A 50p item that will not sell is a 50p loss and a shelf taken.
- **Buying on the first lap.** You spend the budget before you have seen the best pitches.
- **No sold price check.** Your memory of what something sells for is out of date.
- **Not enough change.** Sellers cannot break a £20 note at 7am.
- **Aggressive haggling.** Word travels at a regular sale, and so do faces.
- **Skipping the checks.** Stains, missing pieces and dead electricals turn up at home, not at the pitch.
- **Branded stock in bulk.** The trader with forty identical items is selling fakes more often than not.
- **No receipts or trip log.** Fine until the tax return, or until a platform asks where an item came from.
- **Forgetting the time.** Five hours at a boot sale, plus cleaning, photos and listing, for a £30 profit is a poor rate. Count your time as well as your cash.
