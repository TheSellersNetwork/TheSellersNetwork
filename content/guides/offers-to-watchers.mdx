This is for sellers on eBay UK with fixed price listings that collect watchers and basket adds but not sales. After reading it you will know exactly what eBay does when you send an offer, which discount to send and when, what not to run at the same time, and how to measure whether the offers are shifting stock at a margin or just training your buyers to wait. The mechanics link to eBay's pages and change without much notice, so check the current page before relying on a figure. Nothing here is tax, legal or financial advice.

## What happens when you send one

A watcher is a buyer who pressed the heart. A basket abandoner put the item in their basket and left it there. Neither has told you anything except that they looked twice. Sending an offer sends each of them, up to the 30 most recent, a private price on that listing: your Buy It Now less a discount of at least 5%, valid for 96 hours. They get a notification and an email; they can accept, ignore, or, if you allowed it, counter. If one accepts and the listing has quantity one, it sells and the other offers die with the listing. Nobody else sees the price. The listing's public price does not change.

That last point is the whole reason the feature exists. A markdown lowers the price for everyone, including the buyer who would have paid full price tomorrow. An offer lowers it only for people who have already shown interest and only for four days. Used well, it is the cheapest discount eBay lets you give. Used badly, it teaches your regular buyers that watching for a week earns 15% off, and they will do exactly that.

Two things the rules do not say and the practice does. An offer sends only to watchers who still have the item watched at the moment you send; people who bought elsewhere and unwatched are gone. And a watcher who has received an offer and let it expire cannot be sent another until the button becomes available on that listing again, which in practice is after new interest arrives. So you do not get to send the same 30 people an offer every four days; each send is spent on the audience that exists at that moment.

## What the discount levels do

eBay sets a minimum of 5%. Above that it is your choice, and the choice has a shape that is consistent enough across categories to plan with. These are what sellers see in practice, not figures from eBay.

| Discount | What it does | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% to 7% | A nudge. Converts the watcher who had already decided and wanted an excuse. Costs almost nothing on margin. Acceptance is low but the sales are the best ones you will get. | The default on anything priced at the middle comp with several watchers. Send it first. |
| 10% | The level buyers recognise as "a proper discount". Noticeably more acceptances than 5%, and still inside most business floors on items priced at the middle. | The second attempt on a listing where 5% did nothing, or the first on stock over 60 days old. |
| 15% | Moves stock. Also the level where the accepted price is often below the low quarter of the comps, so run it against the floor before you send. | Stale stock you have already decided to clear, or items with a season ending. |
| 20% and above | A private markdown. If you would not put this price on the listing publicly, ask why you are sending it privately. Watchers at this level are usually bargain hunters who will not pay more later. | End of line, end of season, or an item you overpaid for and want out of. Check the floor twice. |

The number to keep in front of you is not the discount but the accepted price after fees. A 10% offer on a £40 listing is £36. If your floor from [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps) is £34, that is fine. If your floor is £37, you have just offered to sell at a loss to thirty people. Work the floor before you touch the discount slider, and if the floor does not allow 5%, the listing is priced too low to send offers from at all.

Round the offer price rather than the percentage. £35 reads better than £35.60. Set the discount so the price lands on a number a buyer would type.

## When to send

Timing is worth more than the extra percent. What works, in order.

**Send when watchers are fresh.** A watcher from this week is a buyer with the item on their mind. A watcher from two months ago has probably bought it elsewhere and forgotten to unwatch. If a listing has gained several watchers in a few days, that is the moment. The Listings tab in Seller Hub shows watcher counts per listing and lets you filter to listings eligible to send offers.

**Send in the evening, from Thursday to Sunday.** The offer lasts 96 hours. A Thursday evening send covers the weekend, when most buyers on eBay UK are on the site with time to decide. A Monday morning send expires on Friday morning, having spent its life in work hours.

**Send after a spike in views without a sale.** If a listing had a run of page views and nobody bought, the price is close but not there. A 5% offer to the people who watched during the spike answers the question they were asking.

**Send before you mark down.** If a listing is on its way into your markdown ladder anyway, send the offer at the next rung's price first. The watchers get four days of exclusivity, and if enough take it you never have to cut the public price.

**Do not send on a new listing.** Offers in the first fortnight tell early watchers the price is soft. Let the listing find its natural buyers first; most sales that will happen at full price happen early.

**Do not send while a sale event is running.** The offer discount comes off the Buy It Now, and the buyer will compare it with the sale price and the strikethrough; it confuses more than it converts, and it interferes with the 14 day rules around sale prices.

## Offers, sale events and coupons

These three tools touch the same price and eBay has rules about which one wins. The rules matter because a broken combination looks to the buyer like a listing that does not work.

**Coupons.** eBay's [Discounts Manager page](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/promotions-manager?id=4094) says that if an item has a Best Offer or an offer to buyers, the offer takes priority and the coupon cannot be applied. So a buyer with your coupon code and a live offer in their inbox will find the code fails on that item. If you run coded coupons for a Shop, do not send offers on the listings the coupon covers while the coupon is live.

**Sale events and markdown.** A sale needs the listing to have sat 14 days with no change to price, postage, title or description before it can be included, runs up to 14 days, and then the item must be out of any sale for 14 days ([sale event and markdown](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/grow-your-sales/using-promotions-to-boost-your-sales/sale-event-markdown)). The Discounts Manager page adds that if you changed the price as part of a sale you wait 14 days before including it in a new one. Offers to buyers do not change the listing price, so they do not reset that clock, which is one of their quiet advantages: you can send offers in the 14 days before a sale without spoiling the sale's eligibility. Sale events and most discount tools need a Shop subscription; offers to buyers do not.

**Order discounts and multi-buy.** These also need a Shop and apply at checkout. When several discounts run on an item the buyer gets the best one, per the Discounts Manager page, so an offer on top of a multi-buy can stack into a price you did not intend. Keep one mechanism per listing at a time.

The order we would use on a listing that is not selling: full price for the first few weeks, then offers to watchers at 5% and 10%, then a sale event with strikethrough if you have a Shop, then a public markdown, then a bundle or another platform. That is the [stale stock](/guides/stale-stock) ladder; offers are the first rung because they cost the least.

## Offers and Promoted Listings

An offer to buyers does not switch off the ad fee. If any buyer clicked a general ad for the item in the last 30 days and the item then sells, the ad fee is charged on the total sale amount at the ad rate in effect at the sale ([general campaign strategy](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/listings/listing-tips/promoted-listings?id=4164)). Most watchers on a promoted listing arrived through the ad, so assume the fee applies. Your floor for the offer price is the unpromoted floor plus the ad rate on the total. At a 6% ad rate, a £34 floor is about £36.17.

The seller centre page for offers warns that advertising fees may still apply depending on timing. In practice the sequence that wastes money is: promote at a high rate to gather watchers, then send a 15% offer, then pay the ad fee on the discounted sale. If you are paying for the watchers, send the small offer, and if it does not convert, question the ad rate rather than deepening the discount.

## Automating it

After you send a manual offer, eBay offers to send the same discount automatically to future interested buyers on that listing. Shop subscribers can go further and set rules by category, price range and condition that run for up to 150 days, with counteroffers on or off ([Offers to buyers](https://www.ebay.co.uk/sellercentre/grow-your-sales/using-discounts-to-boost-your-sales/offer-to-buyers-best-offer)).

Automation is worth it on catalogue stock: multiples of the same item, priced at the middle comp, with a floor that allows the discount and a sales history that shows the offer converts. It is dangerous on one-offs, on anything promoted at a dynamic ad rate, and on anything you might later put in a sale. Set automatic offers at the small end (5% to 7%), review the Offers page monthly, and turn the rule off on any listing whose price you cut, because the automatic discount comes off the new price.

Allowing counteroffers on automated offers means buyers can come back below your offer price. Only allow it if you have set Best Offer thresholds on the listing that will catch a counter under the floor; see [Best Offer strategy](/guides/best-offer-strategy).

## Tracking whether it moves stock

This is the part most sellers skip, and it is why they end up sending 15% to everyone. Three sources and one spreadsheet.

**The Offers page in Seller Hub** lists every offer sent, its status and the outcome. Once a month, count offers sent, offers accepted, and the average discount accepted. Acceptance rate by discount level is the number that tells you whether 10% is really doing more than 5% for you.

**Performance and Traffic in Seller Hub** gives impressions, page views and sales conversion rate per listing over a chosen period ([Seller Hub](https://www.ebay.co.uk/help/selling/selling-tools/seller-hub?id=4095)). Look at a listing's conversion in the week after an offer against the weeks before. If page views rose and sales did not, the offer went to people who had stopped caring.

**Your margin sheet.** Every accepted offer is a sale at a lower price. Put the accepted price through the same fee line as any other sale and record the net. Offers are working if net profit per month went up, not if the number of sales went up.

A simple log with one row per send: date, listing, watchers at the time, discount, promoted or not, accepted yes or no, accepted price, net after fees. After a couple of months, sort it by discount and the pattern is obvious. Sellers we know typically find that the 5% offers on fresh watchers return the best net, the 10% offers on older stock return the most sales, and anything above 15% is a markdown wearing a disguise.

Questions to ask the log every month.

- Are sales after offers coming from watchers who watched in the last seven days? If not, the audience is stale and the trigger is wrong.
- Did any listing sell at full price within a day of an offer going out? That is a buyer who did not need the discount and the offer was wasted; the send was too early.
- Is the accepted price after fees above the floor on every row? If a row is below, the floor was not checked before the send.
- Has the share of sales that go through offers crept up month on month? If so, buyers have learned to wait. Stop sending on new listings for a while and let full price sales recover.

## What goes wrong

- **Sending 15% as the first offer.** The watchers who would have taken 5% take 15%, and you never learn what the item was worth to them.
- **Sending without a floor.** The 5% minimum is eBay's, not yours. A listing priced to clear cannot afford it.
- **Sending on promoted listings and forgetting the ad fee.** The discount and the ad rate come off the same sale.
- **Sending while a coupon is live.** The coupon fails on that item and the buyer blames you.
- **Sending on day two.** Early watchers include your full price buyers. Let them buy.
- **Sending on Monday morning.** The offer dies on Friday morning without seeing a weekend.
- **Automating 10% across the whole shop.** It becomes the price. Automate small, on catalogue stock, and review.
- **Counting sales instead of net.** More orders at a lower margin can be a worse month. The sheet decides, not the order count.
- **Never unwatching your own dead listings from the plan.** A listing with 40 watchers from last year is not an opportunity; those people have gone. Fresh watchers or nothing.
