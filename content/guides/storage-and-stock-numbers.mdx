This is for resellers whose stock has outgrown a corner of the bedroom, or who have ever spent twenty minutes looking for an item that sold an hour ago. It covers where and how to store stock, a numbering system that works on every platform, the routine from bringing stock home to listing it, and the checks that stop stock going missing. The system here is the one sellers we know end up with after trying the others; adapt the details, but keep the principle that every item has one number and one place.

## The principle

Every item you own for sale has:

1. **A stock number** (SKU), unique to that item or product line.
2. **A location**, written against the stock number.
3. **A record** in one place (a spreadsheet or your listing tool) that links the stock number, the location, the cost and the listing.

When an item sells, you read the stock number from the order, look up the location, and pick it. If any of the three is missing, you are searching the house.

## Where to store stock

### What stock needs

- **Dry.** Damp ruins clothing, books, paper and boxes, and brings smells that buyers notice straight away. Garages, sheds and outbuildings are often damp, especially in winter.
- **Moderate temperature.** Lofts can get very hot in summer and cold in winter, which damages some plastics, adhesives, candles, cosmetics, media and electronics.
- **Dark or covered.** Sunlight fades fabric and packaging.
- **Free of pests.** Clothes moths, mice and silverfish all like stored stock. Sealed containers and regular checks help.
- **Reachable.** Stock you cannot get to easily does not get listed, checked or picked.

### Common setups

| Setup | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spare room with shelving and lidded boxes | Most small and medium stock | Weight on floors if you use heavy shelving fully loaded |
| Clothing rails | Items that crease, coats, anything you photograph on a hanger | Space; dust and fading without covers |
| Folded clothing in labelled clear bags on shelves | Most clothing; dense and easy to number | Creasing on delicate fabrics |
| Garage or shed | Bulky, robust items; outdoor and garden goods | Damp, cold, pests; put stock on shelves off the floor, in sealed boxes |
| Loft | Seasonal stock you will not need for months | Heat, access, and the risk of carrying loads up a ladder |
| Self-storage unit or lock-up | Large volumes once home space has run out | Monthly cost, travel time to pick orders, access hours |

**Our recommendation:** keep everything that is listed in the house or somewhere you can reach in minutes, because every order has to be picked. Use lofts, garages and units only for stock waiting to be listed, off-season stock and packaging. A unit that costs you a round trip for every sale is a problem; one you visit weekly to restock the house is fine.

### Shelving and containers

- **Use one size of box where you can.** Identical lidded boxes stack, label and swap easily.
- **Clear boxes or bags** save opening things to check contents.
- **Label the shelf and the box,** not only the box. Boxes move.
- **Keep heavy items low.** The Health and Safety Executive's [good handling technique](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/manual-handling/good-handling-technique.htm) guidance applies to the self-employed as well as employees: plan the lift, keep the load close, and do not twist.

### Running the business from home

If you store stock at home, check three things. GOV.UK's page on [running a business from home](https://www.gov.uk/run-business-from-home) says you may need permission from your mortgage provider or landlord, that home insurance may not cover business stock, and that you may need to pay business rates on part of the property in some circumstances. Speak to your insurer before a fire or a burglary does it for you. None of this is legal advice.

## A stock numbering system

A SKU (stock keeping unit) is your own code for an item. It does not need to mean anything to anyone else. It needs to be unique, short, and quick to write.

### Two approaches

**Sequential number with a separate location.** Every new item gets the next number: 1001, 1002, 1003. The location (shelf, box, rail) is recorded against the number in your spreadsheet. Easy to issue and never runs out of logic. When you move an item, you change the location in the record, not the number.

**Location built into the code.** The code tells you where the item is, for example B04-017 for box 4, item 17. Faster to pick, because you do not need to look anything up. The cost is that moving an item means changing its code everywhere, including on the listing.

**Our recommendation:** sequential numbers with a location column, for most resellers. Items move between boxes as stock sells and shelves are reorganised, and a code that never changes is easier to trust. If you only ever add and never reorganise, location codes are faster.

### Rules that keep it working

- **One number per item, used once.** Never reuse a number after an item sells. Your records need to be able to point to the same item years later.
- **Short and unambiguous.** Avoid letters that look like numbers (O and 0, I and 1).
- **Optional prefix by category or source.** C for clothing, B for books, or a prefix for the month bought (2610 for October 2026) can help with reporting later. Keep it simple; the more meaning in a code, the more ways to get it wrong.
- **Multiple identical units share one SKU** and a quantity, if you sell multi-quantity listings. One-off items get their own.

### Labelling the item

- **Label the bag, tag or box, not the item itself.** Writing on or sticking labels to an item can damage it and must come off before posting.
- **For clothing,** a paper tag on a safety pin through a label or a clear bag with the number on it.
- **For boxed items,** a removable label on the outer bag or a slip inside the box flap.
- **For books and media,** a slip of paper inside the cover, sticking out so it can be read on the shelf.

## Finding the item when it sells

The stock number has to reach the order, or you are back to searching.

| Platform | Where to put the stock number |
| --- | --- |
| eBay | The custom label (SKU) field in the listing. It shows in Seller Hub orders and on many packing slips and label tools. |
| Vinted, Depop, Facebook Marketplace | These have no private SKU field for most sellers. Options: add the number at the end of the description (harmless, and most buyers ignore it), keep a spreadsheet that links the listing title to the number, or use a cross-listing tool that holds its own stock record. |
| Amazon | The seller SKU field, which Amazon uses to identify your offer. |
| Your own shop | The SKU field in your shop's product settings. |

If you sell on more than one platform, the stock number is also how you end the other listings when one sells. See [multi-channel selling](/guides/multi-channel-selling).

### The picking routine

1. Open the orders. Note the stock numbers.
2. Look up locations in your record, or read them from the code.
3. Pick everything in one walk, in shelf order.
4. Check each item against the order (size, colour, condition) before packing.
5. Pack, label and update the record: status sold, date, price.

## Intake: from bag to listing

New stock that sits in bags is stock you will forget. A set routine turns a sourcing trip into listings the same week.

1. **Unpack in one place.** A table or a clear floor area.
2. **Check.** Faults, stains, missing parts, authenticity for branded goods ([spotting fakes](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy)). Anything that fails goes to a separate pile for returning, donating or disposal.
3. **Clean.** Wash, steam, dust, as appropriate.
4. **Measure and weigh.** Measurements for clothing ([measuring clothes for listings](/guides/measuring-clothes-for-listings)); weight and size of the packed item for postage (the [parcel size checker](/tools/postage?tab=size) helps choose the format).
5. **Number.** Next SKU, tag or bag it.
6. **Photograph.** In batches, with the same background and light. Include the SKU tag in a first or last reference photo that you do not publish, so each set of photos can be matched to its item later. See [photos on a phone](/guides/photos-on-a-phone).
7. **Log.** One row per item: SKU, description, location, date bought, cost, source.
8. **Store.** Straight to its location.
9. **List.** From the photos and the log, with the SKU in the listing.

Doing each step for a batch (check all, then clean all, then photograph all) is faster than taking each item through all nine steps one at a time.

### The log

The [stock tracker spreadsheet](/tools/downloads) is set up for this: one row per item with SKU, item, location, date bought, cost, platforms, date listed, asking price, status, sold price and date sold. It works out days listed and flags anything that has been sitting too long. When stale stock builds up, [stale stock](/guides/stale-stock) has the method.

Recording cost against each item has a second use: it is the backbone of your business records. Receipts and invoices for stock are records HMRC expects you to keep ([GOV.UK: what records to keep](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/what-records-to-keep)), and if you use traditional accounting you will need a stock figure at your year end. See [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers). This is not tax advice.

## Checking the system

- **Monthly spot check.** Pick ten random rows from the log and find each item. If any is missing or in the wrong place, look for the cause (not updated after moving, not updated after a sale, a return put back in the wrong place).
- **Returns.** A returned item goes through intake again: check, relist, restore to a location, update the record. Returns put back without updating the record are the most common source of lost stock.
- **Full count.** Once or twice a year, count everything and compare with the log. Before your year end is a good time.
- **Unlisted stock.** Filter the log for items bought but not listed. If the list is growing, you are buying faster than you list; slow the buying.

## What goes wrong

- **No location recorded.** The number tells you what, not where.
- **Reusing numbers.** Two items with the same code, and one of them gets posted to the wrong buyer.
- **Codes with too much meaning.** Long codes that encode brand, size, colour, source and month get written wrongly.
- **Stock in the garage without checking for damp.** Clothes that smell of damp cannot be sold as they are.
- **Labels on the item.** They damage it and get posted with it.
- **Returns put straight back on the shelf.** Unchecked, unrecorded, and lost.
- **Bags of unprocessed stock.** Stock that is not listed is not for sale.
- **A storage unit for everyday orders.** The travel costs more than the space saves.
- **Lifting badly.** A back injury stops the business faster than anything else on this list.
