This is for UK resellers who have seen returns pallets and liquidation lots advertised and want to know whether they are worth buying. It explains how the trade works, what a pallet realistically contains, the signs of a scam (including the cheap "mystery pallet" adverts that fill social media feeds), and how to check a supplier before you hand over money. It is general information, not legal or financial advice.

## The short version

Returns and liquidation stock can be a good source for a reseller who already knows what sells, has space to sort and store it, and can absorb a share of broken and unsaleable items. It is a poor first source. Most of the value is in the sorting, testing and listing, not in the pallet. Most of the losses come from buying from the wrong supplier.

**Our recommendation:** do not start with a pallet. Start with small manifested lots from a supplier you have checked, record what every item actually sold for, and repeat that several times before you buy a full pallet. The [sourcing guide](/guides/sourcing-for-resale) sets this in the context of other sources.

## Where the stock comes from

| Type | What it is | What to expect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer returns | Items sent back to a retailer by buyers | A mix of unused, used, damaged, missing parts and faulty. Some items were returned because they did not work. |
| Overstock and end of line | New stock the retailer or brand no longer wants to hold | Usually new and working, but often lines that did not sell well at retail |
| Shelf pulls and damaged packaging | Items taken off sale because the box is damaged or the display is being changed | Item often fine, packaging not |
| Insolvency and business closures | Stock sold off by an administrator or a closing business | Varies; often sold through auction houses |
| Salvage | Items damaged in transit, storage or by water | High share of unsaleable items |

Retailers sell returns and overstock in bulk because processing each item themselves costs more than the item is worth to them. Liquidators buy large volumes, sort some of it, and sell on in lots and pallets. By the time a pallet reaches a small reseller, it may have passed through several hands, and each one has taken the best items or a margin.

### Grades and manifests

- **Manifested.** You get a list of what is in the lot before you buy: item descriptions, quantities, and often the original retail price. Buy manifested where you can. Check that the manifest is for your lot, not a sample.
- **Unmanifested, "mystery" or "unchecked".** You do not know what is in it. The risk is all yours.
- **Grades.** Some suppliers grade stock (for example as new, like new, used or untested). There is no official standard for grades; they mean whatever the supplier says they mean. Read the supplier's own definitions and treat them as a promise you can hold them to, not a description of reality.

**The retail value on a manifest is not the resale value.** It is the price the item was sold at new, often at full price. Your resale value is what the item, in its actual condition, sells for on the platform you use, after fees and postage. Work from that.

## What to expect in practice

There are no official figures for how much of a returns pallet is saleable, and anyone quoting a precise figure is quoting their own experience or selling you something. What sellers we know describe is consistent:

- **A share of the items will be faulty, damaged, incomplete or unsaleable.** How much depends on the source and grade. Budget for it from the start.
- **The best items may already be gone.** On pallets that have passed through several hands, expect that someone has picked out the valuable pieces.
- **Most of the work is after delivery.** Unpacking, testing, cleaning, researching, photographing, listing, storing and posting. A pallet can be dozens or hundreds of separate items.
- **Electricals need testing.** Returned electricals may have been returned because they are faulty or unsafe. Anything you sell as a trader must be safe; see the Chartered Trading Standards Institute's [guidance on second-hand electrical goods](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/product-safety/second-hand-electrical-goods), and check items against [GOV.UK product safety alerts and recalls](https://www.gov.uk/product-safety-alerts-reports-recalls).
- **Returned items are not new.** If an item has been opened or used, describe it that way. Listing a returned item as new when it is not is a fast route to returns, claims and account problems.
- **Counterfeits can appear.** Some returns are fakes sent back in place of genuine items. Check branded goods; see [spotting fakes before you buy](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy).

### Do the sums before you buy

For a manifested lot, go through the manifest line by line.

1. **Check the sold price** of each item on your platform in the condition you expect. Use recent sold prices, and the middle of the range. See [pricing from sold comps](/guides/pricing-from-sold-comps).
2. **Deduct fees, postage and packaging** for each item.
3. **Discount for the share you expect to be unsaleable.** Be pessimistic until your own records say otherwise.
4. **Add up the costs of the lot:** price, VAT if charged on top, delivery, and any collection costs.
5. **Add your time.** Sorting and listing a pallet is days of work.
6. **Compare.** If the expected sales after all of that are not comfortably above the cost of the lot, do not buy it.

For an unmanifested lot, you cannot do these sums, which is the main reason to avoid them.

### Space and time

A full pallet is bulky. Before you buy, know where it will be delivered (many deliveries are kerbside, and many drivers cannot bring a pallet inside), how you will move it, and where the contents will be stored while you work through them. See [storage and stock numbers](/guides/storage-and-stock-numbers).

## Pallet scams

The pallet trade attracts scams because buyers expect bargains and cannot see the goods before paying.

### The "£99 pallet" advert

Social media feeds regularly show adverts for "Amazon returns pallets", "unclaimed parcels" or "mystery boxes" from well-known retailers, at prices far below any real pallet. Common features:

- A very low price for a whole pallet of branded goods, often with a countdown or "last few remaining".
- Photos of stacked boxes or high-value items taken from elsewhere.
- A newly created website or page, often using a retailer's name or logo.
- Payment by card on an unfamiliar site, or by bank transfer.

What typically happens: nothing arrives, or a small box of low-value items arrives, or you are signed up to charges you did not expect. Treat any advert of this kind as a scam until you have checked it thoroughly, and in practice, do not buy from them. Real returns pallets are sold by business suppliers, usually with minimum orders, terms and delivery charges, and at prices that reflect the stock.

Report Fraud (the service that replaced Action Fraud in England, Wales and Northern Ireland) warns that if an offer sounds too good to be true it probably is, and advises against paying for goods by bank transfer to sellers you have not checked ([online shopping advice](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/onlineshopping)).

### Other warning signs

| Sign | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Payment only by bank transfer, crypto or a payment app with no buyer protection | Very hard to recover money if the goods never arrive |
| No company details, or details that do not match the register | You cannot hold anyone to anything |
| No physical address, or an address you cannot verify | Nowhere to collect from, nowhere to complain to |
| Pressure to decide now | Genuine suppliers have stock next week too |
| Photos that appear on other sites | The goods may not exist |
| "Guaranteed profit" or claims about resale value | No supplier can guarantee what you will sell for |
| Refuses a viewing, a sample or a manifest | You are being asked to trust without evidence |
| Changes bank details by email | A common payment diversion fraud; confirm by phone on a number you already had |

## Checking a supplier

Report Fraud's [know your suppliers](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/know-your-suppliers/) guidance is written for businesses, and applies to resellers buying stock. In short: verify that a supplier is who it says it is, search its name alongside words like "scam", "complaints" and "fraud", confirm any change of payment details through a contact you already know, and check that what you receive matches what you paid for.

A practical checklist before your first order:

1. **Company check.** If the supplier says it is a limited company, look it up on the free [Companies House register](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/). Check it is active, the name and number match, the registered address makes sense, and it has been trading for a while. A company formed last month selling "thousands of pallets" is a warning.
2. **VAT check.** If the supplier charges VAT, ask for its VAT number and check it with [GOV.UK's VAT number checker](https://www.gov.uk/check-uk-vat-number), which returns the registered name and address. They should match the supplier.
3. **Address.** Check the address is a real business premises, not a residential address or a virtual office, if the supplier claims a warehouse.
4. **Reputation.** Search the name with "scam", "complaints" and "reviews". Ask in the [forum](/community/c/sourcing-and-stock). Treat testimonials on the supplier's own site as advertising.
5. **Terms.** Read the terms of sale. What do the grades mean, what happens if the lot does not match the manifest, is there any return or credit, who pays for delivery, and when does risk pass to you.
6. **Viewing or collection.** A supplier that lets you view or collect from its premises is easier to trust. Where possible, view before you buy and collect the first order yourself.
7. **Payment.** Pay in a way that gives some protection where you can. Confirm bank details by phone on a number you found independently, never only from an email.
8. **Start small.** First order: the smallest lot the supplier sells.

### Your rights when you buy as a business

When you buy stock to resell, you are buying as a business, not as a consumer. Consumer protections such as the right to cancel an online order within 14 days do not apply in the same way to business purchases, and much of the protection comes from the contract terms. Some statutory protections on goods still apply between businesses. Business Companion's guidance and local Trading Standards pages such as [business-to-business contracts](https://www.devonsomersettradingstandards.gov.uk/business/consumer-rights/business-to-business-contracts/) explain the differences. Read the terms before you pay. This is not legal advice.

## If it goes wrong

- **Goods not delivered or very different from the advert.** Contact the supplier in writing. If you paid by card, contact your card provider promptly. If you think you were defrauded, contact your bank straight away and report it to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) or Police Scotland.
- **Lot does not match the manifest.** Photograph everything on unpacking before you move it, compare against the manifest, and raise it with the supplier under its terms.
- **Counterfeits in the lot.** Do not list them. See [spotting fakes before you buy](/guides/spotting-fakes-before-you-buy) for what to do.
- **Unsafe items.** Do not sell them. Dispose of them properly, following your council's guidance for electricals and batteries.

## Keeping records

- **Invoice for every lot,** with the supplier's name, address and VAT number if charged.
- **Manifest and photos** of the lot on arrival.
- **Every item logged** with a stock number and its share of the lot cost. The simplest method is to divide the lot cost evenly across the items you will list; a more accurate one spreads it by expected sale price. Either works if you are consistent. The [stock tracker spreadsheet](/tools/downloads) has columns for this.
- **What each item sold for**, so you can see whether the lot made money. After a few lots, this is the only figure that tells you whether pallets work for you.
- **Unsaleable items** noted and disposed of, with a record.

See [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers) for how stock purchases are treated.

## What goes wrong

- **Starting with a full pallet.** Too many decisions at once, before you know what sells.
- **Pricing from the manifest's retail value.** That is the price new, not your price.
- **Buying unmanifested lots.** You cannot do the sums.
- **Buying from social media adverts.** The cheap ones are scams.
- **Paying by bank transfer to a supplier you have not checked.** The money is gone if it goes wrong.
- **Listing returns as new.** Returns, claims and account damage follow.
- **Not testing electricals.** Unsafe goods, and a legal problem.
- **No space.** A pallet on the drive in the rain is a pallet of salvage.
- **Not recording what each lot made.** Without the numbers, you cannot tell a good supplier from a bad one.
