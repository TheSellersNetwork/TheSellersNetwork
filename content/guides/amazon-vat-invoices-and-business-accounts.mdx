This guide is for UK resellers on Amazon who are approaching the VAT threshold, have just crossed it, or are already registered and finding that VAT is eating margin they did not expect to lose. After reading it you will know when registration is compulsory, how the two main schemes treat a stock business, how to list zero-rated products so Amazon does not charge VAT on them, what an invoice must show for HMRC and for Amazon, and how to run a quarter so the return is a formality.

It is UK only, and it is not tax advice. VAT is a tax where a wrong assumption compounds every quarter, so use this to understand the shape and take the decisions to an accountant. For the bookkeeping habits that sit underneath all of it, see [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers); for the fee figures and the business setup, [the working manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk).

## When you must register

The rules are on [GOV.UK: register for VAT](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat) and its [when to register](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat/when-to-register) page. The points that catch Amazon sellers:

- **The threshold is £90,000 of VAT taxable turnover in any rolling twelve months.** Not a tax year, not a calendar year: any twelve month window, checked at the end of every month. Turnover means sales, including zero-rated sales, before Amazon takes its fees. A seller doing £8,000 a month of gross sales is over.
- **You must register within 30 days of the end of the month you went over**, and your registration takes effect from the first day of the second month after. Go over in March, register by 30 April, charge VAT from 1 May.
- **There is a forward test too.** If you realise your turnover will pass £90,000 in the next 30 days alone (a big Q4 order, for instance), you must register from the date you realised.
- **Businesses established outside the UK register from their first UK sale**, with no threshold. A UK sole trader or UK company using FBA is not in that group; using Amazon's warehouses does not by itself trigger registration.
- **Late registration carries a penalty** on top of the VAT you should have charged and did not. HMRC will treat the sales you made after the date you should have registered as VAT inclusive and want a sixth of them.
- **You can deregister** if turnover falls below £88,000 ([cancel your registration](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat/cancel-your-registration)), which matters if you scale back.

Check the threshold on the page before you rely on it; it has been frozen and then moved in recent years.

### What registration does to a reseller's margin

This is the part most people underestimate. Say you buy a product for £10 and sell it for £20 through FBA.

Before registration, the £20 is yours less Amazon's fees, and the VAT you paid the retailer inside the £10 is simply part of your cost. After registration, the £20 includes £3.33 of VAT that belongs to HMRC, so your sale is really £16.67. Against that you reclaim the £1.67 of VAT inside the £10 purchase (if you have a proper VAT invoice) and the VAT on Amazon's fees. On a standard rated product bought from a VAT registered retailer, the net effect is that your margin shrinks by roughly the VAT on your gross profit. It is a real cost, and it is why sellers who run their calculator without a VAT setting are surprised at month one after registering.

Two things soften it. First, VAT on Amazon's fees: since 1 August 2024 Amazon charges 20% VAT on every seller fee (the [pricing page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) quotes every fee excluding VAT), and a registered seller reclaims all of it. Second, stock you already hold: you can reclaim the VAT on goods bought up to four years before registration that you still have, and on services bought up to six months before, provided you have the invoices ([reclaim VAT on business expenses](https://www.gov.uk/charge-reclaim-record-vat/reclaim-vat-business-expenses)). That first return is often a refund, and it is why the manual says to keep every invoice from the first day even when you are nowhere near registering.

## Standard scheme or flat rate

There are two ways a small business can account for VAT. A high level comparison; the choice is one for an accountant.

| | Standard VAT accounting | Flat Rate Scheme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it works | Charge VAT on sales, reclaim VAT on purchases, pay the difference | Pay HMRC a fixed percentage of your VAT inclusive turnover and keep the rest of the VAT you charged; reclaim nothing on purchases except some capital items over £2,000 |
| Who can use it | Anyone | Turnover of £150,000 or less excluding VAT to join ([Flat Rate Scheme](https://www.gov.uk/vat-flat-rate-scheme)) |
| Rate for a retailer | 20% on standard rated sales, 0% on zero-rated | "Retailing that is not listed elsewhere" is 7.5%, less 1% in your first registered year ([how much you pay](https://www.gov.uk/vat-flat-rate-scheme/how-much-you-pay)) |
| Catch | More bookkeeping: every purchase invoice matters | The flat rate applies to all turnover including zero-rated sales, and a business whose goods cost under 2% of turnover or under £1,000 a year pays 16.5% instead |
| Suits | Businesses that buy a lot of standard rated goods and services | Service businesses with few purchases |

For a reseller the standard scheme is almost always right, and the reason is arithmetic. Your biggest cost is stock, on which you paid VAT, and your second biggest is Amazon's fees, on which you now pay VAT. The flat rate lets you reclaim neither. Paying 7.5% of gross sales sounds cheap until you notice that a typical arbitrage margin is not much bigger than that. Sellers who sell mostly zero-rated products (books, children's clothes, most food) are hurt twice, because the flat rate is charged on those sales too. Model both with real numbers before choosing, and expect the answer to be standard.

## Listing zero-rated and reduced rate products correctly

Not everything carries 20%. The categories are on [GOV.UK: VAT rates on different goods and services](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-of-vat-on-different-goods-and-services). The ones resellers meet most:

| Rate | Examples resellers actually sell |
| --- | --- |
| Zero (0%) | Most food and drink for home consumption, but not confectionery, crisps, alcohol or hot food; tea and coffee; books and printed matter; children's clothing and footwear; babywear; sanitary products; incontinence products; CE marked cycle helmets |
| Reduced (5%) | Children's car seats and carrycots with restraint straps; some energy saving items |
| Standard (20%) | Everything else: toys, cosmetics, supplements presented as such, electronics, homeware, adult clothing, pet food |

The boundaries are fiddly. A cereal bar can be confectionery; a "children's" hoodie is only zero-rated up to certain sizes; a bottled drink may be standard rated where the loose tea is not. If a product's rate matters to your decision, look it up on the GOV.UK page and its linked notices rather than assume from the category.

### Why it matters on Amazon

Once you are registered and give Amazon your VAT number, Amazon works out VAT on your sales for you through its VAT Calculation Service ([VCS help](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/gp/help/external/G202120290); overview on [Amazon's VAT resources page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/learn/vat-resources)). It does that from a Product Tax Code (PTC) on each offer. If an offer has no code, Amazon applies your account's default code, which for most sellers is the standard rate.

So if you sell coffee, a children's coat or a book and leave the tax code blank, Amazon treats the sale as 20% VAT. Three things then go wrong at once: the VAT invoice Amazon issues to a business customer shows VAT that was never due; the VAT report Amazon gives you (and that your accounting software imports) overstates your output VAT, so you overpay HMRC unless someone catches it; and your profit tracker shows a margin a sixth smaller than the true one, which can make a good product look like a bad one.

The fix is at listing time. When you add an offer, or afterwards in the offer's tax settings, choose the PTC that matches the product: there are codes for standard rate, for zero-rated food, for books, for children's clothing and so on. Amazon's help page "VAT rates and my PTCs" (search Help in Seller Central) lists every code and the rate it applies in the UK. Set it once per SKU and it stays. Then make the same choice in your sourcing calculator and your bookkeeping software, so all three agree. A product that is zero-rated in one system and standard in another will produce a return that does not reconcile, and an accountant who charges you for the hours it takes to find out why.

If you are not VAT registered, none of this changes the VAT you owe (none), but it still changes what a business customer's invoice looks like; see below.

## Invoices for Amazon Business customers

Some of your orders come from Amazon Business accounts: companies, schools, sole traders buying for work. Amazon's [Business invoicing policy](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/02/rainier/help/legal/Amazon_Business_Invoicing_Policy_EN_161220.pdf) requires the seller to provide a valid VAT invoice, or a receipt if you are not VAT registered, for every Amazon Business order within one business day of the dispatch being confirmed. Amazon tracks an invoice defect rate on your account health page and writes to you when it passes Amazon's threshold (5% at the time of writing; check the Account Health page for the current figure).

There are three ways to comply, and only one is sensible.

1. **Let Amazon do it.** Enrol in the VAT Calculation Service, with your VAT number and correct PTCs, and Amazon generates a compliant VAT invoice for every business order and makes it available to the customer automatically. You do nothing per order. If you are not VAT registered, Amazon can still generate receipts on your behalf for business orders; the option is in the same tax settings area.
2. **Upload your own.** Manage Orders, find the business order, upload a PDF invoice. Fine at a handful a month; a chore at scale, and a missed one is a defect.
3. **Ignore it.** Your invoice defect rate climbs and it is an account health issue.

Enrol as soon as you have a VAT number. Before that, turn on Amazon's automatic receipts. Either way, do a weekly check of business orders without invoices during your routine, because the automatic option occasionally misses an order whose offer has a broken tax setting.

## Purchase invoices: what HMRC and Amazon each need

Every product you buy to resell needs an invoice, and it will be read by two audiences with different tests. Get both right on every purchase and most of the paperwork problems in this business disappear.

### What HMRC needs

To reclaim the VAT on a purchase you need a VAT invoice from the supplier. Section 16 of the [VAT guide (Notice 700)](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/vat-guide-notice-700) sets out what one must show. A full VAT invoice carries:

- A unique, sequential invoice number and the date of issue
- The supplier's name, address and VAT registration number
- Your name and address (the customer's)
- The time of supply (tax point) if different from the invoice date
- A description of the goods and the quantity of each
- The rate of VAT and the amount excluding VAT for each line, the total excluding VAT, and the total VAT

For purchases of £250 or less including VAT, a simplified invoice is acceptable: the supplier's details and VAT number, the date, a description of the goods, and for each rate the total payable including VAT with the rate shown. Most retail till receipts from a VAT registered shop qualify for this if they carry the VAT number. A card statement, an order confirmation email, a dispatch note or a screenshot of a basket do not; none of them is an invoice.

Two HMRC habits worth adopting. Check that a new supplier's VAT number is real and belongs to them using [HMRC's VAT number checker](https://www.gov.uk/check-uk-vat-number) before you reclaim anything. And treat a supplier that will not give a VAT invoice as a supplier whose VAT you cannot reclaim, which on a 20% product is a large discount you did not get.

### What Amazon needs

Amazon reads invoices when you apply to sell a gated brand, when it asks you to prove a product is genuine, and when a rights owner complains. Its test is different. It wants to see:

- Your business name and address exactly as they appear on your Seller Central account
- The supplier's name, address and contact details, from a business that plausibly sells that product at that quantity
- The product identifiable (name, model, barcode where possible) and the quantity
- A date within the period Amazon asks for, usually the last 180 days for approvals

Amazon will reject an invoice it cannot match to you. The usual reasons are a personal name where the account is a company, an abbreviated trading name, or a delivery address in place of the billing address.

### Billing address versus shipping address

This is where the two audiences collide. Retailers put your billing address on the invoice and your delivery address on the parcel. If you have stock delivered to a prep centre, the invoice must still carry your own business address as the billing address, and the prep centre only as the ship-to. An invoice whose only address is the prep centre's is useless to Amazon (it does not match your account) and weak for HMRC (it is not obviously your purchase). Check it on the first order from every supplier and correct it before the second.

The same rule for the name. The name on the invoice, the Seller Central account, the bank account and the VAT registration should be identical, character for character. A mismatch is not fatal to HMRC on its own, but it turns every Amazon submission into an argument.

## Running a quarter

Once registered you file a return every three months; the deadline for both filing and paying is one calendar month and seven days after the end of the period ([VAT returns: deadlines](https://www.gov.uk/vat-returns/deadlines)). The money must have reached HMRC by then, not left your account. There is an [Annual Accounting Scheme](https://www.gov.uk/vat-annual-accounting-scheme) with one return a year and instalments, but it delays refunds to once a year, which is the wrong way round for a business that often reclaims, so most resellers stay quarterly.

All VAT registered businesses are in Making Tax Digital: the return goes through compatible software, and the records behind it must be digital with digital links between systems ([keeping VAT records](https://www.gov.uk/charge-reclaim-record-vat/keeping-vat-records)). In practice that means accounting software that receives your Amazon settlement data through a connector and your purchases from a bank feed, plus your invoices attached to each purchase.

### The return, in one line

Output VAT (the VAT inside your sales) minus input VAT (the VAT on your purchases and Amazon's fees) equals what you pay. If input is bigger, HMRC refunds you; that happens in quarters where you bought heavily, typically the run up to Q4 and the first return after registration. Refunds attract attention, so expect HMRC to ask for a sample of invoices the first time one is large. If your invoices are as described above, that is an afternoon's work, not a problem.

### What an accountant needs from you

Whether you use one or do it yourself, the return needs the same inputs each quarter. Have them ready in the first week after the period ends.

| Item | Where it comes from | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amazon settlement reports for the period | Seller Central, Payments, Reports | Your sales, refunds and fees by date |
| Amazon VAT transactions report | Seller Central, Reports, Tax Document Library | Output VAT by rate, per order, from your PTCs |
| Amazon's fee invoices | Tax Document Library | The VAT on fees you reclaim |
| Every purchase invoice, dated in the period | Your invoice folder or the attachment on each bank transaction | Input VAT; the number, VAT number and your name must be visible |
| Bank and card statements | Bank feed | To reconcile every line above |
| A list of stock you hold, if this is the first return | Your buy list | For the pre-registration reclaim |
| Anything unusual | You | A refund from a supplier, a write off, a sale outside Amazon, a personal use item |

A sale you make outside Amazon (a marketplace, a private sale, a return sold on) is still your turnover and still needs VAT accounting. Tell the accountant about all channels, not just the one with reports.

Zero-rated sales still go on the return, in the sales box, at zero VAT. That is the second reason PTCs matter: the Amazon report is only right if the codes are.

## Keeping records

You must keep VAT records for at least six years ([keeping VAT records](https://www.gov.uk/charge-reclaim-record-vat/keeping-vat-records)): copies of every invoice you issue (Amazon keeps the ones it issues for you, but download them), every invoice you receive, credit notes, a VAT account summarising each period, and the bank statements and Amazon reports behind them. Digital is fine and expected.

A system that works and costs nothing but discipline:

- One folder per month for purchase invoices, each file named by date, supplier and total.
- A buy list (a spreadsheet is enough) with one row per purchase: date, supplier, invoice number, product, quantity, cost excluding VAT, VAT, where the stock went.
- Each invoice attached to its bank transaction in the accounting software when it arrives, not at quarter end.
- Amazon's monthly tax documents downloaded on the first of each month.

The same invoices serve four purposes: the VAT reclaim, brand approvals, authenticity complaints, and your defence if a product is ever alleged to be counterfeit. They are the most valuable documents in the business.

## What goes wrong

- **Watching the tax year instead of the rolling twelve months.** Registration is late, the penalty is real, and the VAT on the sales in between comes out of your pocket.
- **Registering without changing the calculator.** Every product bought in the first month after registration looks 16% better than it is.
- **Blank tax codes on zero-rated products.** Amazon charges 20% on children's clothes and you pay it to HMRC.
- **Choosing the flat rate because it sounds simpler.** For a business that buys stock it usually costs more than it saves. Model it.
- **Prep centre address as the billing address.** Every future approval with that supplier is compromised.
- **Trading name on the account, legal name on the invoice, or the reverse.** Fix one of them today.
- **Receipts with no VAT number.** No reclaim.
- **Business orders without invoices.** A rising defect rate is an account health problem you can switch off by enrolling in Amazon's invoicing.
- **Paying on the deadline day.** The money has to have arrived.
- **Treating Amazon's VAT report as the truth.** It is the truth about your PTCs; if those are wrong, so is the report.

Northern Ireland: goods sold from or into Northern Ireland follow special rules under the Windsor Framework, particularly for EU customers and suppliers. If you are based there, search GOV.UK for its guidance on VAT for goods moving between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and the EU before you list anything, and an accountant is not optional.
