For arbitrage and wholesale sellers who keep being told to "run PPC" and want to know whether it will make them money or just move it to Amazon. After reading you will understand how Sponsored Products charge, the one rule that makes most ads on shared listings poor value, how to work out your break-even before spending a penny, and a small, capped test for the listings where ads can work.

Every rule here comes from Amazon's own seller and advertising pages and links to them. Amazon changes its ads console often, so check the linked page before you rely on a detail.

## What Sponsored Products are

Sponsored Products are the ads marked "Sponsored" in search results and on product pages. They are the only ad type most resellers will use; the brand formats need Brand Registry, which needs a trademark ([Brand Registry](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/brand-registry)), and the [wholesale and private label guide](/guides/amazon-wholesale-and-private-label-honestly) covers that route.

The basics, from Amazon's own pages:

| What | How it works | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who can advertise | Sellers with an active Professional account, on products in eligible categories | [Amazon Ads on Sell on Amazon](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/advertising), [Sponsored Products](https://advertising.amazon.com/solutions/products/sponsored-products) |
| How you pay | Per click. Impressions are free and there is no monthly or upfront fee | [Amazon Ads FAQ](https://advertising.amazon.com/resources/faq) |
| Your bid | The maximum you are willing to pay for one click | [Best practices](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-best-practices) |
| Your budget | A daily amount, averaged over the calendar month, so some days spend more and some less | [Budget basics](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-budget-best-practices) |
| When the ad shows | Only while your offer is the featured offer and in stock | [New advertiser guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/new-advertiser-success-guide) |

Two points on budgets. The daily figure is not a hard daily cap: Amazon's [budget guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-budget-best-practices) says a day can run over it while the month averages out, and a [2023 notice](https://advertising.amazon.com/resources/whats-new/sponsored-ads-daily-budgeting-policy-and-options) added settings that let a day use unspent budget from earlier days. Set the monthly total you are prepared to lose, divide by the days in the month, and that is your daily budget. When a campaign runs out, its ads stop until the budget resets at midnight.

## The rule that decides everything: no featured offer, no ad

Amazon is explicit. Its [new advertiser guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/new-advertiser-success-guide) says that if your products are not presenting the featured offer or are out of stock, your ad will not display, and its [FAQ](https://advertising.amazon.com/resources/faq) says an ad for a listing not eligible for the featured offer will not be shown to customers. The featured offer is what everyone else calls the Buy Box, and the [feedback and Buy Box guide](/guides/amazon-seller-feedback-and-buy-box#the-buy-box) explains how Amazon picks it and how it rotates between sellers.

Put those together on a listing you share with other sellers:

- Your ad only competes for placements during the share of the day you hold the box. If you hold it a third of the time, your campaign is live a third of the time.
- A customer who clicks your ad lands on the product page. By then the box may have rotated, and they may buy from someone else. You paid for the click; they got the sale.
- Every sale the listing makes, from your ad or not, helps the listing's rank, which all the sellers on it share. You are paying to warm a room everyone is standing in.
- Your competitors can run ads too. Now several sellers are bidding against each other for clicks on the same product, which pushes the cost per click up for all of them, and the winner still has to hold the box to convert.

On a shared arbitrage listing with steady sales, ads rarely add sales that the listing would not have made anyway. The better spend is on price: win more of the box and you get more of the sales the listing already makes. See [pricing to hold a share](/guides/amazon-seller-feedback-and-buy-box#pricing-to-hold-a-share).

## When ads can work for a reseller

There are cases where the maths changes, and they all come down to the same thing: you get most of the sales your ad creates.

| Situation | Why it can work | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You are the only seller on the listing | You hold the box almost all the time, and every sale the ad produces is yours | Another seller joining. Check the offer count weekly and pause if it changes |
| Your own bundle or multipack with its own ASIN | Nobody else sells it, but nobody finds it either; a new listing has no sales history to rank on | The bundle must be allowed under Amazon's listing rules; see [listing accuracy](/guides/amazon-product-safety-and-listing-accuracy) |
| Slow stock you cannot shift on price | Paying a few clicks to sell it may cost less than months of storage, the aged inventory surcharge, or removal | Only if the unit still makes money after ad spend. Compare with the costs in [inventory health](/guides/amazon-inventory-health) |
| A product where the listing sells well but search hides it | Rare for arbitrage, more common with wholesale lines on thin listings | Fix the listing content first; ads to a weak page waste clicks |

What we would do: no ads on shared listings, full stop. On sole seller listings and your own bundles, run the small test below. For slow stock, try a price cut first, because a price cut costs you only when something sells and it also helps you hold the box. Ads come after that.

## Targeting, match types and campaign structure

### Automatic targeting

Amazon picks where the ad appears based on your listing. Its [targeting guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/targeting-with-sponsored-products) splits automatic targeting into four groups that you can bid on separately or switch off:

- **Close match:** searches closely related to your product.
- **Loose match:** searches loosely related to it.
- **Substitutes:** product pages for similar items.
- **Complements:** product pages for items that go with yours.

Automatic campaigns are the right start for a reseller: little setup, and they tell you which searches actually lead to sales.

### Manual targeting

You choose keywords or products. For keywords, the same guide describes three match types:

| Match type | When the ad can show | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broad | The search contains your words in any order, and may include variations | Finding new searches, cheaply, at a low bid |
| Phrase | The search contains your words in the same order | Searches you know convert, with some room around them |
| Exact | The search is your words, in the same order, with nothing added | Your proven best searches, at your highest bid |

Product targeting puts your ad on specific ASINs, or on categories narrowed by brand, price or rating. For a reseller the useful version is targeting the listings of a product your bundle competes with.

**Negative keywords and negative products** stop the ad showing where it will not sell. Amazon's [best practices page](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-best-practices) covers adding them. Every campaign should gain a few each week.

### Bidding strategies

The [best practices page](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-best-practices) describes dynamic bids down only, where Amazon lowers your bid for placements less likely to convert, and Amazon's [dynamic bidding guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/dynamic-bidding-sponsored-products) describes up and down, which can raise or lower bids by up to 100%. There is also a fixed option. Use down only. With a thin margin you want Amazon able to spend less than your bid, never more.

### Structure

Keep it simple, because you will be checking it by hand:

1. One campaign per product, or per small group of closely related products. Amazon's [best practices page](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-best-practices) recommends keeping products in one campaign closely related.
2. An automatic campaign first, at a modest bid, with its own budget.
3. Once searches have produced sales, a manual campaign with those searches as exact match keywords, and the same searches added as negative exact keywords in the automatic campaign, so the two do not bid against each other.

The search term report shows which customer searches led to clicks and sales. It is where every decision in this guide comes from.

## The margin maths

### Break-even ACoS

ACoS, advertising cost of sales, is ad spend divided by ad sales, as a percentage ([ACoS guide](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/acos-advertising-cost-of-sales)). Spend £10 on clicks that produce £40 of sales and your ACoS is 25%.

Amazon's own ACoS guide says break-even ACoS is tied to your profit margin and that to stay in profit your ACoS must be lower than it. In plain terms:

**Break-even ACoS = profit per unit before ad spend ÷ selling price.**

Profit before ad spend is the selling price less cost of goods, the referral fee, the FBA fee, inbound delivery, prep and anything else per unit. Use the same net profit figure as in [the working manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#net-profit-properly). If you are VAT registered, take the VAT out of the profit figure first, as you already should.

### A worked example

These numbers are hypothetical, for illustration only. They are not current Amazon fees; use your own figures for the product in front of you.

| Line | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Selling price | £20.00 |
| Cost of goods | £8.00 |
| Referral and FBA fees | £6.00 |
| Delivery to Amazon and prep | £1.00 |
| Profit before ads | £5.00 |
| Margin before ads | 25% |
| Break-even ACoS | 25% |

At an ACoS of 25% each ad sale costs £5.00 in clicks and makes £0. At 15%, it costs £3.00 and leaves £2.00. At 40% it costs £8.00 and loses £3.00 on every sale the ad produced.

Turn it round to find your maximum bid. If, in this example, one in ten clicks becomes a sale, ten clicks must cost no more than £5.00, so £0.50 is the break-even cost per click. Bid below it, because you want profit, not break-even, and because a new listing usually converts worse than you hope.

A useful rule from this: the lower your margin, the less room you have. A product with a 10% margin needs an ACoS under 10%, which on a competitive search is very hard. Most arbitrage margins sit in the range where ads are a struggle before they start.

### ACoS and TACoS

ACoS only measures the sales Amazon credits to your ads. TACoS, total advertising cost of sales, is a figure you work out yourself: ad spend divided by all your sales of that product, ad and organic together.

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ACoS | Ad spend ÷ ad sales | Whether the ad sales covered the ad cost |
| TACoS | Ad spend ÷ total sales | How much of all your takings go on ads |

Watch both over a month. If ad spend rises, ACoS looks fine, and total sales do not move, the ads are taking credit for sales you would have made anyway, often from customers who would have found you organically. That is the most common way resellers lose money on ads without the dashboard ever looking bad. On a sole seller listing, what you want to see is total sales rising and TACoS falling over time as organic sales pick up.

Nothing here is financial advice. It is arithmetic; run it with your own numbers.

## A simple test plan

For one sole seller listing or one of your own bundles.

1. **Before you start**, write down the unit's profit before ads, its break-even ACoS, your target ACoS (below break-even, say two thirds of it), and its sales per week over the last four weeks.
2. **Set a total you are prepared to lose.** Treat it as the cost of the lesson. Divide by the days you will run the test for a daily budget.
3. **One automatic campaign**, dynamic bids down only, a starting bid below your break-even cost per click.
4. **Check twice a week for the first week**, as Amazon's [best practices page](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/sponsored-products-best-practices) suggests, then weekly. Each time, open the search term report and add as negatives the searches with clicks and no sales that are clearly irrelevant.
5. **After two to four weeks**, look at three numbers: ACoS against your target, total weekly sales against your before figure, and whether you still hold the box.
6. **Decide.** Under target and total sales up: move the converting searches to a manual exact campaign and keep going. Over break-even with no lift in total sales: switch it off. Anything in between: lower bids once and give it two more weeks, then decide.

Do not run tests on several products at once until one has worked. You will not have time to read the reports properly.

## What goes wrong

- **Advertising a shared listing.** Paying for clicks during the hours you hold the box, then watching customers buy from the seller who holds it next.
- **No break-even figure.** Judging a campaign by whether ACoS "looks reasonable" rather than against your margin. 30% is fine on a 45% margin and ruinous on a 20% one.
- **Trusting ACoS alone.** Ad sales rise, total sales do not, and the ads are buying customers you already had. Check TACoS.
- **Up and down bidding on thin margins.** Amazon can double your bid on placements it thinks will convert. Use down only.
- **Never adding negatives.** Automatic campaigns wander. Irrelevant searches with clicks and no sales need excluding every week.
- **Ads on the wrong day.** Out of stock or losing the box means the ad stops. Keep stock in and price right before blaming the campaign.
- **Forgetting the campaign.** A test left running after the stock has sold out or a new seller has joined. Set a calendar reminder to review or end every campaign.
- **Treating ads as the fix for a bad buy.** If the product only sells when you pay for traffic, it was a poor product for arbitrage. Price it to go and learn from it.
