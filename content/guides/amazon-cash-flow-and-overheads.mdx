This guide is for resellers who are selling on Amazon, are profitable on paper, and keep finding there is no money to buy the next lot of stock. After reading it you will be able to trace every pound from the retailer's checkout to your bank account with realistic timings, know what Amazon holds back and why, cut overheads to the ones that pay for themselves, and run a monthly check that says in one number whether the business is getting bigger. It assumes you have read [the working manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk); the bookkeeping side is in [bookkeeping and tax for resellers](/guides/bookkeeping-and-tax-for-resellers).

It is not financial advice. Amazon's payment timings are taken from Amazon's own pages and can change; check them before you plan around them.

## The cash cycle

A stock business is a loop: cash becomes stock, stock becomes a sale, the sale becomes cash again, and the cash buys more stock. The length of the loop decides how much money you need and how fast you can grow. Here it is for a typical online arbitrage purchase, with the timings you can rely on.

| Step | What happens | How long, in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. You pay the retailer | Money leaves your account or goes on a card | Day 0 |
| 2. Stock arrives with you | Retailer delivery | 2 to 7 days |
| 3. You prep and ship to Amazon | Labelling, boxing, carrier collection | 1 to 7 days depending on your routine |
| 4. Amazon receives and checks in | Transit plus receiving at the warehouse; longer in Q4 | 3 to 14 days |
| 5. It sells | Depends on the listing, your share of the Buy Box and your price | Days to weeks |
| 6. It is delivered | FBA delivery | 1 to 3 days |
| 7. Funds become available | Amazon's delivery date reserve: standard is seven days after delivery | 7 days |
| 8. Amazon pays out | Automatic settlement every 14 days, or a manual transfer when funds are available | 0 to 14 days |
| 9. Your bank receives it | Bank processing | Up to 5 working days |

Step 7 is set by Amazon's delivery date based reserve policy: for a standard account, the proceeds of an order become available for disbursement seven days after the delivery date, using the carrier's confirmed delivery date or, where there is none, the latest estimated delivery date ([Amazon on account level reserves](https://sellercentral.amazon.co.uk/seller-forums/discussions/t/aac4b8e9-c776-418e-8373-9484fbf8baa1)). Steps 8 and 9 come from Amazon's own description of how payments work: accounts settle every two weeks and, once Amazon initiates a payment, it can take up to five working days to show in your bank ([how seller payments work](https://sell.amazon.com/blog/amazon-seller-payments)). The current figures for your account are on the Payments dashboard in Seller Central.

Add it up. From a customer's payment to money in your bank is commonly ten to twenty days. From the retailer's checkout to money in your bank, for a product that sells within a couple of weeks of going live, is six to ten weeks. For a product that takes two months to sell, it is a quarter of a year. Everything in this guide follows from that gap.

### Reserves

Amazon holds money in three ways, and the Payments page labels them.

- **Deferred transactions** are the delivery date reserve above: the proceeds of every order for the seven days after delivery. This is normal, it applies to everyone, and it rolls forward continuously.
- **Account level reserve** is money Amazon holds beyond that. New accounts often have a larger one for their first weeks. Accounts under review, with a rising claims rate, or with an unusual spike in sales, can have a large share of their balance held for a period Amazon decides. Amazon says the reserve period can be extended following an assessment of risk and history; it does not publish the criteria.
- **Unavailable balance** covers money Amazon needs for open claims, chargebacks and refunds in progress.

You cannot negotiate the deferred transactions. You can keep the account level reserve small by keeping account health clean and by growing steadily rather than in a single jump.

### Withdrawing

Amazon pays out automatically on its settlement schedule, but you do not have to wait for it. Whenever there is an available balance, the Payments dashboard offers a manual transfer to your bank (Request transfer, or Disburse, depending on the page version). Doing that daily, as the manual recommends, moves money out of Amazon as soon as it is free. The reasons are practical: money inside Amazon cannot buy stock, it is exposed if your account is reviewed, and a daily rhythm means you always know what you have. A transfer on a Monday morning is in the bank by the end of the week.

If your bank account or card details change, Amazon holds payments until the new details are verified. Change them in a quiet week, never in the run up to a big purchase.

## The overheads

Overheads are what you pay whether or not anything sells. Stock is not an overhead; it is the thing that earns. The distinction matters because a pot that is mostly overheads cannot grow, and because most new sellers carry more of them than their sales support.

| Overhead | Type | When it earns its keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Professional selling plan | Fixed, monthly | From about 34 sales a month; before that it is a cost of being eligible for the Buy Box |
| Price history tool | Fixed, monthly | Day one; you cannot buy well without it |
| Sourcing calculator | Fixed, monthly | Day one, or use Amazon's free calculator until it slows you down |
| Repricer | Fixed, monthly | Once you have more than about ten live products |
| Profit and stock tracker | Fixed, monthly | When the spreadsheet stops being enough |
| Accounting software | Fixed, monthly | From VAT registration at the latest; earlier if it saves accountant time |
| Accountant | Fixed, annual or monthly | From the first year end; earlier for a company |
| Prep centre | Per unit | When your time is worth more than the fee and margins can carry it |
| Prep materials | Per unit | Always; labels, boxes, tape, poly bags |
| Product liability insurance | Fixed, annual | Required by Amazon once gross sales pass £4,000 a month for three months running; see the manual |
| Business bank account | Fixed, monthly | Some are free; do not pay for features you do not use |
| Sourcing lists and lead services | Fixed, monthly | Only if the leads pay for the subscription several times over, measured |
| Fuel, parking, postage | Variable | Only for retail arbitrage; count it |
| Storage at home | Usually nothing | Until it is |

Two rules keep this lean.

First, match overheads to sales, not to ambition. A tool that costs more per month than it makes you is a loss even if everyone recommends it. Review the list monthly against actual net profit: if overheads are more than a third of net profit, cut until they are not, starting with the one you use least.

Second, add tools in the order the business needs them, and only when the previous step is running. The manual's list is the order: price history and calculator first, repricer at ten products, tracker when the spreadsheet breaks, accounting software by VAT. Everything else waits until it is clearly the bottleneck.

Amazon's own fees are not overheads in this sense; they are a cost of each sale and belong in the margin calculation. Remember they carry 20% VAT since August 2024, reclaimable only if you are registered.

## Gross is a mood, net is a number

Your tracker shows a profit figure for the month. It is almost always gross profit on sales: sale price less cost of goods and Amazon's fees, summed across orders. It is a useful number and a dangerous one, because it ignores everything you paid that was not attached to an order.

Net is gross less overheads less the costs that do not attach to an order: prep materials, delivery from the retailer, returns that came back unsellable, storage, advertising if you run any, and VAT if you are registered and did not set the tracker up for it. Net is the only figure that can go back into stock, and the only one that tells you whether the business works.

### A simple monthly formula

On the last day of each month, work out one number:

**Growth = net profit for the month minus what you took out.**

Where:

- **Net profit** is gross profit on sales for the month, less every overhead and non-order cost for the month, from your tracker and your bank statement, not from memory.
- **What you took out** is any money you moved to yourself, plus any tax you set aside (a fixed percentage of net is the sensible habit; the rate depends on your structure and other income, so ask an accountant what to hold).

If growth is positive, the business has more money in it at the end of the month than at the start: more stock, more cash, or less debt. If it is zero the business is standing still. If it is negative you are running it down, whether or not the tracker says you made a profit.

Three companion numbers make it useful:

- **Overhead ratio**: overheads divided by net profit. Over 100% in months one and two is normal; the target is under a third by month three or four.
- **Stock turn**: cost of goods sold in the month divided by average stock at cost. A reseller working replenishable products should see stock turn over in well under two months. Stock older than 90 days is a cash flow problem in a warehouse; review it, reprice it, or remove it.
- **Cash position**: bank plus available and deferred balances in Amazon plus stock at cost minus card and loan balances. This is what the business is worth in cash terms. Watch it monthly; it is the number that catches a slow leak that the monthly profit figure hides.

A business is growing when growth is positive for three consecutive months, the overhead ratio is falling, and stock turn is not slowing. A business that is "profitable" but whose cash position falls every month is buying the wrong things or paying for the wrong tools.

## Cash flow rules that keep you buying

1. **Buy things that sell in weeks.** A product that returns 30% in three months is worse than one that returns 15% in three weeks, because the second one can be bought four times over.
2. **Do not go deep on one product.** Twenty units of five products is safer than a hundred units of one, however good the numbers look. A price drop, a listing change or a competitor's pallet on one listing then costs you a fifth of your stock, not all of it.
3. **Price to move, not to maximise.** If a product is not selling at the top of its range, take a smaller margin and get the cash back. Holding out for the last pound while the cash sits in a warehouse costs more than the pound.
4. **Withdraw daily and plan buying around when the money lands**, not around what is on offer. A calendar of expected payouts is the single most useful planning tool in the business.
5. **Keep a buffer.** One month of overheads and any minimum card payment, in the bank, untouched. It is the difference between a slow month and a crisis.
6. **Put profit back in until the business can spare it.** The first months' net goes to stock. Taking it out early is the same as never having made it.

## Using credit carefully

Credit can shorten the cycle: if a business credit card is paid in full on a date that falls after Amazon has paid you for the stock it bought, the card has funded the stock for free. That is the whole case for it, and it works.

The conditions are strict:

- You understand your own cycle from the table above and it is reliably shorter than the card's interest free period.
- You pay the full balance every month without exception. The interest rate on a card exceeds the return on almost any product; one month of carrying a balance can erase a quarter's growth.
- You buy only what you would have bought with cash. The card is a timing tool, not extra capital.
- You use a business card, on the business name, so the invoices and the statements match the rest of your paperwork.

High interest lending, including the offers that appear inside Seller Central, is a different thing. The margins in arbitrage do not cover the interest, and borrowing against stock that has not sold is the route out of the business. Sellers we know who have grown steadily did it with retained profit and a card paid in full; the ones who borrowed to go faster mostly went faster in the wrong direction.

A cashback site or card cashback on stock purchases is a small, free improvement to every buy; record it as income and do not let it choose the product.

## Alongside a job

Most successful small resellers started with a job, and the job is an advantage, not an obstacle: it removes the pressure to take money out early, which is the pressure that kills the cycle. What makes it work is deciding in advance what the business gets and what it does not.

- **Time.** Fix the hours: an hour of sourcing on weekday evenings, a prep and shipping session at the weekend, ten minutes a day on the dashboard. Written down, in the calendar, the same each week. A routine survives a bad week; enthusiasm does not.
- **Systems before speed.** A buy list, an invoice folder, a shipping checklist, a monthly close. When the routine runs on checklists you can do it tired, and you can hand a piece of it to someone else later.
- **Cash.** The job pays the bills, so the business does not have to. Decide a monthly amount you can put in and a date at which you stop putting in, and then let profit fund the rest.
- **Family and space.** Stock in the hallway is a negotiation. Agree a corner, a night, and a maximum number of boxes before the first delivery arrives.
- **Stress.** The moments that hurt are a deactivation email at work and a stock delivery on a day you cannot handle it. Both are easier with the second user on the account and a prep centre once volume warrants it.
- **When to go further.** Not when a good month arrives. When net profit has covered your take home for six consecutive months, the cash position has grown through all of them, and the routine runs without you thinking about it. Anything sooner is a bet.

## What growth realistically looks like

Sellers who last describe a similar shape, though the numbers vary with how much they put in and how many hours they give it. Without anyone's income figures, this is the pattern:

**Month one.** Overheads exceed net. First stock goes in; a few sales. A small loss is normal and expected.

**Months two and three.** First replenishable products found. Net turns positive; overhead ratio still high. The cash cycle from the first buys completes, and it is longer than you thought.

**Months four to six.** Overheads covered. Growth positive most months. A list of products you have sold before that you can buy again, which is where most of the profit now comes from. Brand approvals start to arrive that once refused you.

**Months six to twelve.** Growth compounds because every month's net becomes next month's stock. Tools added as they pay. VAT threshold on the horizon if you are moving quickly; start preparing at £80,000 of rolling turnover, because registration changes the margins, and an accountant needs time.

**Year two.** The routine runs without thought; the numbers are stable; the question changes from "does this work" to "how much of my time do I want it to take". First year for learning, second year for earning is a fair summary.

The money doubles slowly and then quickly. A business that reinvests all of a modest net profit every month for a year has far more stock in it at the end than one that made a big month in March and took it out.

## What goes wrong

- **Reading gross as net.** The tracker's big number is not the one you can spend.
- **No expenses logged.** Net profit is unknown, so growth is unknown, so decisions are guesses.
- **One deep buy.** A single product holding most of your cash for a quarter.
- **Chasing the last pound.** Stock aged past 90 days because you would not reprice.
- **Money left inside Amazon.** Not withdrawn, then held during a review.
- **Subscriptions that outrun sales.** Overheads above a third of net for months on end.
- **A card balance carried.** Interest eats the growth number every month.
- **Taking out too early.** The first months' profit is stock, not wages.
- **Growing in a single jump.** A sales spike triggers a larger reserve and a cash squeeze at once.
- **No buffer.** One late payout and a card due date is a crisis rather than a nuisance.
- **Ignoring the VAT line.** Crossing the threshold without changing the calculator turns a growing business into a shrinking one in a month.
