This is for people selling, or about to sell, on Amazon UK who want to buy stock from retailers' websites and make a margin on it through FBA. It assumes you have read the [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk) and have an account, a price history tool and a sourcing calculator. It goes deeper on the one skill that manual says decides everything: the buy. Figures link to official pages where they matter; none of this is tax or financial advice.

## The model in one paragraph

A retailer prices a product below what it sells for on Amazon, often because it is on sale, on clearance, or part of a promotion. You buy it, it is delivered to your door, you label it, you send it to Amazon's warehouse, and it sells at the Amazon price. The gap, less every fee and cost, is yours. The product already has a listing with sales history, so you are not guessing whether anyone wants it; you are only judging whether the gap is real and whether it will still be there when your stock lands.

It suits beginners for three reasons. Every purchase comes with an invoice bearing your name and address, which is the paperwork Amazon and HMRC both ask for. Most retailers give you a change of mind window on distance sales, so a buy you got wrong can usually go back ([GOV.UK on online selling](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) explains the 14 day right; a retailer's own policy is often longer). And you can start with a few units of a few products, which is the only sensible way to learn on money you cannot afford to lose.

The catch is the same for everyone: any price you can see, thousands of other sellers can see. The skill is not finding a gap, it is judging which gaps will hold.

## Reading a price history chart

A price history tool records, for each Amazon listing, the price over time from Amazon itself and from third party sellers, the sales rank, the number of sellers, and who held the Buy Box. Learn to read the chart before you trust any number the tool prints beside it. Here is what each line means and what it is hiding.

### Price

There are usually separate lines for Amazon's own price, the lowest new third party price, and the Buy Box price. Read the Buy Box line, because that is what customers pay and what you will get. The lowest third party line is often a single seller with a broken price, or an FBM offer with a long delivery time that never wins the Buy Box.

Look for the shape over at least ninety days, ideally a year. A flat line is a stable product. A line that steps down and stays down means the market moved, usually because someone brought in a lot of stock. A line that dips and recovers within weeks is a seller clearing out, and it will happen again, possibly while your units are on the shelf. Never work your margin from the top of the chart. Work it from where the line spends most of its time.

### Sales rank

Sales rank is Amazon's rolling measure of how recently and how often a product sells relative to its category. Lower is faster. On the chart it is a jagged line: every sale sends it down, and it drifts up between sales. A product that sells several times a day shows a tight, low, busy line. A product that sells once a fortnight shows long climbs and occasional cliffs. The number of drops in a period is a rough count of sales, and most tools estimate a monthly figure from it. Treat that estimate as an order of magnitude, not a fact, and remember the estimate is for the whole listing, shared between every seller on it.

Rank is not comparable across categories. A rank of 50,000 is fast in some and dead in others. Learn the ranges in the two or three categories you work and ignore rules of thumb from other people's categories.

### Seller count

The seller count line tells you how many offers there are. Watch its direction as much as its level. Rising sellers with a falling price is a product everyone found last month; you are late. Falling sellers with a stable price is a product other people are giving up on, which is often where a quiet margin lives. Five sellers on a listing that sells ten a day is fine. Twenty sellers on a listing that sells two a day is a queue, and the queue reprices downward.

### Buy Box history

Some tools show who held the Buy Box over time. What you want to know is whether Amazon itself holds it. When Amazon is in stock and selling, it usually wins the Buy Box and you will sell only when it runs out or when it lets you. A chart where Amazon is in and out of stock, with third party sellers winning in the gaps, can be profitable if you time it. A chart where Amazon has held the Buy Box for six months straight is not a listing for you, however good the gap looks.

### Drops, gaps and the things that are not on the chart

A price drop at the same time as a rank drop is a real sale at a low price. A price drop with no rank movement is a price that nobody bought at. Gaps in the price line mean nobody was offering the product; if Amazon's line disappears for weeks at a time, that is your window.

What the chart cannot tell you: whether the product is gated for your account, whether the brand serialises units (Transparency), whether it is hazmat, whether it is dated stock, and whether the listing you are looking at is the exact pack size and variant the retailer is selling. Check all five before the calculator, because none of them are fixable after you have bought.

## What the calculator must include

A sourcing calculator takes a buy price and a sell price and returns net profit and return on investment. Most tools do this well; the mistakes are in what people leave out. Set the calculator up so that every one of these is included by default, and check its settings monthly because fees change.

| Cost | Where the figure comes from | What people forget |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Referral fee | A percentage of the sale price by category, mostly 8% to 15%, on [Amazon's fee page](https://sell.amazon.co.uk/pricing) | That it is charged on the price the customer pays, including any delivery charge |
| FBA fulfilment fee | Per unit by size tier and weight, on the same page | That the size tier is set by packaged dimensions, and a lightweight item in a large box pays the large fee |
| Monthly storage | Per cubic foot, higher from October to December, on the same page | That slow stock pays it every month, and stock held past 241 days pays an aged inventory surcharge |
| VAT on Amazon's fees | 20% added to every fee since August 2024 | If you are not VAT registered this is a cost, not a reclaim, so every fee above is a fifth higher for you |
| Delivery to you | The retailer's checkout | Free delivery thresholds that push you into buying more than you wanted |
| Inbound delivery to Amazon | Your partnered carrier rate per box | Spread across the units in the box |
| Prep | Labels, poly bags, bubble wrap, and the prep centre fee if you use one | Your own time is not free either |
| Returns allowance | A percentage of sales, set by you from the product's return history | Amazon keeps the fulfilment fee and takes an administration fee on the referral fee refund, and an opened return is unsellable |
| VAT on the sale | Only if you are VAT registered: a sixth of the sale price goes to HMRC, less the VAT you reclaim on the purchase | Registered sellers who leave the calculator on "not registered" overstate every margin |

Two settings decide whether the number the calculator gives you is real. First, the sell price it uses: set it to the Buy Box price the chart spends most of its time at, not the current price and not the highest. Second, the return on investment threshold you will accept. Sellers we know will not buy under about 20% return on investment at a realistic price, and want more than that for anything slow, seasonal or with a return problem. Pick your own floor and refuse to go below it, because the calculator will present you with a hundred items a week at 12% and every one of them looks fine on the screen.

The [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#net-profit-properly) walks through the calculation by hand. Do it by hand for your first twenty products, then let the tool do it.

## Six ways to find stock

Every method below produces leads. None of them produces buys; the chart and the calculator do that. The best sellers use several methods and keep notes on which one found their best products.

### 1. Retailer sales and clearance

The plainest method. Every large retailer has a sale, offers, or clearance section on its website, and stock moves into it on a rhythm: end of season, end of line, packaging changes, overstocks. Work through it product by product, look each one up on Amazon, and run the chart and the calculator on anything with a gap. It is slow, which is the point: most people will not do it, so the products you find this way have fewer competitors than anything from an alert.

Build a list of retailers as you go, with notes on what each one is good for and when its sales fall. Sign up for their emails on an address you keep just for that, because sales and codes arrive there first. Smaller and specialist retailers are worth more of your time than the big names, because fewer sellers are looking at them.

### 2. Voucher stacking

The price you pay is rarely the price on the page. Codes from the retailer's own email, first order discounts, student and key worker schemes, cashback sites that pay a percentage on the order, gift cards bought at a discount through a cashback or reward platform, and a card that pays cashback on spending: each takes a little off, and they often combine. A product with no margin at ticket price can have a good one at ticket minus 10% minus 5% cashback. Test combinations at checkout before you commit to a basket, and read the code's terms, because many exclude sale items or need a minimum spend.

Record cashback and discounted gift cards properly. Cashback is income; a gift card bought at a discount reduces your cost of stock. Your accountant will want the trail.

### 3. Reverse sourcing from listings

Instead of starting at the retailer and looking for the product on Amazon, start at Amazon and look for the product at retailers. Pick a listing that has the shape you want (stable price, healthy rank, few sellers, no Amazon), then search the product name and barcode across retailers and price comparison sites to find who stocks it and at what price. Do this for every product in a brand or a subcategory and you build a map of which retailers under price which brands. It is how sellers find replenishables that are never on sale anywhere obvious.

### 4. Storefront stalking

Every seller on Amazon has a public storefront listing everything they sell. Find a seller who is on the listings you like, open their storefront, and work through their products with the chart and the calculator. Then reverse source the ones that look good. You are borrowing their research. The method is legal and everyone does it, which is also its weakness: a storefront that a thousand new sellers are copying is a storefront whose products are about to be crowded. Use it to learn what good products look like and which brands and retailers a competent seller works with, not as your only source. And expect others to do the same to you; it is one reason not to go deep on any single product.

### 5. Category filters in the price history tool

Most price history tools have a product finder that filters Amazon's whole catalogue by the things you care about: sales rank range, price range, seller count, whether Amazon is on the listing, price and seller trends over the last ninety days, review count, category. Set filters that describe your ideal listing (for example: rank inside your working range, three to eight sellers, Amazon not in stock, price stable or rising over ninety days, seller count falling) and let it produce a few hundred candidates. Then reverse source the best of them.

The value is in filters nobody else uses. "Best selling, cheapest, most sellers" is what everyone runs. "Seller count down by half in ninety days with a stable price" is where the quiet products are. Change one filter at a time and keep the sets that produce buys.

### 6. Your own sales history

The best product in the world is one you have already sold. You know the listing, the speed and the competition. Keep a list of everything you have sold, with the buy price, the retailer and the sell price, and check the list before every sourcing session: has the retailer's price come back down, has the Amazon price recovered, has the seller count thinned out? Products come round again. Sellers we know get a third or more of their volume from things they have sold before, which is the only reason a weekly routine takes hours rather than days.

## Replenishables

A replenishable is a product you can buy again and again at a price that works, because the gap is structural rather than a one off sale. Think everyday branded goods that a particular retailer always under prices, or products where Amazon is regularly out of stock. They are worth more than any deal because the research is done once and the profit repeats.

Finding them is mostly method 3 and method 6 above. Keeping them is a matter of discipline: buy in quantities the listing can absorb, check the chart before every reorder rather than assuming, and watch for the moment other sellers find the same source. When the seller count doubles and the price slides, stop reordering and let your stock clear. It will often come back.

## Deal alert groups

A deal alert group is a paid or free community, usually on a chat platform, where a small team posts leads: a product, the retailer selling it, the Amazon listing, and the calculated margin. Members buy from the leads. Some groups also post Amazon to Amazon price drops and retailer flash sales in real time.

How they work is simple. The people running the group do the sourcing, or run software that does, and share the result with everyone who pays. That is also the problem. Every lead is seen by every member at the same moment, and every member can see the same chart you can. A good lead in a group of several hundred sellers is a good lead for the first twenty people to buy, and a crowded listing for everyone who arrives a week later when the stock lands. The seller count on a group lead can triple within days.

Used well, groups are worth the fee for a beginner, and here is how sellers we know use them:

- **Treat the lead as a starting point, not an instruction.** Run the chart and the calculator yourself, every time. Groups get pack sizes wrong, miss that Amazon is about to come back into stock, and post margins at the top of the chart.
- **Assume the price will fall.** Recalculate with a sell price 10% to 15% below the posted one and buy only if it still works.
- **Buy small.** Two or three units of a group lead, never twenty. You are testing whether the listing survives the crowd.
- **Look at the group's leads for the last month, not this week.** Which listings held their price, which collapsed. That tells you how good the group is and how much to trust its margins.
- **Use the group for education.** The best groups discuss why a lead is good, which retailers are worth watching and what to do when things go wrong. That is worth more than the leads.
- **Leave when the leads stop teaching you.** The goal is to source your own products. A group that has become your only source is a subscription that owns you.

Never pay a large joining fee or a long contract up front. A month at a time, cancel when it stops paying.

## Buying rules

These are the rules the sellers we know actually follow, and every one is a scar.

**Quantities.** For a new product, buy what the listing sells in a week or two, divided by the number of sellers, and never more than you can afford to have sit for three months. For most beginners that means two to six units of a product. Go deeper only on products you have sold before, and even then never so deep that one price collapse takes a month's profit.

**Testing.** Every first purchase is a test. Buy the small quantity, send it in, watch it sell, watch the price. Reorder on evidence, not on the calculator.

**Returns windows.** Know the retailer's returns window before you buy, and diarise it. If a product's chart turns against you before the window closes (Amazon comes back in stock, the price drops, the listing is gated when you try to add it), send it back and take the small loss on delivery. Under [distance selling rules](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses) you have 14 days from delivery for a change of mind refund on most online purchases, and many retailers give longer. Stock you return costs you a few pounds; stock you keep and cannot sell costs you all of it.

**Paperwork.** The order confirmation is an invoice with your name and address on it; keep it. If you ever need to apply for brand approval, Amazon's application form asks for supplier invoices showing your details, the supplier's details, and a combined quantity of at least ten units, dated within the last 180 days, and it says retail receipts and online order confirmations are not accepted for that purpose. Retailer invoices are still the evidence you rely on when a buyer or brand questions authenticity, and they are your tax records. Address to your business address exactly as Amazon has it, not a prep centre.

**Gating and restrictions.** Check whether you can sell the product before you pay for it, in Seller Central, on the exact listing. Check for Transparency, hazmat, expiry dates and meltable goods. The [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#what-most-guides-leave-out) covers each.

**Exact match.** Pack of three is not pack of four. 500ml is not 750ml. The chart and the calculator are only true for the listing you are attaching to.

**Retailer limits.** Retailers cancel orders that look like resale and close accounts that order unusual quantities. Keep orders to sensible household sizes, use one account per retailer, and do not chase a good price with a second account in a family member's name; when it goes wrong it takes the address with it.

**Cash.** Never spend money you will need before Amazon pays you. Buying with credit against stock that has not sold is the single most common way this business ends; the [FBA manual](/guides/how-to-start-amazon-fba-uk#the-money) explains the cash cycle.

## A weekly sourcing routine

Sourcing is a habit, not an event. This is a routine that fits around a job.

**Two evenings a week, ninety minutes each.** Start with your sold list (method 6): check prices and charts on everything you have sold before and reorder what still works. Then one method from the list above, rotated week by week, for an hour: one week the clearance sections of three retailers, the next a product finder run with a new filter set, the next a session of reverse sourcing a brand that has been good to you. Log every lead that nearly worked, with the price it would have needed; retailers cut prices and those become buys later.

**Every morning, ten minutes.** Retailer emails and any alert group. Act only on things that pass your own chart and calculator. Do not let alerts set the pace of your day.

**Once a week, thirty minutes.** Reconcile. What did last week's buys cost, what has landed at Amazon, what sold, at what price, and how the seller count moved on each. Update the sold list and the retailer notes. This is where you learn which methods are earning and which are just busy.

**Once a month.** Review calculator settings against the current fee page. Review subscriptions. Check every product's chart for signs of Amazon returning or a seller dumping stock, and reprice or clear before it hits.

## What goes wrong

- **Margin worked at the top of the chart.** The price you get is the price the line spends most of its time at, and after your stock lands it is often lower.
- **Amazon on the listing.** You will not out sell Amazon while it is in stock. Buy only if the chart shows it goes out of stock regularly, and buy small.
- **Missing the VAT on fees.** Every fee is 20% higher than the rate card for an unregistered seller. A margin that ignores this is wrong by that much on every unit.
- **Buying the alert, not the product.** A crowded lead is a crowded listing. Small quantities, your own numbers, and a price 15% under the posted one.
- **Too deep on one product.** Twelve products at three units survive a price collapse. One product at thirty-six does not.
- **Ignoring the returns window.** A calendar reminder on the day it closes is free insurance.
- **Wrong variant.** Check the listing's pack count, size and colour against the retailer's product page, then check the product photos, then check again.
- **No retailer notes.** The seller who remembers which retailer clears which brand in which month out sources the seller with the better software.
- **Subscriptions running past their use.** A tool you have not opened in a month is a cost, not a tool.
